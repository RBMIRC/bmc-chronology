Pedagogy of Presence:

The Black Mountain College Method of Instruction




                  Angela Muir

--- Page 2 ---
In the midst of a global pandemic, a crumbling economy and a critical human rights

movement, it is natural to conclude that all truths must be reconsidered. Among these, the

foundation of our higher education system and the methods in which we instruct are not

exempt from consideration. Academia is largely based on measurement, an assessment

process in which the institution determines a student’s potential based on a series of

metrics. These metrics are letters and numbers assigned from standardized testing,

classroom achievements, and subjective valuations. These letters and numbers will then

assign the student to a category. What happens when we take this evaluation process

away? Or redefine the metrics in which we measure a student’s potential? What becomes

the foundation of academia when the assessment becomes secondary or even obsolete?

What framework is essential to make this possible?

       The elimination of the grading standard would require a pedagogical approach that

is able to track a student’s progress in the moment. This type of pedagogy emerged from

within Black Mountain College’s core values and I have named it, Pedagogy of Presence,

because it requires the teacher to rely on a fully immersive learning experience.

Perception, process and abstraction are the three pillars of this pedagogy, and one can see

how these three elements function by examining the methods used at Black Mountain

College. Its success could simply be a list of associated names which are considered

essential players in postmodern art; John Cage, Merce Cunningham, Willem and Elaine de

Kooning, Franz Kline, Robert Rauschenberg, Cy Twombly, Buckminster Fuller, and Ruth

Asawa. The list of writers has similar weight; Francine du Plessix Gray, Hilda Morley, Ed

Dorn, Robert Creeley, Joel Oppenheimer, Robert Duncan and John Wieners, to name a few.

--- Page 3 ---
I believe that this method of instruction is worth exploring in our contemporary

predicament as Black Mountain itself began in a similar historic moment.

       In 1933 the United States was on the tail end of the Great Depression, World War II

had already started in the Pacific, The Third Reich had come into power in Germany and

John Andrew Rice founded Black Mountain College in North Carolina. Rice was recently

dismissed as a professor from Rollins College for his radical opinions and behavior. Rice

had a rather progressive and controversial view of academic measures, he considered

“regulations the last refuge of mediocrity,” he disdained “numerologists in education”, and

thought that learning should happen “in one’s own way and according to one’s own

timetable” (Duberman, 5). He admired the work of philosopher and educator, John Dewey,

and borrowed from him some of the founding principles for Black Mountain College.

       These borrowed ideologies included an arts central education, an interdisciplinary

approach to curriculum and a focus on a “holistic” education, which educates the “whole

person” by encouraging service and collaboration. The college also dismantled the

hierarchies typical in academia by positioning the student and the instructors on equal

ground, both in the management of the college and the community. Black Mountain College

was not solely an academic institution, it was not even accredited. Rather, Black Mountain

was an attempt at community living and learning, a view into pioneer life, while at the same

time a walk towards the avant-garde.

       With art education at the core, Black Mountain modeled the way creativity could be

experienced not as isolated instances, but as central to all life. In the 1933 Black Mountain

Catalogue, this was described:

       Dramatics, Music, and the Fine Arts, which often exist precariously on the fringes
       of the curriculum, are regarded as an integral part of the life of the

--- Page 4 ---
College...through some kind of art-experience... the student can come to the
       realization of order in the world; and, by being sensitized to movement, form,
       sound, and the other media of the arts, gets a firmer control of himself and his
       environment than is possible through purely intellectual effort.

All students, whether they considered themselves artists or economists, would actively

experience the arts. This points to a measure of student potential not as an endpoint, but as

an integrated process or method.

       Aside from the arts centrality, the interdisciplinary approach, defined by the open

curriculum, made the student active in their education. Will Hamlin, a Black Mountain

student, said of this methodology, “Academically, I don’t think we suffered from having the

freedom to study whatever we wanted to study… There was a sense of involvement in

Black Mountain classes. You were there because this was something you were interested

in” (Duberman, 100). Freedom empowers students to direct their own learning, but also

requires confidence. This model establishes a system of trust between the student and the

institution.

       The open curriculum has been adopted by few colleges and has been made famous

by Brown University. Brown has kept their open curriculum policy central to

undergraduate education for over fifty years and continues to maintain its status as an Ivy

League institution with high success rates from graduates. Brown maintains a 98%

retention rate for returning sophomores and a 95% graduation rate among

undergraduates. In addition, the 2019 class reported 70% employment rate and another

21% went on to graduate or professional studies after graduation. Brown could have been

pulling a line directly from the Black Mountain Bulletin when it states on its website, “By

cultivating such openness, you will learn to make the most of the freedom you have, and to

chart the broadest possible intellectual journey.”

--- Page 5 ---
A broad intellectual journey could also be defined as a “holistic” education, in the

case of Black Mountain, this was the blending of curriculum and extra-curricular. By

keeping the students engaged outside of the classroom in duties around the community,

such as building and farming, the students were not only gaining skills but were also

learning to see their impact on the larger group. In a recent study published in the The

Journal of Positive Psychology, researchers showed that participants who were more

engaged in social activities, such as volunteering, had a greater sense of purpose in their

lives and better relationships. John Dewey writes,

       unless [the] end is placed in the context of service rendered to others, skills gained
       will be put to an egoistic and selfish use and may be employed as means of a trained
       shrewdness in which one person gets the better of others. Too often, indeed, the
       schools, through reliance upon the spur of competition and bestowing special
       honors and prizes, only build up and strengthen the disposition that makes an
       individual when he leaves school employ his special talents and superior skill to
       outwit his fellows without respect for the welfare of others.


Service to others, in the context of art, shifts away from the traditional definition of art as

“self-expression” and instead self-expression becomes a byproduct of the experience. A

famous example of this is are “the happenings” at Black Mountain.

       Based on the doctrine at that “art is completed by the observer,” the talent and

resources of the community would come together in an “interdisciplinary” performance

(Duberman, 370). The idea developed in a conversation between composer John Cage and

pianist David Tudor. Cage would outline “the happening” into time brackets and then invite

various artists to fill the time, all acting “by means of chance operations” (Duberman, 370).

For example, one famous performance included readings by Charles Olson and M.C.

Richards, paintings by Robert Rauschenberg, piano compositions by Cage and Tudor and a

dance number by Merce Cunningham. Cage’s aim was “purposeless purposefulness.”

--- Page 6 ---
Performances were not recorded, in fact recollections of these experiences varied wildly.

Each individual artist and audience member would recall different details, a demonstration

in diverse perceptions and focus. The “happenings” embodied the essence of Black

Mountain; art centrality, an interdisciplinary experience, free community entertainment,

and a non-hierarchical cast of talent. Teachers and students would perform alongside one

another without distinction.

       Black Mountain aimed to dismantle hierarchies in every aspect of its structure.

Students and instructors held equal positions on the board, worked side by side on

building, farming and other familial tasks, sat together in the cafeteria, and cooperated on

creative projects. This effectively endorsed both a sense of freedom and a blending of the

public/private and personal/political. Furthermore, the policies and values for the students

were the same policies and values for the teachers. Many instructors took classes in other

disciplines while they taught at the college. Notorious examples are English literature

lecturer M.C. Richards who studied pottery at Black Mountain with Karen Karnes and David

Weinrib, and writer Charles Olson who studied dance with Merce Cunningham. This

modeled the way for students and established a mutual respect for both study and

instruction.

       These guiding values of Black Mountain set the stage for the Pedagogy of Presence to

materialize. This pedagogy can be best outlined through the methods used by Josef Albers

and Charles Olson, two of Black Mountain’s leading arts educators. Albers’s and Olson’s

Pedagogy of Presence was a method in instructing art while educating the whole person by

offering an experiential course for learning to see (perception), working with a medium

(process), and creating a product (abstraction).

--- Page 7 ---
Perception refers to the way in which the artist understands an experience. The way

one sees something is not simply an ocular occurrence, rather it is a full sensory one. Our

sense receptors are triggered by our environment which activates our memory. This

collection of available knowledge fed by our previous experiences directs our attention.

Perception is the comprehension of how information is organized, interpreted and then

consciously experienced. Lessons in perception give students the ability to work with

seeing fact from opinion and differentiating what one sees from what one feels. It promotes

emotional intelligence by creating experiences of relativity and empathy. It also gives

students practice in active seeing and listening, and the ability to be fully present.

Therefore, “the way we look at things is a creative work” (Richards, 17). Our perception is

exclusive and is enacted in our process.

        Process simply stated is mastery of an artists’ medium, construction and craft. For

example, students actively working on problem solving, engaging their personal will power

and produced a natural response to act. Francine du Plessix Gray, though a critic of Olson,

said of his teaching methods,

       …how to arrive at intense experience was really his crucial problem and it had to be
       arrived at by a way of life. And he did have a conception of craftsmanship. He said
       that you go to work every day, you’ve got to write every day. And this daily activity
       of writing will sharpen your experience… You picked up a journal habit from Olson
       (Duberman, 398).

Similarly, Albers’s sense of discipline and structure was infectious. He demanded his

students become experts in their craft before they moved out into the experimental.

       Mastery led to one’s ability to problem solve and therefore create art, or

abstractions. Abstractions were the artists translated experiences, the unique

interpretation on what has been observed. In his 1935 lecture “Abstract Art,” Josef Albers

--- Page 8 ---
says, “As life is more than nature, so is art more than life. Because art is spirit—that means

an essential seeing—instead of imitation we need translation.”



       Josef Albers, an already distinguished art teacher at the Bauhaus in Germany, was

asked by John Rice to teach at Black Mountain via telegram in 1933. Albers had recently

lost his position at the Bauhaus as it had closed to avoid accepting Nazi instructors. Albers

and his wife Anni were consequently looking for a reason to leave Germany to avoid

conflict over Anni’s Jewish heritage. Josef spoke little English, but when he arrived in North

Carolina fresh from Berlin he told newspapers, “I want to open eyes.” This simple

statement was the core trait to his approach to instruction. He believed that, “academic

studies under academic teachers, and academic methods, and for academic measurements

will produce school stars which will wane after school. Any work done for the sake of the

teacher or the sake of the school is not enduring, because life is everything but academic”

(Albers, “Abstract Art”).

       Albers held that self-expression should not be taught as central to the arts, instead

an understanding of direct observation and self-discovery were key. Art is not simply a

result of imitation or reproduction, but a communication of feeling. This enactment would

find best results in training the eyes and the hands. He says, “art is a province in which one

finds all the problems of life reflected—not only the problems of form (e.g. proportion and

balance) but also spiritual problems (e.g. of philosophy, of religion, of sociology, of

economy)” (Albers, “Concerning Art Instruction”). This makes art a rich medium for

general education and development.

The pedagogy of Josef Albers was made clear in the 1934 Black Mountain Bulletin,

“Concerning Art Instruction.” Albers writes, “our art instruction attempts first to teach the

student to see in the widest sense: to open his eyes to the phenomena about him and, most

important of all, to open to his own living, being and doing.” Therefore, experimenting with

the “branches of art” and confronting fundamental problems, gives students a window into

their own unique abilities both as artists and as reflection of their own connection and

problem-solving abilities. Albers broke down the disciplines in his instruction into three

main categories: Drawing, Color-Painting, and Basic Design (Werklehre). Drawing for Josef

Albers was regarded as “graphic language.” He says,

       Just as in studying language it is most important to teach first the commonly
       understood usage of speech, in drawing we begin with exact observation and pure
       representation. We cannot communicate graphically what we do not see. That which
       we see incorrectly we will report incorrectly. We recognize that although our optical
       vision is correct, our overemphasis on the psychic vision often makes us see
       incorrectly (Albers, “Concerning Art Instruction”).

In other words, before an artist can represent a “self-expression,” they must understand

proper unbiased sight. This is why the students were taught to “test” their seeing. This

included systematic study of foreshortening, overlapping and articulation of “nearness and

distance.”

       Albers famously tested his student’s perception with the study of color. In his 1963

manual, Interactions of Color, Albers writes, “With the discovery that color is the most

relative medium in art, and that its greatest excitement lies beyond rules and canons, a

more sensitive discrimination is needed.” Albers thought that due to its relativity one

should become fundamentally aware of one’s way of seeing. This can only happen through

involvement. Albers used color demonstrations to highlight the subjective dimension to

color. For example, in demonstrating the reactions of a specific red shade Albers comments,

--- Page 10 ---
“all group members will have the same visual perception. But still the individual

associations and emotional reactions will differ vastly” (Diaz, 24). A demonstration such as

this one gives the student the opportunity to track their own relationship with the color, as

well as others in the group, bringing an awareness to not only the variability in opinion but

also an experience in mindfulness and empathy.

       Albers’ color experiments also included looking at color relationships. Rather than

teaching complimentary or contrasting color theories, as many art schools do, Albers

would demonstrate how one’s relationship with color changes depending on what other

colors surround it. For example, if the particular red hue from the example above were to

be placed next to lilac or brown, how does one’s interaction with the color change? Does it

appear to be the same hue of red or has it somehow altered? In Eva Diaz’s book The

Experimenters, she says of Albers instruction that

       “visual memory is amazingly poor” as compared with, say, auditory memory, and
       suggests that “color is deceiving us all of the time”; these influences on vision have
       the effect of converting “the optical (physio-physiological) susception [“stimuli’] into
       a psychological effect (perception).”


Because optical impressions are highly influential, the artist must be trained in the process

of perception. Training includes both experience with the interaction of color, but also the

evaluation of the resulting data from these interactions.

       Albers taught further the mutability of color by exploring gradient, reverse ground,

after image, color mixture, transparency, warm and cool, along with many other interfaces.

In each demonstration Albers emphasized the shift in reception of a color based on the

situation. He thought that careful analysis of optical discrepancies versus their material

realities could activate in the student an ability to render this awareness with other

--- Page 11 ---
habitual patterns of meaning. The artist could then become more consciously aware of

their “seeing” and therefore gain greater intuition and drive for discovery. Through

experience the student is taught that in visual perception “there is a discrepancy between

physical fact and psychic effect” (Albers, 2).

       Once the student is trained to see, then they are asked to explore process.

Experimentation with color was just the beginning for Albers. Investigation into shape,

geometry, landscape, and material followed. Each examined from this multi-tiered lens,

teaching his students to have keen awareness of their perceptions, observing them more

actively through the experience and therefore through their art. The manual act of drawing,

for example, was instructed with the most basic and technical aspects in the foreground;

measuring, dividing, estimating, and so on. He says, “the hand must be sensitized to the

direction of will” (Albers, “Concerning Art Instruction”). The awareness of the mind to

manual act, or thought to will, progresses this deep study of perception into a profound

look at one’s own process. By excluding “expressive drawing” at the beginning of the

student’s studies, one is able to produce distance between the object, the art and the

audience.

       Albers also believed that a deep understanding of the medium would lead to clearer

intentions with the students process of painting. It would also result in proper execution.

For example, with paint, Albers says, “to prepare for a disciplined use of color to prevent

accident, brush, or paint-box from taking authorship” (Albers, “Concerning Art

Instruction”). Albers’s aim was not to decentralize the artist, but rather to make them

experts in process and medium so that their own “authorship” would not falter at the hand

--- Page 12 ---
of their materials. Experience with the materials becomes knowledge with which to make

decisions and act, just as in life.

       Albers blends perception with process in his explorations with the material

composition of form, which ultimately led to abstraction. Basic Design divided this into two

components material and matière. Material studies were more concerned with the

technical nature and capacities of the materials. Students would test the materials by

folding, bending, compressing and stretching the medium. This resulted in a dynamic

exploration in the relationships and use of objects. Through the construction process

students were then able to understand deeper the more economic qualities of form such as

space, volume and dimension. This led to an active sense of balance, proportion and

composition. As Albers says, “Basic Design is a training in adaptability in the whole field of

construction and in constructive thinking” (Albers, “Teaching Form Through Practice”). Art

must therefore be objective before it can be abstracted.

       Albers focus on abstraction was rooted in Matière studies, which sought to explore

the optical and tactile nature of things; their texture, structure and contrasts. Students

would be asked to combine found materials, remove them from their normal context, and

obscure their identities by using them in a new way. They were able to use a single material

or several different ones to create a new abstracted work. In essence, by using materials

out of their standard frameworks and placing them in relationship to one another, Albers’s

students began to see the world around them more acutely. One student, Ray Johnson, for

example, used caterpillar nests and metallic paper to create tent-like structures. When one

sees a caterpillar nest in a tree it is apparent what it is, but when paired with the reflective

paper, both materials become unrecognizable.

--- Page 13 ---
Albers’s students would engage a hyper sense of awareness to question their

perceptions, work with the materials through their process, and create a product that was

an abstraction of the original through the unique lens of the student. These practices

noticeably impacted his Black Mountain students, for example, sculptor Ruth Asawa and

painter Robert Rauschenberg.

       Ruth Asawa’s work exemplifies the success of the Pedagogy of Presence. Asawa’s

inspiration came from nature, however her materials, just as in the Matière studies, were

used non-traditionally. Her iconic installations were a combination of brass, iron, and

copper looped wire structures that often suspended from the ceiling. Her sculptures

created geometric shapes that were “tree-like” and explored the relationships between

nature and one’s inner life. Even her works on paper have a distinctive textural quality,

using geometry and color to create dimension. There is a sense of parsimony in her art, a

mastery of process, and a blending of the interior and exterior worlds. Asawa once

commented, “An artist is not special. An artist is an ordinary person who can take ordinary

things and make them special” (Asawa, “Asawa at Work”).

       A similar sentiment is shared by former Albers’ student Robert Rauschenberg, who

says of him, “He didn’t teach you how to do art. The focus was always on your personal

sense of looking” (Rauschenberg, “Rauschenberg Papers”). Rauschenberg considers Albers

his greatest teacher and continues to posthumously honor his time at Black Mountain

through The Rauschenberg Residency, which “fosters the ideal that artistic

practice advances mutual understanding and engenders a focus on the conservation of

a sensitive and pristine environment and integration with the local surroundings.”

Rauschenberg is known for his wide range as an artist, using multiple subjects, styles,

--- Page 14 ---
materials and techniques. His work often epitomizes the Pedagogy of Presence, for example

his “Transfer Drawings.” In these works, Rauschenberg recycles print media combined

with hand-drawn and painted passages to create abstracted pictorial poems. The disparate

images produced a sense of familiarity and at the same time a replacement from a linear

history.



       Charles Olson, still fairly unknown, was invited to teach at Black Mountain by Josef

Albers in the autumn of 1948. He refused initially, but an undeterred Albers offered a more

reasonable proposition, asking Olson to come for a week or more to give some lectures and

seminars. Needless to say Olson accepted that proposal and then the subsequent offer to

stay at Black Mountain. Olson thought Black Mountain was a “special sort of thing and was

worth more to me than any other educational situation I knew” (Olson, 138). In his years

at Black Mountain, he established himself as not only a progressive poet-pedagogue, but in

1954, a determined rector of the college fighting for its survival.

       For Olson, to be a poet was to be a teacher and visa versa. He believed that all

communication was educative, poetry was the highest form of communication, and

therefore poetry was the preeminent method of education. “The poet is the only pedagogue

left, to be trusted,” says Olson. Therefore, Olson’s own bibliography is primary proof of his

pedagogy. His teaching methods and demeanor, though very different from Josef Albers,

engaged the same three-pronged Pedagogy of Presence, focusing on perception, process and

abstraction.

       In his essay, “The Human Universe”, Charles Olson offers, “we are ourselves both the

instrument of discovery and the instrument of definition.” Olson is recognizing the

--- Page 15 ---
relativity of perception, however for Olson the medium was not color but language. He goes

on to say,

       Which is of course, why language is a prime of the matter and why, if we are to see
       some of the laws afresh, it is necessary to examine, first, the present condition of
       language – and I mean language exactly in its double sense of discrimination (logos)
       and of shout (tongue).

What Olson is saying here is twofold: he is, of course, making a distinction between the

written word (logos) and the spoken word (tongue) but there is also the subtle distinction

of truth (common definition) and classification (individual emphasis).

       Olson argues that the intellectual world of language is subject to habitual patterns

and usage, which indicates that perception must be an active practice. The trouble with

habit as an artist, writer or intellectual comes when one aims to identify or define an

experience, which intermits “our participation in our experience” (Olson, 156). It becomes

a demonstration. The “product,” therefore, is a “separating out, an act of classification”

(157). Similar to teaching color theory as a standard set of rules, the tradition of human

experience has been pre-qualified, giving limited opportunities as artists to accomplish

much more than compare or create a set of symbols. Olson remarks, “All that comparison

ever does is set up a series of reference points: to compare is to take one thing and try to

understand it by marking its similarities or differences to another thing” (157).

       For Olson, this problem of habit is systemic. It comes from either “making the soul

mechanical” or worse, by allowing “that external reality is more than merely the substance

which takes man in” (161). By simply consuming matters one is taking for granted the act

of experience. This neglect isolates form from content, as Olson says, “the process of an

image…cannot be understood by separation of the stuff it works on” (162). In other words,

--- Page 16 ---
one must make language an extension of experience. Art is “the only twin life has” and it

does not “seek to describe but to enact” (162).

       The solution for the artist is complete presence, a blending of perception and

process to enact the abstraction. The “meeting edge” of the artist and the world is the

ability to both see the world and comprehend his own process simultaneously. “If man is

active, it is exactly here where experience comes in that it is delivered back, and if he stays

fresh at the coming in he will be fresh at the going out.” Olson discusses this method in his

1951 essay “Projective Verse.” If the artist is fully present in the experience, they will be

able to perceive, recall fully through their process, and then produce their abstraction,

which is not a reenactment, but rather an inimitable expression.

       Coupling Robert Creeley’s statement that “form is never more than an extension of

content” and Edward Dahlberg’s credence that “one perception must immediately and

directly lead to a further perception,” Olson establishes that breath is central in the poetic

process. Rather than focus on meter or rhyme, as is common in poetic instruction, he states

that breath is the “Single Intelligence” that directs a line. The syllable and the line shape the

poem. He writes,

       the HEAD, by way of the EAR, to the SYLLABLE

       the HEART, by way of the BREATH, to the LINE

The ear he refers to here is the ocular, the breath transmits the sound to the page making

the poem visual or written. Logos is word as thought, tongue is word as sound, and the

projective is word as verse on page.

       Perception, process and abstraction are inextricably intertwined for Olson. To

methodize this, the poet would enact the words aloud as speech, type the lines accordingly

--- Page 17 ---
using the typewriter, and the time taken for breath, would become space on the page. The

breath is the “suspension” of syllables, it indicates pause. Olson writes,

       If a contemporary poet leaves a space as long as the phrase before it, he means that
       space to be held, by the breath, an equal length of time. If he suspends a word or
       syllable at the end of a line… he means that time to pass that it takes the eye—that
       hair of time suspended—to pick up the next line (245).

This methodology can be implied on both ends of the product, by artist and audience. For

example, when using this rule while reading the reader is brought closer to the writer, they

experience the sound of the line more similar to when the line was written, indicating its

original intent or enactment. In the same manner, if the writer “wishes a pause so light it

hardly separates the words,” he could use a colon, whereas the comma “is an interruption

of the meaning rather than the sounding of the line” (245).

       Olson goes on to demonstrate the use of the backslash (/) or the tab key as tools to

utilize to enhance abstraction. Each of these tools creates visual triggers for the ear which

allows the verse to be composed by the ear, through the eye, for the ear, “which once had

the burden of memory to quicken it” (246). This movement, defined by Olson as

“objectivism,” would remove the “individual ego” from the work. This by definition

removes the self-expressive nature of art and instead inserts abstraction in its place.

Because “projective verse” requires that be written in the moment, abstraction is the

“projective act,” the layer between the experience and the artist.

       How these studies manifested in Olson’s class is largely unknown but can be

interpreted by student’s class notes. Olson often explored the performative, the blending of

writing with the theatrical. He taught a class called “Projective Verse” at Black Mountain in

1952. The Black Mountain Bulletin from that spring includes a description stating, “The

course focuses on problems of projection as the voice and speech are parts of the projective

--- Page 18 ---
art, and on the usages of the poet historically and again now as the root of drama.” As

demonstrated above, the act of recitation is crucial in the enactment of experience, the

blending of perception and process.

       Olson’s classes spent a significant amount of time on reading and discussing the

student’s work, the presentation of the abstractions. Olson would famously not allow

students to attend class if they did not have something written. He wanted students to

become “personal revolutionaries,” in order to learn through the process of writing

(enacting an experience) that “the person is his or her own material” (their abstractions).

This philosophy puts significant weight on the art because it becomes more than a product,

but an extension of the artist.

       The influence of Charles Olson is apparent in looking at the work of former student

Ed Dorn, whose first published work in 1960 was an essay on the importance of Olson’s

The Maximus Poems. Dorn focuses on Olson’s theme of space and discusses landscape as

inseparable from human influence. Olson’s influence seeps its way into Dorn’s poetry as

well. Dorn explores presence in his poem “The Mountains”, which seems to drop the reader

right in the center of a painting. He says, “The base of the mountains are blue and flecks of

moving brown are there like a lingering image.” The language is plain, descriptive, and

unquestionably present – extending the experience of the poet to the reader. The second

half of the poem breaks this pattern and moves inward, as if giving the reader a glimpse

into Dorn’s thoughts in observation. He concludes the poem with the line, “there is no

coming back from the space you make.” Place, just as the poem itself, is not strictly

geographical but a collaboration of viewer and view. Reiterating the principles of Olson’s

--- Page 19 ---
Pedagogy of Presence, Dorn indicates that perception, process and abstraction are

intertwined.

       Joel Oppenheimer, another former student of Olson and author of over a dozen

works of poetry, also exhibits the impact of this pedagogy in his body of work.

Oppenheimer’s poems are known for his exploration of intimacy in the quotidian. He writes

in his poem “N.B.” that “love is not memory, love is the present act.” A nod towards Olson’s

focus on presence and enactment. The poem uses sharp line breaks and lacks

capitalization. These choices elicit a sense of presence, as if Oppenheimer is allowing the

reader in on his active mind. Oppenheimer also uses present tense, “you are sitting on a

rock in the moonlight…” The use of “you” brings the reader into the poem, as if they are

embodying an abstracted version of the poem’s subject.



       The success of Albers and Olson went far beyond their Black Mountain years, in fact

each had continued success with these methods of instruction. After leaving Black

Mountain Josef Albers went on to teach at Yale University until he retired in 1958. There he

served as the head of the design department and worked to expand the graphic design

division. Charles Olson moved on to become a distinguished professor at the State

University of New York and a visiting professor at the University of Connecticut. His speech

at the 1965 Berkeley Poetry Conference was formative for the understanding of

postmodern poetry movements such as the Beats and the New York School.

       Despite its lack of accreditation, graduate schools such as Harvard and Radcliffe

accepted Black Mountain students based on recommendation (Duberman ,101). This is not

only a nod to the veneration for Black Mountain inside of academia, but also a reminder

It is apparent that the framework of higher education is actively being questioned in this

       There are models from our past that can help us move forward in this complex time,

our Ivy League and top-ranked universities should embrace the need for change and be the

models for innovation. As Albers stated,

       If we must accept education as life and as preparation for life, we must relate all
       school work, including work in art, as closely as possible to modern problems. It
       is not enough to memorize historical interpretations and           aesthetic views of
       the past or merely to encourage a purely individualistic expression. We need not be
       afraid of losing the connection with tradition if we make the elements of form the
       basis of our study. And this thorough       foundation saves us from imitation and
       mannerisms, it develops       independence, critical ability, and discipline (Albers,
       “Teaching Form Through Practice”).


              We have an opportunity to educate the individual by questioning our

standards of measure, looking closely at the opportunities of our curriculums and engaging

in pedagogies that promote experience and mastery. In a letter to potter Marguerite

Wildenhain, Olson writes, “I despair where teaching is put on any other ground… than the

individual.” The Pedagogy of Presence does precisely this, puts the individual at the center

of their own experience by instructing their method of perception, process and abstraction.
