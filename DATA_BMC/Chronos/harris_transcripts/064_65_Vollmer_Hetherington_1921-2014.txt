Boston University over here, in the graduate school, and was looking for a
job. And at the time Erwin Bodky was at Black Mountain College. Did you
know him at all, or of him?
MEH: [AFFIRMATIVE] I’m going to talk to his daughter.
VH: [INAUDIBLE REFERENCE TO FOOD] [OVERTALK] You say you know
his daughter?
MEH: [OVERTALK] I’m going to talk to her on Sunday. No, in fact this afternoon.
VH: Are they still in Belmont?
MEH: No, he’s dead. She in Watertown.
VH: That’s probably where they [INAUDIBLE]
MEH: You say you had graduated here, at Boston . . .
VH: University.
MEH: University. And was that your Master’s?
VH: I have an M.A. from [INAUDIBLE] And at the time I was studying with a
fellow named Wolf WolfInsohn, who was first violin with the Strad Quartet
and Bodky played, as I did, with a quartet on occasions, doing this like the
Seasons of Vivaldi, as an example, I recall we did. So I got to know him
fairly well. As a matter of fact, my wife studied harpsichord with him for a
short period of time, and he was primarily a harpsichordist. So it was
through Bodky who asked me to go to Black Mountain to replace him as
he was coming to Brandeis. So I went to Black Mountain. In fact, my hope
was that, in discussion with him, was that I would come back here to
Brandeis myself at some point. But it didn’t work out.
MEH: What was your particular interest in music?
VH: I was a musicology major in school, but I was really, I’d say, a professional
violinist. I played the fiddle and earned money playing the fiddle. So as far
as musicology, I think I was like – I’d say that I earned as much as $150
once in musicology. But that was really probably just a matter of getting a
piece of paper. I have all the credentials for a Master of Music degree but
figured I should have an M.A. if I wanted to get into teaching anywhere.
That’s why I did that.

MEH: So you came to Black Mountain in the fall of 1949, just after the big fracas
when Albers and that whole group had left?
VH: Right. In fact, I never even saw Albers. He had gone. And we, as a matter
of fact, lived in the house that Albers had vacated, so –
MEH: How – Did you find the school to be what you had expected?
VH: Well, there were all these local jokes about Black Mountain College and
basket weaving and other types of endeavors, as opposed to more
academic studies. I wasn’t quite sure what I was going into except I knew
they had an artistic kind of climate there, so it looked like an excellent
challenge in a sense. And, of course, as a, really a first job for me, I
thought it had a lot of prestige in kind of a left-wing way. So I was pleased
to go there.
MEH: I think there was some prestige in teaching at Black Mountain within the
arts then.
VH: Well, if you look at the roster, there were some very good people there.
Certainly Albers is one example of that, I guess. But there were many
other people who had great competence. Certainly a lot of the students
that emerged from Black Mountain went on to [UNINTEL]. But I think it
was prestigious in that sense.
MEH: What were you – Were there many music students there at that point?
VH: No.
MEH: Any students you remember who were interested in music?
VH: [AFFIRMATIVE].One student I recall is Jay Watt. There were a couple of
others who were seriously interested. Merrill Something comes to mind.
MEH: Gillespie.
VH: Yes. Okay. You know them better than I do. And there was another one,
Mark.
MEH: Hedden.
VH: Hedden. Okay. I, at this point, forget all these people. Mostly I recall
[INAUDIBLE] what they’ve done since, I don’t know.
MEH: You were teaching, well, the basic curriculum in music.
VH: Right.
MEH: Your wife was teaching piano?
VH: [AFFIRMATIVE]
MEH: There was a Margaret Freeman who was teaching chorus?
VH: Well, if you could say she could teach chorus, I think she led the chorus.
MEH: Led the chorus, yeah. Yeah, I haven’t found her. Do you know anything
about her?
VH: Last I knew, they were at McGill. No, excuse me, after that they went to
Pennsylvania – Meadville, Pennsylvania. What’s that school? [Allegheny
College]
MEH: Was that Meadville College? Is that the name of it?
VH: I don’t think so. I bet Louise my wife would know. The fellow Al Freeman,
her husband, taught history at Black Mountain College. Only a couple of

MEH: How would you describe the curriculum at the college? You had basic
courses in theory and composition and whatever? What about concerts? I
found a couple of programs of concerts that were given at the college that
year. Was this a thing that you did regularly, like weekly, or was it a big
event, or –
VH: As a matter of fact, the year I was there, the only significant event would
be the quartet concerts in the summer. During the winter, I recall I played a
few recitals, if you call them, student violin-piano stuff, and I hate to say I
don’t recall any other recitals that were given of that nature on programs.
There was no money for any kind of outside talent so it was strictly pick up
locally, mostly ourselves. I think probably I did about three recitals a year
for them. So—
MEH: That summer, though, you had a special string quartet which concentrated
on contemporary –
VH: Relatively contemporary music, yes.
MEH: Why did you decide in particular to do contemporary music?
VH: Well, I hate to tell you this, but I had an ulterior motive in doing it which
was that we might attract some attention by doing such a program. Had
we gone the typical Bach, Schubert, Beethoven programs nothing would
be made mention of it probably. But I felt playing the really in-depth kind of
program of strictly contemporary things that we might even get recognition
from, say, Time Magazine or something – anything to give us some kind of
publicity at the college and perhaps attract students obviously. So that was
at attempt in that direction. Plus the fact that that audience was pretty well
geared for that kind of music. To put it rather kindly, worst music
[UNINTEL: won ?] the applause. Take that for what you will. But, of
course, I appreciate all kinds of music, but I think particularly they were
interested in things new and different, and it was an opportunity for me, in
a way, too, to work with some contemporary things that normally you
couldn’t just play anyplace. A good example of that might be the Cage –
that quartet was certainly very narrow in scope and would only attract a
certain audience. Beethoven Opus 18 might be a pleasant thing for
anyone. I think that’s kind of the rationale for the programs.
MEH: The two pieces that were premiered that summer – you said there was
some question about whether that was a premiere performance of the
Cage, but I think in his listing he does credit that as the premiere.
VH: The only reason, the question in my mind was because as I mentioned to
you, I had picked up a record of it recently, and on the jacket they stated
the New Music Quartet had performed it for the first time. And I was kind
of surprised to see that because as I look back, or think back, it seemed to
me that we had indeed done the first performance.

MEH: It’s listed in the program as the first performance. It’s listed in the Black
Mountain program as the first performance. Do you remember whether
you obtained the manuscript directly from Cage at that time?
VH: Oh, yeah.
MEH: Did you write and ask him for some music, or –?
VH: Yes. Now the mechanics of that I’m not too swift about right now, but it
seems to me that Cage was well-known at Black Mountain before I went
there, to others on the faculty, and that perhaps somewhere there had
instigated some kind of dialog or what you call it and may have started the
thing going. I do recall having written to him at some point relative to
anything he might be able to contribute for the program. And he did.
MEH: What about the Henning piece?
VH: Well, that was a piece that was written by Ervin Henning for Eleftherios
Eleftherakis, who was the violist in the quartet. As I recall, the
performance was a disaster. You don’t need to quote that, but it was a
very esoteric kind of thing, twelve-tone, and it kind of rambled on and on
and on. I think it was some twenty-four minutes long which, for something
of that structure, is, it’s hard to hold. So we managed to get through it, but
my god it’s—it was sort of just before recording became popular. We had a
recorder, as I recall, but I don’t think they kept the tape, fortunately.
MEH: Of the other three people in the quartet – there was Eleftherakis, Fielder
and Bob Brink. These were people you had known up here?
VH: Yes. I didn’t know Arthur Fielder. He was a friend of Eleftherios
Eleftherakis, and he was recommended by Ted. So he came and joined us
from somewhere like Ohio or somewhere. I can’t recall. It was my only
time that I worked with him so I really didn’t know him too well. He was a
nice person. His wife was a fine pianist, as I recall. They – the Fielders
and Bob Brink did a concert too of classical various trios. That was it.
MEH: Why did you decide to leave the college? You stayed there through an
academic year and through the summer session.
VH: Right. In the fall – I did go back, incidentally, in the fall to find that they had
decided to cut salaries…
MEH: I remember now.
VH: – exactly in half. Well, at that time I had accumulated a couple of
expenses that were pending like I think my wife was pregnant, and I think
we had just bought a new car or something, and all of a sudden it got
rather bleak but the kind of money they were paying – When I went there,
as I recall, I was being paid for one month what I was able to earn while I
was going to school during one week. So you can see that it was a
dramatic change in income, so when they decided to cut the salaries, I felt
that they had in effect violated the contract that we had. I didn’t look it that
way then but [UNINTEL] I guess now I say that. So I felt that I should look
elsewhere for something more lucrative. As it happened, I had been
offered a job while I was up here on vacation, and I turned it down and

MEH: That’s interesting, because now – you’ve said this, I remember – of the
people who came that year, when they hired a lot of new faculty after the
big blow-up, you were actually the only one who came back, you know,
that fall for a while. Most of the people left at the end of the year like Ellen

MEH: Maybe they did.
VH: It didn’t seem to me that too many people left. Ellen Siegel I think was

but I do recall my wife had stayed on a little longer, hopeful that they might
replace me. And in the meantime she was trying to hold the fort, I guess.
But I don’t recall any of the others leaving. [INTERRUPTION IN TAPING]
MEH: When you left Black Mountain, where did you go?
VH: We came back to Boston, and actually I went with a typical road show kind
of affair. It was an orchestra and a singer by the name of Hildegard who
was a so-called “chanteuse” in those days. And I did that just as a
stopgap. I only did that for a couple months until we came back to Boston,
and I stopped off in Boston and decided that that was certainly nothing I
wanted to do. And at that point I thought maybe I would be around at a
time when there was more [UNINTEL] with the Boston Symphony or
something, and at that point I took a job in the electronics business, where
I stayed until now.
MEH: So you’re in the electronics business.
VH: Sort of. At this point I’m kind of semi – I’d say semi-retired. I’m just
continuing on now for tax and other reasons and at some point within the
next year or so I will formally retire.
MEH: What did you do in the electronics business?
VH: Well, I’m a manufacturer’s representative, which is to say I’m a peddler of
sorts. We sell to retailers and [UNINTEL] manufacturers on an
independent basis.
MEH: Have you continued as a performing musician to play?
VH: Well, I did when I first came back. I used to do a lot of radio and theaters
and that sort of thing here in Boston. But I became busy in the electronics
business. You’ve heard of Radio Shacks? Okay, well, I was with Radio
Shack for about eight years, and I ran their high-fidelity department for that
time. So it was the kind of the thing where I got very busy and didn’t have
time really to play anymore and so I stopped playing. In fact, I stopped
playing for thirteen years, went back to it after I came across a very good
cellist here with one of the companies I was representing, and we played
quartets again right here locally in Cambridge, as a matter of fact.
MEH: What about your wife? Did she continue professionally?

VH: Not at all. No. As a matter of fact, she’s worked with me in the business in
the sense of handling the books, as we say. But she didn’t continue
playing. [BREAK IN RECORDING]
MEH: You said the concerts during the summer were the most intensive work
you had done.
VH: Oh, yes. Yeah. Well, when you think of six concerts in six weeks with a
brand-new quartet – people I’d played with before occasionally but not
formally. And all relatively new music to all of us. It was difficult. I can
remember somewhere about the third or fourth week we were doing a
thing by Arthur Bliss, which as a piece of music wasn’t great but
technically it was horrible for first fiddle. And somewhere about halfway
through the week I couldn’t lift the fiddle anymore. It was – you know, we
were doing like ten hours a day on this horrendous piece that – so many
pyrotechnics, you know, meaningless, but they were there. I found I just
couldn’t lift the fiddle after about Wednesday. There was a concert
Saturday night. What do you do? Well I had no choice. I had to stop
playing. So for about a day and a half I didn’t touch the instrument and
finally we rehearsed again for about a day and a half, as I recall, before
the concert in order to play that thing. That was frightening. But the whole
session was done just on a week-to-week basis of – we’d have a concert
Saturday night and we’d have three pieces to perform. And we would
rehearse, as I said, as much as ten hours a day to get them done. And
that excluded individual practice, learning the notes, as we put it.
MEH: How would you judge the quality of the performances? You had said that
the Henning piece you felt was not –
VH: I would say that the performances varied, which is, you know, kind of
getting off the hook. Let’s face it, when you’re a pick-up quartet and you’re
trying to play works of that stature with one week’s preparation, literally, it
can’t be a great performance. However, I would say that they were
workmanlike and some of them actually came off very well.
MEH: You gave these performances in the dining hall?
VH: Yes. Yeah. I always remember that dining hall because of having a tin
roof, because at one of the concerts we gave one night, we were playing a
little Bohuslav Martinů thing for violin and viola. And somewhere along in
the second movement, which was a quiet thing, there were lots of little
“noodles,” as we call them, where you play double stops with a
background of [MAKES de-de-da-da SOUNDS] while the other instrument
plays a little melody of sorts. And we were in the midst of that thing when
a big rain came, and all these big heavy raindrops were dropping on that
tin roof as we were playing. It was just almost perfect accompaniment to
what we were doing, so it worked out nicely. [MEH. The roof actually was
shingles.]
MEH: That summer session, who were the other faculty who were there? I’m
trying to think. Was Motherwell there that summer?

VH: No, he came though just as I left, I believe. My wife, I’m sure, would know.

VH: Yes. I think they’re the ones that became divorced. I remember their flag
waving about living together, and when the faculty was in two factions, one
side against the other. It was too bad.
MEH: Have you remained in touch with any Black Mountain people who were
there?
VH: Not really. We’ve, you know, touched base with one or two, like the
Turners, a couple of times we’ve seen since then. Don Warrington I once
saw. Al Freeman I once saw. Again, that’s the sort of thing. That’s pretty
sparse considering it was thirty years ago, I guess. I’ve seen Joe Fiore’s
name on art posters up in Maine for shows and things. Have you seen or
heard from him at all? Yeah? Is he active up there permanently, or –
