INTRODUCTION:

                                           “This Other Way”
                                  Photography at Black Mountain College

           During an interview for the Center for Creative Photography’s Oral History Program, the

art historian Terence Pitts asked the photographer Harry Callahan how his two-month experience

at Black Mountain College (BMC), a former experimental liberal arts school in North Carolina’s

Blue Ridge Mountains, influenced his thinking on education.1 Comparing his experience to the

Institute of Design (ID) in Chicago, the esteemed photo school where he taught and for which he

is most remembered, Callahan replied:

           At the Institute of Design, well, I thought that was the only way. That’s all I knew. And
           then when I went there, I saw this other way. It was an excellent experience. They called
           us in when we got there and said, “alright what do you want to do?” […] Just everything
           they did was sort of democratic and free.2

Callahan’s answer gestures to what was unique about BMC within the history of mid-century

American photographic pedagogy. The College radicalized the conditions of teaching

photography by replacing the prescriptive set of technical assignments offered in prominent

photography departments with a curriculum based on freedom and collaboration.

Responding to Pitts’s later question about whether he “thought photography could be taught,”

Callahan proposed: “I don’t think anyone can be taught to be creative, but I think they can be put

in an environment which will let it happen.”3

BMC’s photography program, albeit loose and undefined, emerged when photographic

education in America was in flux. Pedagogical institutions then either focused on teaching

technique for vocational practices or for one’s creative vision.4 Starting in the mid-1930s, certain

programs, such as those at ID, The New School for Social Research, and the California School of

Fine Arts (CSFA), gained a reputation for their artistic approach to photography. Although each

of these programs employed a diverse approach from one another and stressed different

applications, they stood out from vocational schools because they taught photography as a

unique branch of the fine arts. At BMC, however, divisions between the commercial and the

artistic, the photographic and the painterly, and the graphic and the plastic disappeared. In

contrast to the rigid curricula of other photography schools, BMC allowed students and faculty to

experiment with a broad range of techniques and collaborate with others across disciplines in a

happenstance environment. The desire to differentiate one’s work from other forms of art faded

in the pursuit of enacting a collaborative environment.

        In his sociological study Art Worlds, Howard S. Becker defines an “art world” as a

community in place and time that produces a set of conventions around the work that their group

creates. Turning to artist communities that diverge from established customs, Becker notes that

unconventional art worlds trade the wider recognition of dominant institutions for greater

autonomy within their own community.5 Applying Becker’s propositions to the writing of

photography at Black Mountain, my thesis argues that the college forged a community of

photographers that embraced isolation and sacrificed recognition on account of forming their

Chapter Outline

       In Chapter One, I analyze the photographic publicity campaign of BMC. Prompted by the

large amount of material on publicity in WRA, I examine how the image of the College in local

and national publications shaped the College’s approach to documentary and photojournalistic

aesthetics; a style that strove to contrast campus life at BMC with that of other colleges without

jettisoning the institution as a site of discipline. I largely focus on the campus bulletins designed

by students and faculty and the news stories commissioned by the publicity committee.

Throughout the chapter, I tie these publications to other material in the archive that recounts the

tedious production of bulletins and the process of working on publicity assignments alongside

Josef Albers to show the complexity of seemingly clear and concise photographs. I describe how

the photographic approach shifted from campus life during the College’s beginning years to

worker photography upon its move to Lake Eden and ultimately to art documentation when the

College’s reputation strengthened after World War II. From the opening to the closing of the

College, publicity constituted a chief objective of photography at Black Mountain.

       Chapter Two begins at a later moment, in 1940, when the College started its move from

the original Blue Ridge Campus to Lake Eden. Upon completion of the Studies Building and with

the end of the war in sight, Albers envisioned an expansive arts program, in which I argue

photography played a critical part. It was at this phase that his emphasis on photography turned

away from publicity as the College’s prestige as an arts center solidified. I organize my discussion

of the photography program at BMC around four teachers: Josef Albers, Beaumont and Nancy

Newhall, and Hazel Larsen Archer. I also discuss critical turning points that paved the way for the

emergence of the program, namely the photographic exhibition and the visiting photographers of

           This chapter explores the photographic promotion of BMC over its quarter-century

existence. These pictures often reappear throughout American art history as documents of who

was there and what occurred. However, the procedures by which these images were circulated,

composed, and edited remain ignored. Nearly every student and faculty member joined BMC

because of the allure of its images, but many realized that the pictures did not reflect the reality

of the College. For example, the student Joe Martin found the campus unkempt and vacant upon

arrival in September 1933, and he “decided the pictures he had seen of ‘Black Mountain College’

did it altogether too much justice.”43 While Martin felt misled by publicity photographs, his

mention of the pictures proves the influence these images had on his decision to enroll. The goal

of the publicity campaign was not to depict the daily life of BMC with veracity, but rather to

promulgate a romanticized view of the College to a wide audience.

The Beginning Years

        Once the opening of the College was announced, there was a need to publicize it to

secure enrollment and funds from donors. This stemmed from the financial hardships of getting

the College off the ground and from the faculty’s desire to establish Black Mountain as a

reputable liberal arts center. The first initiatives were the photographic bulletins designed by

members of the campus community and the commissions of picture stories by outside

photographers working for national periodicals. Much of what was published on BMC featured

recycled photographs, often without proper accreditation for students, faculty, and visiting

photographers.47 The beginning years of the publicity campaign shows signs of experimentation,

shifting between publication format and layout arrangement, but the mode was standardized,

focusing on clear picture stories of the days in the lives of students.

The Work Program

           In the fall of 1942, Albers and the administration increased their publicity efforts due to

the high costs of the construction of the Studies Building. Enrollment and resources were low on

account of the war. On September 18, 1942, Albers held a “Meeting of the Publicity Committee”

with Robert Wunsch and Dorothy Boyd Mattison “to discuss photographs needed.”88

Photographs for trade magazines and news stories were the top priorities, particularly those that

depicted students of the Work Program tending the campus farm and constructing the new

buildings of the Lake Eden campus. The minutes from the meeting resemble a shooting script,

with instructions to show “the land being plowed,” “Lake Eden in tropical summer growth” or

“Lake Eden under a snow blanket,” and “an art student in various activities.” Direct phrases are

also included, such as “No dated pictures,” “Take out the portraits,” or “It is a good time to

photograph now. Trees are more plastic now.” Albers, Wunsch, and Mattison talked of the

importance of the stories that would be published alongside these images, which strove to

romanticize a day in the life of Black Mountain students.

the Work Program. Photographs of the Work Program do not reveal the hardships students and

faculty underwent building the Studies Building. The injuries, frustration, and precarious

working conditions went unrecorded. When analyzed against the college records that illuminate

the production of these images, we see how the documentation of the Work Program resulted in

an extraordinary photographic project that surpassed objective representation.

Publicity After the War

            After the G.I. Bill secured enrollment in the postwar period, photographic publicity at

BMC geared toward establishing the school’s reputation as a locale of the avant-garde.111 The

publicity committee’s approach became less conservative, and the picture stories reflect a more

creative arrangement. Rather than touting the Work Program or the transformation of the natural

landscape, attention shifted to the classroom and the work being produced through BMC’s

radical pedagogy. Elements like the Blue Ridge Mountains remain present in publicity images as

a regional motif, but emphasis is instead placed on the environment as a site for artistic

inspiration rather than as place of work for the cultivation of American citizens. Coinciding with

the end of the war, the workers trope at Black Mountain was replaced by the ethos of the artist.

            A new form of publicity emerged to match the image of BMC as a site of the avant-

garde. Beginning in 1944, the arts at BMC were popularized through the establishment of the

        Black Mountain College entered a new age in the 1940s. After securing nearby property

at Lake Eden in 1937 and completing construction of a campus there by 1941, the College

moved from the Blue Ridge Assembly in the hills to its new home down in the valley. Rather

than being in a central edifice like Lee Hall, the College now took the form of various structures

and the Studies Building arranged around a man-made lake. Students, faculty, and staff no longer

had to pack up the property for summer storage like they did at the assembly.130 They were free

to stay and establish themselves in a setting they could call their own. The initial move and

duties of the war effort minimized the centrality of the arts at Black Mountain, but a sharp,

intense focus on the arts was renewed in the postwar period. With the promise of financial

backing and a strong reputation, Black Mountain was now able to assert itself as a liberal arts

institution where experimentation and self-discovery could flourish. It was against this backdrop

that the photography program emerged.

        Art historians often see BMC as a translation of the Bauhaus in the United States, as

Josef Albers adapted the German school’s practice of merging technology and craft upon his

move to North Carolina. His courses and workshops focused on the material quality and

handwork of crafts, getting at the essence of objects rather than optimizing the outputs of

production.131 Whereas the Bauhaus enabled Albers to experiment in new media, particularly

The Photo Exhibition at BMC

            With the end of war in sight and enrollment on the rise because of the G.I. Bill, Albers

positioned the College as a center of the American avant-garde by inaugurating the Summer Art

Institute in 1944. Although his plans for a permanent gallery were never realized, Black

Mountain hosted travelling exhibitions and offered spaces across its campus for visiting summer

faculty to mount their work. The exhibition program and Summer Institute worked hand in hand,

as the opportunity for a solo show provided another incentive for visiting faculty who received

only a minimal weekly stipend. Visiting photographers of the Summer Institutes were among the

first to host exhibitions at Black Mountain. For many of them, they took the opportunity to

employ new curatorial methods.

The lack of gridlock from a bureaucratic board allowed Newhall to teach as he pleased. While he

           At CSFA, for example, Adams and Minor White revamped the Department of

towards professional applications.”213 Adams implemented a teaching model based on the music

conservatory, in which he emphasized a one-on-one relationship with the instructor rather than

group cooperation.214 Adams and White arranged terms of intensive study with outlined

objectives, required readings, attendance to local photo exhibitions, and individual meetings with

instructors.215 Visiting lecturers for summer workshops were invited to teach courses from six to

eight weeks within a tight timeline of events. Photography students and lecturers at CSFA were

held to high expectations and deadlines, whereas at BMC they could immerse themselves in the

medium without the pressure of mastering photographic techniques and meeting the demands of

professional assignments for outside clients. While the funds of the Photography Department of

CSFA exponentially surpassed BMC’s, the Newhalls initially favored autonomy in how to teach

over fully equipped facilities.

           According to Alice Sebrell, the doors of the Quiet House “provided a revelatory surface

Quiet House represented a metaphor of the camera itself for Larsen Archer and her students.257

The very name of the Quiet House further connects with Larsen Archer’s objective of

strengthening her student’s perception through “visual and acoustical training.”258 For Larsen

Archer, teaching photography extended beyond vision to consider a range of senses that

impacted the awareness of the student. Photography was a psychological process, and it could be

better understood in a state of silence within the reflective space of the Quiet House.

           Larsen Archer and Albers both taught their students to see variance through repetition,

but to different effects. Albers’s process followed a series of manipulations of form and material,

whereas Larsen Archer’s involved active observation until the structure became apparent before

her eyes. Both teachers approached their subjects with a slow and scientific manner, following

the belief that empirical observation reveals the ever-changing variation of form. Yet Larsen

Archer removed Albers’s guiding hand in her pursuit to enact freedom and individual revelation.

experimentation. These teachers never taught photography to the extent that they did at BMC,

photography with limited equipment. Their approaches leveled the hierarchy of the arts on

campus, bringing photography to the ranks of the plastic arts. Most importantly, they cultivated

an environment in which students could forge their own plan of study. This approach constituted

CHAPTER THREE:

                               The Photo Students and Their Projects

           This chapter turns to the photography student. Seven case studies will be reviewed and

examined in connection with the pedagogical influences outlined in the previous chapter, as well

as within the cooperative network that students fostered among themselves. Due to the loose and

collaborative nature of photography at Black Mountain, students at times learned more from each

other than they did from their teachers. The stories of these students detail how photographers

explored, embraced, and even opposed the rules of how to be a “proper” photographer that

circulated throughout the College. Certain groups formed around their similar approaches, like

that of Jerrold Levy, Leo Krikorian, and Felix Krowinski, who captured photographs (mostly

portraits) as a form of community formation. Others, meanwhile, initiated their own projects out

of dissatisfaction with the work of their peers, as seen with Fred Stone and Robert Rauschenberg.

What is unique about the student work discussed here is that their styles cannot be unified under

a homogenous category such as “the school of Black Mountain,” a statement that Edward

Steichen made while viewing photographs that Rauschenberg created at BMC.278 What unites

this group is that they studied the medium for extended periods of time at the College and

encouraged one another, despite their divergent approaches. As Howard S. Becker writes about

art worlds, “Their mutual appreciation of the conventions they share, and the support they

mutually afford one another, convince them that what they are doing is worth doing.”279 The

photographs that exist in the archive revive the conversations that occurred between these

        From 1941 to 1947, photography at Black Mountain remained reserved to the publicity

campaign and the activity of the visiting summer photographers. Through Beaumont Newhall’s

instruction course in photographic technique during the 1947 summer session, interest in creative

photographic pursuits reached new heights among the student body. Hazel Larsen Archer served

Her work came to represent the photographic achievement of postwar Black Mountain, but the

work of the students who tapped into that same momentum remains underexamined.

        Rather than viewing student photographs as merely documentation of friendships for

distant memories, they can be viewed as vehicles for strengthening their bonds and feelings as

artists at that moment in time at Black Mountain. As Elizabeth Edwards and Janice Hart point

out, photographs fit within the material view that “objects are enmeshed in, and active in, social

relations, not merely passive entities of these processes.”300 After departing the College, the

photographers kept collections of one another’s work and relied on these photographs to trace

their memories in various interviews about the College. Yet another layer of this history is

revealed within the archive, as the students’ personal reflections on these photographs attests to

the nuances of their production. Through the framework of their collaboration with other student

Jerrold Levy

           Jerrold Levy is a case in point. Levy came to BMC in 1947 at the age of seventeen after

learning about the College from his stepfather Herbert Bayer. Levy struggled finding his place at

other schools, so Bayer, along with their family friend Ati Gropius, urged his stepson to apply

after he said that he did not want to attend college.301 Levy’s arrival to BMC took some

adjustment, as he was one of the only male students at the time who did not serve in the army

were present. When responding to Mary Emma Harris’s question of how photography was taught

little darkroom with Army surplus stuff.”303 Levy and two other students, Leo Krikorian and

Felix Krowinski, bonded over photography throughout the year, a bond which manifested in

expressive work that engaged with the collaborative and experimental ethos of the College.

photographs played in the maintenance of BMC’s memory, but they also reveal the close

associations that could live on between artists due to the presence of an active photographer.

       The following photography students all studied under the direction of Hazel Larsen

Archer after she established photography as part of the BMC art curriculum in 1949. The move

set forth the idea that these photographers were artists within the larger BMC community, and

that the camera was their medium of choice. When compared with the experience of Levy,

Krikorian, and Krowinski, Larsen Archer’s students primarily worked alone with an individual

studio practice. They benefitted from the exposure to other artist photographers in the forums

and critiques of Larsen Archer’s classroom and collaborated on joint projects, such as Five

Photographers and short films she referred to as “movie photographs,” but the mode of

instruction emphasized individual revelation. Whereas photography was seen as a benefit to the

community, it could now be the conduit through which a student saw the Black Mountain vision,

one by one.

At Black Mountain, Oates began a portrait project inspired by Matthew Brady.399

Attempting to mirror the daguerreotype, Oates purchased Orthochromatic film and posed his

sitters for extended periods of time. Working with a view camera on a tripod, Oates took

portraits for thirty to sixty seconds, embracing unexpected blurs.400 His portrait of Nick

Cernovich, for example, presents a similar sense of uncomfortable stillness found in the pinhole

photographs by Rauschenberg of Twombly (fig. 3.53). While Cernovich’s shirt appears crisp, the

details of the hem and his uneasy expression remain out of focus. Whereas dappled light creates

a lattice-like web of contrasts within Larsen Archer’s portraits, it morphs into a gestural mark

within Oates’s work that causes the countenance of the subject to fade.

student Vera Williams, he places her in the middle ground within a field of flowers (fig. 3.54).

Seen in different stages in the process of blooming, the flowers cause a confusion of depth as

they pop in the foreground. The bright white petals stand out as they envelop Williams and

recede into the background. One of the flower’s stems runs perfectly along the shadow casted

under her right eye, accentuating the mysterious gaze evoked through Oates’s dark exposure.

Oates’s precision matches the framing of his teacher’s portraits, paying particular attention to the

forms of his sitter within nature, though Oates brings the flora into the frame to weave his subject

in and out of darkness. Compared to Larsen’s close-up portraits, in which the expression of the

sitter is brought to the fore, Oates’s photograph of Williams blurs the boundary between portrait

and landscape as the viewer’s focus toggles between Williams and the foliage.

            Looking back on the photography students at Black Mountain, Arthur Siegel states that

“the level of photography was abysmal.”405 Yet the work and stories of these seven students

provides a counter argument. Despite their photographs being overlooked within the broader

narrative of BMC and the history of American photography, the environment of the college

produced profound bursts of photographic activity that speak back to Harry Callahan’s statement

on how to teach photography. Many of the students discussed here moved into vocational

practices and put their cameras away to collect dust, but they held their photographs from the

College close. The student projects that exist today within the BMC archive narrate a grand

experiment in photographic education, one that evened the playing field between student and

teacher, amateur and professional, artist and photographer.

CONCLUSION

        Photography, like all other activities, declined during the last five year of BMC’s

operation.406 Photographers remained present on campus during this period, most notably the

students Marie Tavroges Stilkind, Mary Ann Fretz Giuisi, and Jonathan Williams, but interest in

the medium waned after its peak during the 1951 Summer Session.407 Hazel Larsen Archer

officially departed in 1953, opening a photo studio nearby in the town of Black Mountain

fulfilling portrait commissions for the locals until 1956.408 Unable to pay current faculty, the

College did not attempt to fill Larsen Archer’s position. The Black Mountain Review (1954–57),

a publication founded by Charles Olson, the College’s last rector, and the poet Robert Creeley,

attempted to keep BMC’s photo legacy alive. The Review published an edition with sections

dedicated to Aaron Siskind and Harry Callahan. Student poems precede a selection of the

photographer’s work, reproduced as black-and-white full-bleed spreads. The photo historian

Jonathan Green lists the Review as one of the important antecedents of Aperture, as the

publication drew connections between photography and the literary arts, particularly poetry.409

Poetry emerged as the dominant art of the final years of BMC, forming a movement

dubbed as the “Black Mountain Poets” after Donald Allen and Warren Tallman anthologized the

school in The Poetics of New American Poetry.410 Later comparing the work of the BMC poets

and photographers, Jonathan Williams makes a striking remark in an essay he wrote on Aaron

Siskind: “Were they [Callahan and Siskind] not precisely on another Black Mountain poet’s

wavelength?” 411 Further looking past medium specificity, Williams states in another essay on

Callahan that “Black Mountain was a place that gave you a real chance to find out who you were

and what you could do.”412 For Williams, the importance of BMC rested in its atmosphere of

simultaneous intensity and informality. The locale’s removal from the mainstream sites of

artistic collaboration was key: “You would not have done better at the Deux Magots or the Café

Flore in Paris that summer. And I somehow doubt that you would have found Brassaï and

Cartier-Bresson there amidst the too-many tourists.”413 While the history of Black Mountain is

often divided by developments in certain media, William’s poetic reflections on the state of

photography during the final years of the institution offers a throughline to the influence and

close association that occurred between artists at the College.

           Perhaps what is most overlooked in the history of photography at BMC is the role the

medium played in maintaining connections between artists after they departed the College.

Photographs not only accompanied letters sent between Black Mountaineers, but they were also
