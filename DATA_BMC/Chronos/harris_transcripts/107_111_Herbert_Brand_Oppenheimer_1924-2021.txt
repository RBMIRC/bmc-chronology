MEH: Now you came to Black Mountain in the summer of 1942.
HO: Actually, yes. The very early summer, because the War had begun and all of us
graduating from high school realized our time in school might be limited. So I
went down there I think probably in late June of 1942, and it was – I had been
away from home before. I was eighteen years old.
MEH: You were just out of high school?
HO: Right. I'd been away for the summers, and traveled by myself, but this was
really the first time that I felt I was living away from home.
MEH: How did you hear about the college?
HO: Through a high school art teacher, Victor D’Amico, who was a– Maybe you
knew of Victor?
MEH: Sure.
HO: Oh, really. A remarkable guy, who was the head of the art department at
Fieldston School, where I was in high school. Victor absolutely said "Herbert,
you should go to this school, Black Mountain."

MEH: Why did he say that you should go to Black Mountain?
HO: I'm not sure. He certainly knew of Josef Albers and of the school generally. I
had worked as his assistant. My last year of high school, Victor was teaching
art at Teachers College. He and I really were very close, and I became his
assistant teacher for that summer. But by that time I had already determined,
through his advice, to go there. I thought I wanted to be an art teacher, and I'm
sure it was because I was interested in art. I drew somewhat, I painted very
badly. I really didn't think I had the talent to be a great artist. I was much too
insecure for that. So I had determined that I might be an art teacher, which I
suppose in retrospect must have been flattering to Victor. But he really was
quite impressive. He was the head of the Education Department at the Museum
of Modern Art. I don't know if he was yet running his program out in Long
Island. Was it Skowhegan? I'm not sure what it was. Of course, I hardly realized
how renowned he was as an art teacher. I just knew him as my high school art
teacher who I liked so much. So I went down there.
MEH: You had been part of the progressive education circle.
HO: Sure. Absolutely. I knew of other schools like Antioch. Friends had gone there. I
somewhat knew of Bennington. I don't think I'd heard of Black Mountain though,
until Mr. D'Amico said "Herbert, you would like this place."
MEH: So you went.
HO: And did. And expected particularly to study with Josef Albers. When I arrived,
