CS: I think maybe that many of those.
MEH: Of these. Why don’t we go back a bit? How did you first hear about Black
Mountain?
CS: Well, when I was in graduate school in Boston, I heard about it because
Ruthabeth, among other people—Ruthabeth Krueger at that time—and I gave
a benefit concert for Black Mountain College at the home of one of the Forbes
families there. Eliot Forbes, who was on the faculty at Harvard, was a good
friend of mine. His family was interested in supporting Black Mountain College
because of [circumstance (?)].
MEH: How would he have related to Malcolm Forbes?
CS: They’re probably cousins.
MEH: Probably cousins. Okay.
CS: And I had never heard of Black Mountain College and knew absolutely
nothing but somebody said, you know, “Let’s get a benefit concert for this
school.” “Sure.” So I was involved and I think maybe we gave two or three,
and then Ruthabeth became so interested that she decided to attend Black
Mountain College. She was a very, very gifted violinist. As a young girl she

was sent to Boston and decided to attend—or she went through the Newton
schools very quickly, and I think as a 16-year-old probably went down to
Black Mountain. We kept in touch for a while and she was involved with
helping build the building and so on. But it was a distinguished music faculty
that she went to at that time. Then after the war I moved to North Carolina to
Asheville for a time. Then I became interested again in Black Mountain
College and especially the faculty. Edward Lowinsky, of course, was there,
and I didn’t know very much about him, but a friend of mine who was a
violinist Edwin Frederic Balazs was playing recitals and I accompanied him
and he wanted to go and work with Lowinsky for a little bit. So we played a
recital at Black Mountain College, and I think I played one myself. By this time
of course Ruthabeth had gone. I attended recitals there as often as I could
because it was a very handsome place, a very pleasant place to go. But it
was a very, very minimal kind of association while I lived in Asheville.
MEH: What were in you doing in Asheville?
CS: Trying to make a living. [LAUGHTER]
MEH: Was it involved with music or were you doing something else?
CS: After the war I had no place else to go, and, you know, like everyone else
didn’t want to go home. So I decided to go there. And I was there four-and-a-
half years and I was fairly desperate. I had no job, nothing to do. And just
trying to make a living somehow. I scratched around. Taught a little. Played a
little and so on. And a friend loaned me a cabin in the mountains and I went

there. Brought my piano and started practicing again. And that started the
whole thing off, and I never did leave that area. I came here from there. So—
MEH: Was Jalowetz still at the college when you were associated with it when you
were in Asheville or had he died? He died in January ’46.
CS: Well, I would just have moved there. In January 46 was when I came.
MEH: How would you and describe the program at the college in terms of the type
of thing they were doing in music?
CS: I really know very little about that except they had such a distinguished
faculty.
MEH: Lowinsky was there. Did you know Bodky? Erwin Bodky.
CS: Erwin Bodky. Yes, I knew him, and I knew him in Boston. I didn’t know him
very well. He played the harpsichord I thought rather badly but he has a very
fine book [LAUGHTER] on Bach. Yes, I knew him when he was at Longy
School.
MEH: Were there any other comparable music programs in the Asheville area that
you were aware of?
CS: Oh, no. I taught at Asheville Biltmore College which is now the University of
North Carolina at Asheville, but there was nothing. There were a couple of
interesting people in music in Asheville, but they had virtually no contact with
the college so far as I know.
MEH: Do you know of any persons besides yourself who did have contact with the
college? Did you hear any talk about the college?

CS: Gossip only. [LAUGHTER] “That college!” But—No, it really wasn’t that much
talked about. It was—Very few people would go to the concerts from
Asheville. It was a trip there. Not much these days at all. But it was an effort
to get to it, and you really didn’t know much what was going on except by
chance. So, it was not very frequent, but we went out there, and I didn’t really
see people I knew there. I certainly knew the entire arsenal crowd musically.
MEH: Were you at all aware of activities at Converse College at the time? I think
Ernst Bacon was there then, and there was some communication between
Converse and Black Mountain at the time. I didn’t know that if you might be
aware of that.
CS: No. My home was in outside Asheville and the first year I did almost nothing
except sit there and practice and then I found I had to earn a living so I began
teaching here and there. I became very busy right away. No, I was blithely
unaware of almost everything that was going on in that area.
MEH: So how did you co and in me upon the library? The music library?
CS: After I came here, I heard that the college had closed up and I understood
there were things there. So I went up—A friend from here and I went up to
find out what was going on. Lena Albright—it would be interesting to talk to
Lena Albright just for that one day’s experience perhaps. She and I went up.
And I saw that there were things to be had. I called the president of the
college and said, “May I buy?” He said “Yes” and sent a truck up. Or perhaps
we drove a truck. I’ve forgotten how that worked. We bought what was left of
the library—everything that was left of the library. I think most of the best

things have been taken out already. But we got a lot of this kind of thing. We
have a lot of scores. Parts. Chamber music things. I can show you later but….
They’re widely dispersed now. They’re all over the building. Some of them are
catalogued in the library. And we bought—I think I told you in the letter. We

other one I moved to my house for the summer and it hasn’t come back yet
because mine was off being rebuilt. We got a cello which at that time was a
four hundred dollar cello. A really very nice cello which we’re still using. And
then an assortment of things which were almost unusable. A trumpet. A
clarinet and so on. We got all of that for a thousand dollars.
MEH: That’s a pretty good deal.
CS: It was a wonderful deal. [LAUGHTER] And we figured the reason we got it
was because the poet—What’s his name?
MEH: Charles Olson.
CS: Charles Olson wanted to leave quickly. And we thought, well, he probably
wanted to leave that weekend. Lena Albright admired the looms particularly.
She is a weaver [in the arts?] And she wanted to get a loom. And he told her
price and she said well that was impossible. By the end of the day he gave
her the loom or looms. [LAUGHTER] So she did. She brought them I think for
the Arts Council here in town. The Arts and Crafts Association and the Arts
Council. And that was virtually the only contact I’ve had since that time. I
haven’t even seen the school since it changed hands.

MEH: How would you describe the scores that were still there? Are they mostly
German composers?
CS: There were a very few things that hadn’t been picked over which I found very
interesting. There were some editions of Lili Blumensheh [PH] for instance
and just little sheet music things which were completely unavailable. And we
thought fairly valuable at the time. They all came out of the Surette library.
MEH: Did you have any idea who Surette was at the time?
CS: Oh, yes. I knew his name very well.
MEH: You had been living in the Boston area.
CS: And the Surette—Well, the house is now the summer school at Yale. I think
that was his house. In Norfolk, Connecticut.
MEH: That’s interesting because there’s an oil portrait of Surette that came with the
library that apparently you didn’t get that—It’s still is at the camp. And I think
Wesleyan College—Wesleyan College bought the library—and I think they’re
going to take the portrait just to get it out of the closet where the kids are
graffiti-ing it.
CS: What a shame.
MEH: I didn’t know if there was any—I didn’t know of any place that was around
Concord that might be interesting. They might have been. But anyway,
Wesleyan I think has its hands on it.
CS: They certainly would be very interested. The Yale school.
MEH: Well I’ll check. If Wesleyan finds they’re not interested.

hang in the house in Norfolk, Connecticut. Maybe there is one there.
[LAUGHTER]
MEH: Right. So you were discussing the types of music that were in the collection—
those that remained.
CS: Mostly chamber music. I would say this kind of thing. A few opera scores. Oh,
I’d say maybe 15 or 20 opera scores. And most of the standard literature in
chamber music. The string quartets. All the trios. All this kind of thing. I
believe most of it—that’s what it is. And then there would be a fair, you
know… I’d say almost all the standard chamber music was there. And a fair
collection of the older editions of, oh, the Beethoven piano sonatas and
Mozart sonatas and things of that sort. Old editions—not really old—but the
things that Surette would’ve bought in the early 1900s. All the keyboard
literature.
MEH: That was the idea. I think the college felt it was a very good teaching
collection of standard materials.
CS: And we’ve used the materials from time to time. We don’t really use them all
that much because the editions aren’t that good anymore.
MEH: Do you recollect there being any bindings that were outstanding? I mean,
most of the stuff college bound when it came. It’s very utilitarian.
CS: All that I got was just like this.
MEH: What about records? Did you get records?
CS: No.

CS: No. But I was here when he came on his way home. [LAUGHTER] Yeah. An

MEH: What do you remember about it?
CS: Well, I don’t remember who fought with him. But there was a… he was at one
end of the room I think with Merce Cunningham. And I’ve forgotten who was
at the other end. Ruthie Julian had somehow invited people who hated each
other, and they were all the way to New York at the end of the season and
found themselves at the Julian’s house. And I’ve never heard anyone
attacked as John Cage was attacked that evening. It just went on and on. We
sat in the middle. It was like a tennis match. [UNINTE] going this way and this
way. And John Cage’s extraordinary calm and good humor maintained even
though he was attacked constantly about what he was doing in music, about
his life, about his sex, about everything that… and, you know, and still all this
charm and will good humor continued. [LAUGHTER] There was a violinist.
No, there was a pianist there who wanted to play nothing but the Kreutzer
Sonata. A violinist [on our faculty (?)]. Which he did.
MEH: Was it David Tudor? [MEH: Correction. I think Tudor was not with them.]
CS: I’m not sure it was David Tudor.

MEH: Yes, I met her when I was with the North Carolina Museum.
CS: Because she would have something to say about that. Some program. She

MEH: Do you remember any of the summer programs at Black Mountain when

CS: There was a—The thing that struck me one time was an exhibit of leaf

was the first place that I saw that had just lots of very beautiful girls dressed

men with beards and all that, of course, predated anything that took place
later on by a number of years. Well, I don’t know.
MEH: Do you think Black Mountain had any real influence in the Asheville area?
CS: It would be difficult to say that it had an important influence. Yeah. It had a
very strong negative influence, [LAUGHTER] and I think in the Black
Mountain area itself. No, there were too few people who really knew and
appreciated what Black Mountain was. And it took me a long time to become
the least interested in it except as a peripheral kind of thing. It wasn’t the
easiest time of my own life and I was—I was scratching around as hard as I
could to make a living so I didn’t have much time for that. I don’t know who
would be better able to talk about that. One of the very old people. A Mrs.
