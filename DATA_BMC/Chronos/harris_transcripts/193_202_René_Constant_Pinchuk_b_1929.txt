MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Rene, how did you come to be at Black Mountain
College?
RP: Well, a friend of mine was attending Black Mountain. Ray Johnson. He kept
writing me. He kept writing me details of his classes and so on, and I was just
out of high school at the time and I was about to go to another art school but it
sounded really interesting. So, I went there for the Summer Art Institute of
1946, and it turned out to be totally fascinating. It was without a doubt the most
inspiring school I had ever been to.
MEH: Where had you been in high school?
RP: Detroit. Detroit, Michigan.
MEH: And the high school?
RP: Cass Tech High School.
MEH: Do you remember how you physically got to Black Mountain that summer?
RP: Yes, I remember going through I guess the Blue Mountains, was it, on the
Southern Railway. It was one of these old fashioned carriages, with swinging
emergency chains and the lights – the electric lights – looked like kerosene

MEH: What was Albers teaching? Was he teaching Color or Design, or – ?
RP: Both. I took both courses, as I remember. My memory is not that trustworthy,

Then, at the other end of the spectrum, there was John Varda there, and his
attitude towards color was kind of a mystical, symbolic thing that he had. He
had these theories of being able to do anything as long as there were islands in
the midst of something else. So, we would do islands, and there was merit to it,
you know. It's like everything. There were things to be learned about, from his
approach as well. Yet, he was diametrically opposite to Albers, who was terribly
methodical and terribly – well Germanic, of course, is what everybody says. But
nevertheless it was true (LAUGHS).
MEH: How would you describe Varda from that summer?

Ford or whatever with floorboards which were missing and always loaded up
with good-looking young females. He had a great preference for that, of course.
