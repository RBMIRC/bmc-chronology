FOREWORD

The story of Black Mountain College (BMC) has grown to mythical proportions since the liberal arts school officially closed its doors in 1957. Countless books, exhibitions, and artworks have been created to capture what it was like to live and learn at this singular college and to chart its unparalleled cultural legacy. But before this book, nobody had written about the farm at BMC in any great detail.

Nestled in the breathtaking Appalachian Mountains of Western North Carolina, Black Mountain College was a beacon of experimental art practice and communal living in America. Against the odds, the school was founded with limited financial resources during the Great Depression and, over the years, it became one of the most unique creative nexuses of the 20th century. Emphasizing creativity and freedom, BMC nurtured an extraordinary cast of practitioners of all types from dancers and designers to poets and painters. BMC’s teachers and students dramatically changed the worlds of art and education in the US and beyond.

The Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center (BMCM+AC), founded in 1993 in Asheville (about 15 miles from the college’s old site), is dedicated to preserving BMC’s history and continuing its legacy through an active series of programs and publications. Every fall BMCM+AC hosts an incredible gathering called REVIEWING Black Mountain College, an interdisciplinary conference where researchers and enthusiasts present findings and new perspectives on the school. One of the most captivating regular speakers is David Silver, professor and chair of environmental studies at the University of San Francisco, who has presented at the conference every year for the past 10 years. David’s passion and charisma shine through when he speaks about BMC’s often overlooked farming operation, which ran for the length of the school’s existence and engaged so many of its students, faculty, and staff.

David’s deep research and attention to detail is awe-inspiring; so is his enthusiasm as he recounts the names and types of animals, every student involved in the farm’s upkeep, and the minutiae of what it took to run a farm on the grounds of one of the world’s most famous art colleges. Through robust, dedicated, and original research, David’s book sheds light on the land’s significance and impact on college life.

The farm was crucial to BMC’s mission, providing students with hands-on learning opportunities and promoting sustainable agriculture practices—not to mention feeding the college’s residents. David has not only looked over every timetable and all accounts kept at the farm, he’s transformed anecdotes and ephemera into a cohesive and captivating narrative that tells the story of the land and the people who made it happen, as it’s never been told before.

The Farm at Black Mountain College offers fresh perspectives on the creative life of the school by examining how the farm provided the conditions for significant innovations in craft, performance, drawing, photography, painting, sculpture, and writing. It vividly describes the farm’s inauspicious beginnings, in 1933, and the fruitful relationships with local organizations that helped it thrive. The book introduces a new cast of characters to BMC lore and shows how some of the college’s most recognized names, like artists Josef Albers and Ruth Asawa, were involved in its upkeep. Significantly, this compelling narrative challenges the conventional understanding that BMC was an isolated utopia and provides a fresh perspective on how the college’s philosophy of experiential learning and collaboration influenced students’ artistic work, ultimately shaping much of the American art scene.

We sincerely thank David for his exceptional work bringing this story to life. Through his creativity, he has produced a captivating collection of ideas, showcased in this book and the exhibition, The Farm at Black Mountain College College, co-curated by Bruce Johansen, on view at BMCM+AC from 27 September 2024 to II January 2025.

The Farm at Black Mountain College is a remarkable project that showcases the work of many artists and collaborators. It is made possible thanks to major support from the North Carolina Humanities, an organization devoted to connecting North Carolinians through culture experiences. We are also deeply grateful to the Wyeth Foundation for American Art, the Ruth Foundation for the Arts, and the Windgate Foundation for their contributions.

Finally we thank the entire BMCM+AC and Atelier Editions teams who have worked tirelessly to make this book a reality. David’s work sheds light on BMC’s interdisciplinary approach and its commitment to social and ecological engagement through transformative practices that extend beyond the boundaries of the art world. INTRODUCTION

The bullying began as the five men piled into art and writing student Tom Field’s Buick Roadster. It continued for four miles, down and around the curvy mountain roads from Black Mountain College to Ma Peak’s, the local tavern. Over multiple rounds of Ballantine Ale the needling escalated, coming mostly from writing professor Robert Creeley and art student Dan Rice, and focused exclusively on Tom. Some of the taunting was homophobic, some stemmed from jealousy of Tom’s superior painting skills, some was fueled by literal hunger. By then—late summer 1955—the last Black Mountain College farmer was long gone, and the campus, which had come to rely on the now-collapsed farm for subsistence, was in the grips of a severe food crisis. Fuming, Tom drove his tormentors back to campus. The bullying did not cease.

At his wits’ end, Tom aimed his car at Meadows Inn, the two-story cottage he shared with other students, and pushed the pedal to the metal. A massive Boom/, then silence, then groans. The Buick had rammed against the fieldstone chimney and rested, student Michael Rumaker remembered, “like an accordion.” The cottage shook, then shuddered, then was still.

“It was a battlefield,” recalled college rector Charles Olson of the post-crash scene. “The place was strewn with these bodies—broken backs, broken hips.” An hour later, after the injured had been carted off to the hospital, a confused sheriff surveyed the scene and scratched his head. He’d seen everything, he said to no one in particular, but “I have never heard of a man driving his own car into his own home.”

More than two decades earlier, in 1933, and against all odds, Black Mountain College (BMC) was established in Western North Carolina, just outside of Asheville. From the start the small, rural liberal arts college was a radical departure from other institutions of higher education. Faculty owned the college. Students, faculty, and a small staff operated it together. There were no departmental chairs, deans, provosts, or presidents, and certainly no board of trustees. Fine arts, music, theater, and dance were placed at the center of the curriculum. Learning by doing was encouraged, and students were expected to take ownership of their education. Most striking, the college was communal. Students, faculty, and faculty families lived, studied, worked, and socialized together.

Funds were always an issue. The school survived through the Great Depression and World War II, partially on the kindness of donors, but also on the rewards reaped by hardworking members of the BMC community who kept the campus farm producing food. Despite these earlier efforts, throughout the 1950s, with no money and a wayward faculty and student body, the college started to crumble, ultimately collapsing in 1956—just a year after the fateful car crash.

The story that’s already been told time and again is that despite its perpetual financial woes, remote location, and brief 23-year existence, Black Mountain College attracted a jaw-dropping band of famous and soon-to-be famous artists, writers, thinkers, and visionaries from the US, Europe, and beyond, a veritable who’s who of mid-20th-century Western art and ideas. An abridged list of faculty and students includes Anni and Josef Albers, Ruth Asawa, John Cage, Robert Creeley, Merce Cunningham, Willem de Kooning, Robert Duncan, Buckminster Fuller, Paul Goodman, Ray Johnson, Franz Kline, Jacob Lawrence, Robert Motherwell, Charles Olson, Robert Rauschenberg, M. C. Richards, and Cy Twombly. Nearly all books and exhibitions about Black Mountain College focus on the famous folk who taught, visited, or studied at the school.

This book is different. The Farm at Black Mountain College follows renegade students, faculty, farmers, and community partners as they start and sustain a campus farm in the 1930s, build a better farm in the 1940s, and watch it all collapse in the 1950s. What emerges is a new cast of characters with riveting stories that shed a brighter light on what exactly happened at BMC through the decades. The farm was of vital importance to the college, not only because it provided necessary food from organic farming, but because it served as a testing ground for self-sufficiency, communal living, and collaboration—the most precious and precarious ingredient at the college. In these pages, the spotlight is on the extraordinary contributions of these folk, many of whose stories have seldom, if ever, been explored.

The Farm at Black Mountain College is the product of extensive archival research. Over the last decade, I have made a dozen trips to the Western Regional Archives (WRA) in Asheville, North Carolina, to immerse myself in their Black Mountain College Collection. I have conducted research using other BMC-related collections at ten other archives and centers, including the Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center, the Getty Research Institute, the Swannanoa Valley Museum & History Center, and University of Arizona’s Center for Creative Photography. I have also had the privilege of speaking with ten former Black Mountain College students—Mary Daniels, Ted Dreier Jr, Marita Pevsner Dresner, Martha King, Trueman MacHenry, Ron Robertson, Dorothea Rockburne, Claude Stoller, Marie Tavroges Stilkind, and Martha Treichler—most of whom lit up at the very mention of the farm.

A NOTE ON NAMES

Whenever possible, I refer to people by the names their peers used. For example, Mary “Molly” Gregory, faculty of woodworking, was called Molly, not Professor Gregory or similar. In cases where I am unsure what they were called, I use the standard practice of referring to them by their last names.

CHAPTER 1

BEFORE BLACK MOUNTAIN

[Légende] Anne “Nan” Howard Chapin, Rollins College student and dropout, and founding member and student of Black Mountain College, 1934.

Without Nan Chapin, there is no Black Mountain College. Born in 1913, Anne “Nan” Howard Chapin grew up in Stafford Springs, Connecticut, the youngest of six children raised on a shoestring budget by her widowed mother, Anne. When Nan was 15, her mother married Colonel Arthur S. Dwight, a well-to-do engineer. The family moved to Dwight’s home in Great Neck, Long Island, the new-money town that F. Scott Fitzgerald used as a model for arriviste suburb West Egg in The Great Gatsby. With a deep interest in experimental education, Anne persuaded Nan to attend the School of Organic Education in Fairhope, Alabama, for two winters during her junior and senior years of high school. Founded by education reformer Marietta Johnson, the school espoused progressive principles by eliminating tests and grades, offering students active, group, and experiential learning environments, and encouraging community building. Through her experiences in Alabama, Nan, who pulled her hair back into a ponytail and whose conventional style belied her open-minded nature, started to develop her own interest in unconventional education. When it came to thinking about college, she found Rollins College, in the small leafy city of Winter Park, Florida, near Orlando, most interesting.¹

In 1930 Rollins College was indeed interesting. Five years earlier, Hamilton Holt, former editor and publisher of the liberal weekly magazine The Independent, master of generating publicity, and possessor of mildly innovative ideas about education reform, had become president of the sleepy liberal arts college. Through tireless fundraising, especially among the wealthy inhabitants of Winter Park, Holt had raised enough money to rebuild the campus in a Spanish-Mediterranean style, dominated by white stucco and orange tile roofs, and secured commissions to build a new library, theater, dormitory, and the Knowles Memorial Chapel. With a huge donation from Frances Knowles Warren, made in memory of her father, one of the college’s original trustees, Holt commissioned world-renowned college and church architect, Ralph Adams Cram, to replicate a 17th-century classical chapel Cram had seen in Toledo, Spain. Holt established new interdisciplinary professorships with provocative titles like “Professor of Books,” “Professor of Evil,” and “Professor of Leisure,” which he filled with what he called “golden personalities” who would, ideally, ignite engagement among students. He curbed traditional lectures and classroom recitations, replacing them with small group discussions and one-on-one meetings between student and professor. Students were granted near freedom to pursue electives related to their own interests.²

For three years, Nan poured her mind and muscle into Rollins College. She got good grades and was a sorority sister of Gamma Phi Beta, where she became best friends with Elizabeth “Betty” Young and Sara “Sally” Sylvester. Nan was also a member of the Glee Club, the Student Association, the International Relations Club, Le Cercle Français, Rollins Literary Society, and the Cosmopolitan Club. She was especially active in the Rollins Theatre Workshop, where she worked closely with drama professor Robert “Bob” Wunsch. Throughout the 1932–33 school year, she accompanied Wunsch to nearby Eatonville where a young Zora Neale Hurston was writing, rehearsing, and directing a community play that would become From Sun to Sun. Visiting night after night to watch rehearsals, Nan recalled being followed by local Ku Klux Klan members who would tell her and Wunsch to stay where they belonged. Nan continued attending the rehearsals, as well as the actual play, and worked with Wunsch to stage From Sun to Sun at Rollins. Although Holt agreed to host the play, he demanded it take place in the Recreation Hall, rather than the college’s new Annie Russell Theatre, and that the all-Black cast perform to a whites-only audience. Holt may have considered himself a progressive, but his decisions were influenced by the status quo.³

While Nan enjoyed theater, her favorite class at Rollins was whatever class John Andrew Rice was teaching. Like Nan, Rice had arrived at Rollins in September 1930. He was one of Holt’s hotshots, hired to teach classics. Already forced out of three schools (the Webb School, the University of Nebraska, and the New Jersey College for Women), Rice arrived with a reputation as a campus gadfly, burner of bridges, and phenomenal teacher. A Rhodes Scholar and master of Socratic dialogue, he would enter a classroom, light his pipe, ask a few questions, and let the conversation take over. Nan especially enjoyed his Athenian Civilization class in which students would consider what constituted an “ideal college,” and how such considerations could and should apply to Rollins. Rice relentlessly encouraged students to explore not just ideas, but their own preconceived notions of those ideas. Sometimes the discussion was so interesting that when the end-of-class bell rang, students would rise from their chairs, walk out of the classroom, cross a few well-manicured lawns, circle a lake, and climb the stairs to the large patio outside Rice’s cottage where they would continue the discussion over iced tea for several more hours. Many of Rice’s neighbors, including Theodore “Ted” Dreier, faculty of physics, and his wife Bobbie, joined the lively conversations. “Rollins was sort of rocking,” Bobbie recalled. “Rice made a there, there.”⁴

Despite his loyal student following, Rice was a bully. In class, he taunted and teased intellectually weaker students, often bringing them to tears. “He got a kick out of putting people down when they were stupid,” remembered Bobbie. A shortish, pudgy, tweed-wearing man who perpetually had a pipe in his mouth, Rice had a reputation for impropriety in all regards. One day, a potential donor was touring the campus when she came upon Rice sunbathing in the backyard of his college housing, dressed in “extremely revealing underwear.” Unfazed, Rice stood up and conversed with the shocked woman for 15 minutes. As art historian Lewis Shelley wrote of the unperturbed professor, “His agnostic beliefs, his frank manner of expression, and his occasionally immodest dress had caused him to be condemned by a small segment of people in the conservative community.” Dreier put it simply: “He antagonized the donors.”⁵

[Légende] During her three years at Rollins, Nan was interested and involved in everything, including the Cosmopolitan Club, Gamma Phi Beta sorority, the Glee Club, the International Relations Club Le Cercle Francais, Rollins Literary Society, Rollins Theater Workshop, and (pictured here, seated, second from left) Rollins College Student Association, 1932.

Once, after the campus chapel’s inaugural Christmas service, during which praise was showered on attending donors, and which concluded when a suspended star spotlighted in bright pink traversed on a wire to dramatic effect from the back of the chapel to the front, Rice had had enough. As he walked out of the vestibule, Rice, vexed by what he considered the vaudevillian nature of the service, pointed to a bronze plaque declaring the place of worship open “to the glory of God,” in significantly smaller lettering than that which marked the memory of trustee Knowles. Turning to one of his students, but loud enough so others could hear, Rice declared the service “obscene.” Holt, the board of trustees, and attending donors were predictably unimpressed.⁶

[Légende] Rollins College professor of classics and “golden personality” John Andrew Rice, steering, with faculty of history and crew coach Cecil Oldham, left, and Theodore “Ted” Dreier, faculty of physics, in the Marlin on the Indian River near New Smyrna Beach, Florida, 1932.

To make matters worse, Rice chaired or was a member of multiple faculty committees that assessed several of President Holt’s most cherished initiatives. In spring 1932, for example, Rice chaired a faculty committee that recommended abolishing fraternities and sororities on campus, claiming they favored rich students and encouraged class divisions and cliques. Holt, as well as some alumni and donors, understood the report as a personal attack. Then, in fall 1932, Rice served on a committee examining Holt’s deep cuts in faculty salaries, a decision the president argued was caused by the Great Depression. The committee’s report, written by Rice, reached an entirely different conclusion, namely that the source of the college’s deficit was Holt’s building spree. Lastly, Rice helped write a report in favor of eliminating one of Holt’s curriculum models that was creative but confusing to students and faculty. Holt was livid.⁷

Finally, in spring 1933, Holt fired Rice, a tenured professor, for being “very, very indiscreet, and worse than that, quite intolerant, and worse than that, insulting.” By the end of the semester, representatives from the American Association of University Professors (AAUP) were called to campus to convene a public hearing on the matter. The hearing took place in Knowles Chapel for a full ten days and consisted mostly of Holt personally reading 52 letters and signed statements from students, faculty, staff, and townspeople against Rice. Some were petty or amusing (one testimony accused Rice of walking around the college’s beach house in a jockstrap, to which the classics professor retorted, “I’ve never worn a jockstrap in my life!”), while others contained accusations of bullying. A few days after the public hearing and that year’s graduation, Holt fired five more professors, including Frederick Georgia (chemistry) and Ralph Lounsbury (political science), accusing them of supporting Rice. Holt wanted to fire physics professor Dreier but hesitated, since he was the nephew of Margaret Dreier Robins, a wealthy and influential trustee. Out of protest, Dreier, and later Wunsch, resigned. By mid-June, the purge was complete: nine out of 45 Rollins professors had been fired, forced to resign, or had resigned out of protest.⁸

Nan was furious and quickly rallied fellow students into action. Together with Nathaniel “Nat” French, Rollins’ student body president, and George Barber, editor of the student newspaper The Sandspur, Nan led a student delegation that met with Holt to proclaim their unwavering support for Professor Rice. When that didn’t work, Nan and others organized a campus protest. When that didn’t work, Nat and Barber invited the local press to campus and announced they were leaving the college. When that didn’t work, Nan organized the final action: she and over a dozen Rollins students dropped out. Dejected, Nan returned to her family home in Great Neck. It was early June 1933.⁹

Later that evening, the Colonel invited his friend William S. Barstow to stop by. An electrical engineer who had made a small fortune in public utility investment banking, Barstow listened to Rice’s pitch, witnessed Dwight’s exuberance, and declared, “If the Colonel’s going to put up a thousand dollars, I will, too!” Amidst all the excitement, Nan boldly declared that she would be attending the new college, making her its first student. As the night came to an end in Great Neck, Rice departed with $2,000 in seed money, one full-paying student, and a summer home for his family.

STARTING A COLLEGE

The very next day Nan hit the ground running by taking charge of student recruitment. First, she contacted a number of Rollins rebels, shared the idea for a new college, and asked if they could commit to $1,000 a year for tuition and board. Her friends from the Student Association, Nat French and George Barber, were early enthusiasts, as was Betty, Nan’s former sorority sister, who was shocked when her conservative parents approved.

Next, Nan assigned geographic regions to the early commits so they could recruit prospective students. While former Rollins student and water polo player Ed Jenks canvased Philadelphia, Nat, boyishly handsome and on crutches due to polio, covered the Boston and Cambridge areas. Nan took the tristate area of New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut, meeting with fellow Glee Club member Mary Trowbridge, International Relations Club member Everett “Ev” Dwight, and former Rollins schoolmate Norm Weston. Together Nan, Nat, and Jenks kept in touch with Rice and others.¹¹

Nan’s smartest move was to organize a gathering of committed students in New York City where they wrote a collective letter announcing the new college and sent it to other Rollins students, current and former, inviting them to join. The letter read:

    We, the undersigned students, who have participated in the organization of Black Mountain College, join with the members of the faculty in presenting for your consideration the enclosed announcement of the college.

    We feel that you would appreciate the opportunity to participate with a congenial group in a thoroughly cooperative educational enterprise. We are confident that the things that were talked about at Rollins can be realized in the new environment and that other important advances can and will be made.

    This letter and the enclosed announcements are being sent only to such students as have been selected by a committee of faculty and students. It should not be construed as an attempt to persuade you to leave Rollins if you have reached a definite decision to return.

It must have felt empowering for these young people, especially during a time of grave financial uncertainty, to take control of their educational pathways. It must have felt exciting, during an era in which everything seemed to be on pause, to strive toward something with conviction. Whatever the case, as a result of their spirited letter, five would-be seniors at Rollins—Sydney Carter, Laura Belle Fisher, Marcella Martin, Alice Lee Swan, and Sally Sylvester—left Rollins and agreed to come on board. As August came to an end, the college had more than 15 committed students.¹²

With Nan leading the recruitment charge, Dreier and Rice focused their efforts on fundraising. From the beginning, Dreier—blond, boyish, and with seemingly unending reserves of physical energy and idealism—was the money guy. Maybe this was because he came from a lot of money. Born in 1902 in Brooklyn Heights to a family that had inherited a fortune made in the iron trade, Dreier’s extended family, especially the women, were active leaders in social reform and the arts. His mother, Ethel Eyre Dreier, was president of the Women’s City Club of New York and head of the Women’s Suffrage Party of Brooklyn. His aunt, Margaret Dreier Robins, was president of the National Women’s Trade Union League and, among other things, had been a trustee at Rollins College but resigned in protest when Rice got fired. Another of Ted’s aunts, Katherine Dreier, was a painter who, along with Marcel Duchamp and Man Ray, founded the Société Anonyme, an organization that promoted modern and avant-garde art.¹³

In 1921 while still enrolled at Harvard, first to study geology and later electrical engineering, Dreier became best friends with his roommate Malcolm “Mac” Forbes. When he graduated, Dreier married Barbara “Bobbie” Loines, who came from an equally wealthy and social reform-minded family. After working five years as an engineer at General Electric, the course of Dreier’s life would change in late summer 1930 when the head of Rollins’ physics department was killed in a car accident. With a recommendation from Forbes, who by then was teaching psychology at Rollins, Holt interviewed and hired Dreier as an assistant professor of physics. For three years Dreier did nothing to distinguish himself at Rollins, except near the end when he resigned to protest the firing of Rice.¹⁴

Dreier began his fundraising efforts for the new college close to home. His parents, Henry Edward and Ethel, got things started with a $2,000 contribution. Next, Dreier hit the road to tap into his and wife Bobbie’s extensive family networks. Oftentimes the pair would bring along Nan, who charmed potential donors with her bright insights into what progressive education could and should be. With her help the Dreiers secured a modest $500 from family friends.¹⁵

Though he had a rocky start of it, Rice would eventually prove a more successful fundraiser. He managed to secure meetings with higher-ups at both the Carnegie Foundation and the Rockefeller Foundation, but each time he was asked for something concrete—a vision statement, learning goals, a plan—Rice refused, sabotaging the college out of potential funding. In mid-August, however, Dreier’s old roommate Forbes invited Rice to his family’s sprawling estate on Naushon Island, Massachusetts. Forbes found Rice arrogant, but believed him to be an exceptional teacher, and he listened with growing interest as Rice shared his vision of a whole new kind of college. Forbes agreed to give $5,000 to the venture on the condition that the donor remain anonymous. Not to be outdone by her husband, Forbes’ wife Ethel matched his pledge.¹⁶

By late August, after only two months of recruiting students and fundraising, Rice and Dreier had secured $30,000 through tuition and donations. With these funds they rented Blue Ridge Assembly for the school year and retained three staff members: Jack and Rubye Lipsey, as chef and assistant chef, and Bascombe “Bas” Allen to fire the furnaces. They were also able to hire ten faculty members who agreed to join the venture for room and board, but with no salary. A few of these were Rollins rebels, others had been discovered by Rice while traveling to recruit students. As education professor and Rice biographer Katherine C. Reynolds writes, “In six weeks, less time than it now takes to decide who should speak at a college commencement, a small band of men had started a small college.”¹⁷

Yes, but, the “small band of men” didn’t do it alone. It was Nan who organized Rollins students after Rice’s firing, bringing together a group willing to take chances for an education they believed in. It was Nan who arranged the dinner that resulted in seed money for the college and a summer base for Rice’s operations. It was Nan who became the first committed student. And finally, it was a group led by Nan that cajoled 16 students to commit time and money to a college that was still just an idea. Without Nan Chapin, Black Mountain College never happens.

Back at Great Neck, in mid-September 1933, Nan excitedly packed up her belongings and traveled in a Packard limousine with her parents from Long Island to Penn Station. There she met up with Jenks and English and drama professor Hilda “Peggy” Loram, as they piled into fellow BMCer-to-be Norm Weston’s Buick and drove 700 miles south, just outside the town of Black Mountain, North Carolina, to start a new college.¹⁸

CHAPTER 2

THE FOUNDING OF THE FARM

[Légende] Norman “Norm” Weston, part-time student, part-time assistant bookkeeper, and full-time founder of the farm, 1934.

In early October 1933, Helen Boyden, a Radcliffe-trained economics instructor at the two-week-old Black Mountain College, received a book in the mail from a friend. It was Ralph Borsodi’s Flight from the City: An Experiment in Creative Living on the Land, published earlier that year. The book followed the author, his wife Myrtle Mae, and their two young sons’ flight from Manhattan, where Borsodi worked in advertising, to rural Upstate New York where they started a homestead, a modest dwelling combining subsistence agriculture with do-it-yourself food preservation, and driven by the goal of self-sufficiency. The Borsodis grew and canned their own food, raised livestock, and made, sewed, and built as much as they could. Along the way Borsodi kept detailed financial logs of costs and savings, and discovered that with help from modern technologies, such as tillers in the garden and an electric mixer in the kitchen, the homestead was able to turn a modest profit. “It was a romantic vision,” Boyden recalled over three decades later about one of the effects the Great Depression had on family life. “Since there were no jobs in the city, there was quite a movement back to the countryside.” Boyden passed the book along to one of her new students, John Applegate, not knowing that within a week or so the book would pass through dozens of student hands. Borsodi’s withdrawal from urban life and quest for self-sufficiency on a rural, modern homestead resonated with these optimistic students and faculty looking for a new way to live and learn. By the end of October, Boyden remembered, Flight from the City “swept the college like wildfire.”¹⁹

At the center of the wildfire was Norm Weston. Tall, slender, and usually shy, Norm had struggled in school from the start. Born in 1913 in Wilton, Connecticut, he suffered from both dyslexia and disinterest. His parents sent him to Solebury School, a small prep school in Pennsylvania, where Norm underachieved. “I wasn’t very bright,” he recalled. “The word ‘dyslexia’ was not in the dictionary in those days.” One of his favorite school days was also one of his last. In late spring 1932, Solebury hosted Holt, who told of how Rollins had done away with traditional lectures and classroom recitations in favor of small group discussions. Holt also mentioned that Rollins did not require College Board Exams, SAT-like tests that Weston had bombed earlier that spring. That was the clincher. After graduation he returned home and somehow managed to convince his financially struggling parents to pay for a year at Rollins.²⁰

Norm arrived at Winter Park in September 1932 with a hazy interest in economics and a passion for crew. He rowed varsity and became close with teammate Ed Jenks and Coach Cecil Oldham, who also taught history. He also formed a friendship with his next-door neighbor Nat French. But by far the most interesting person Norm met at Rollins was Nan, who seemed to know everyone and be involved in everything.²¹

Early the next year when Holt fired Rice, Norm was indifferent. He knew it was wrong to fire a tenured professor but had never taken a class with Rice and barely followed the controversy. Norm was far more concerned with how he would pay for a second year of school. In early June, he hitched a ride home to Wilton with Coach Oldham, who had just been fired by Holt. Relieved of his job, Oldham was driving to New York to sail back to his home in England. By the time they reached New York, Norm had agreed to use all of his savings to purchase Oldham’s car, a 1929 Buick Roadster.²²

The summer of 1933 was rough for Norm. Living at home, broke but with a Buick, he applied for a scholarship for another year at Rollins but was rejected. Besides “selling a bit of Real Silk hosiery to my mother’s friends,” he had no prospects. Norm’s big break came in July when the Dreiers and Nan were on a fundraising trip in nearby Redding, Connecticut, and Dreier’s car broke down, leaving the trio stranded. Nan called Norm; Norm jumped in his Buick, picked up the deserted party, and drove them to the prospective donor. From that point forward, Norm became a regular chauffeur for the fundraising party as they traversed the tristate area. The more he learned about the new college, the more interested he became. With neither money nor good grades to make him an appealing candidate, he must have breathed a sigh of relief when in late August Dreier said he could attend BMC for free.²³

High on Borsodi’s book, Norm, Nan, Nat, and others shared their excitement with Dreier who, although generally well-liked by students was, by all accounts, a lousy teacher. His math and physics classes rarely attracted more than one or two students—sometimes none. “I tried to learn trigonometry with Ted Dreier,” Norm recalled. “Either he couldn’t teach or I couldn’t learn.” Dreier’s contributions lay elsewhere, primarily as the college’s treasurer, chief fundraiser, and advocate for physical labor. Tireless, he was always the first volunteer to chop wood or haul coal, ascribing spiritual virtue to manual labor—a tendency that some people found inspiring, some annoying. “Ted had this notion,” Rice recalled, “having been born in Brooklyn Heights, and never having seen more than a few blades of grass, that there was some kind of mystical experience in touching the soil.” Predictably, Dreier inhaled Flight from the City and spent many breakfasts, lunches, and dinners with students excitedly discussing the possibility of starting their own farm. Often joining the conversations was Dreier’s younger brother, John.²⁴

John Dreier had arrived at the start of the school’s first-ever semester with the intention of staying on as a teacher or staff member. He had a keen interest in agriculture, land use, and conservation and because of this, the Board of Fellows, run by faculty and students, had invited John to investigate agricultural possibilities on campus. John enthusiastically surveyed the land, tested the soil, and checked water sources; he spoke with James P. Parker, manager of the Blue Ridge Assembly, about past agricultural efforts and met with experts at the nearby Swannanoa Test Farm to learn more about local practices. By the end of October, John Dreier had submitted his four-page “Notes on Farming” to the Board of Fellows.²⁵

This slender document contained multiple findings that would have a huge influence on the development of agriculture at Black Mountain College. First, Blue Ridge Assembly had a history of farming and the soil was in decent condition for growing. By 1933, two sizable spaces were under cultivation. Second, there were no fewer than eight orchards on the campus grounds, mostly apple, which just two years prior had produced 1,500 bushels of marketable apples (the orchards were then being rented to Bas Allen, the college’s all-purpose handyman). Third, John cited the possibility of raising livestock. Chickens were entirely feasible, his report claimed, and efforts could be made to grow oats and corn to feed a future flock. However due to an inadequate barn and absence of pastures, raising cows would not be easy. Fourth, John provided contacts for local experts in agriculture that he had met and were willing to help, including S. C. Clapp and G. K. Jones, superintendent and “poultry man” of the Swannanoa Test Farm, respectively, and Professor Roy S. Dearstyne, a poultry expert at the State College in Raleigh. Fifth and most importantly, the farmable land, derelict barn, farmhouse, and storage sheds, which were located a half-mile from Lee Hall, appeared to be available. The lease for the farm’s current tenant would be up on 1 January of the next year, and the Assembly was not eager to renew it to the current holder.²⁶

While his report would go on to have a big impact on the development of the farmland at BMC, John Dreier’s time at the college would prove short. After producing “Notes on Farming” in early November, he left for Washington, DC, to take on a job in Subsistence Homesteads, a New Deal program to help out-of-work and seasonally working Americans acquire small tracts of land to farm part-time. John spent the next two decades working for the federal government; though over the years he would provide counsel for his brother about how to sustain the BMC farm.²⁷

[Légende] Ted Dreier, one of the college’s founders, treasurer, main fundraiser, lousy teacher of math and physics, and chief advocate for the farm and physical labor, on the farm, circa 1936–37.

Back on campus, Dave Bailey was getting frustrated. Born into a big house on Boston’s prestigious Beacon Hill, Bailey came from privilege: his father was a wealthy and well-respected doctor, his mother a New York aristocrat. Ruggedly handsome and a bit older than the rest of that first crop of BMC students, as a teenager Bailey had suffered from tuberculosis, which was still a common affliction then. After spending time at a summer camp in the Swiss Alps, his health improved dramatically and he returned to the US in good spirits, though having missed some of his youthful years. In 1929 he enrolled at Harvard but dropped out two years later. He spent the next two years flailing around Cambridge, Massachusetts before meeting Nat French in Harvard Square in summer 1933. Nat told him about a new college being started in North Carolina. Bailey’s interest was piqued, but he considered his parents’ dwindling patience and decided to attend the more acceptable University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, mostly in hopes that the climate would ease the return of his tuberculosis. When Nat learned of his plans, he suggested Bailey hitch a ride to Chapel Hill with Rice, and by the time they had arrived in North Carolina, Rice had changed Bailey’s mind. Recalling his son’s earlier recovery in the Swiss Alps, Bailey’s father reluctantly agreed to allow him to attend BMC, set amid the fortifying air of the Blue Ridge Mountains.²⁸

Urban kid that he was, Bailey read Flight from the City with considerable interest. He especially appreciated Borsodi’s, pragmatic thinking, including his suggestion to select dual-purpose chickens, like Rhode Island Reds, which can be raised for both eggs and meat. He participated in meal-time conversations with fellow students and Dreier, viewing them as opportunities to crack the Rollins social circle, but he became frustrated by all the talking. The talking at meals, the talking throughout classes, the talking during meetings. Bailey was tired of talk. He was ready for action.²⁹

One day over lunch in late October Bailey asked out loud, “Why not raise chickens?” Without hesitation, Norm was in. Within a week Nat had built a chicken coop modeled after Borsodi’s, and a few days later Bailey and Norm hopped into the Buick and drove to the Swannanoa Test Farm where they bought a dozen Rhode Island Red chicks, and, with permission from no one, brought them back to campus. The chicks attracted considerable attention, required significant labor, and represented a big step toward farming among a group of willing students and Dreier. Less clear was whether the rest of the college wanted a farm.³⁰

THE FARM DEBATE

A new college requires ample discussions and decisions. For that, Black Mountain College established three administrative bodies, all comprised of students and faculty, with no full-time administrators, as per Rice’s wishes. The Student Committee addressed student-related issues, the Faculty Committee decided on curricular issues, and the Board of Fellows was responsible for the college’s finances and the hiring and firing of faculty. When larger issues arose, students, faculty, faculty families, and staff—the whole BMC community—were invited to participate in general meetings. Decisions were reached painstakingly through consensus. In early December of that year, six students were granted a general meeting to discuss whether or not the college should start a campus farm. This meeting would set the tone for many meetings to come, it was long, democratic, and full of drama.

It began with fire.³¹ All afternoon a work crew of students and faculty had chopped dead chestnut trees into logs for a huge evening blaze in the fireplace in Lee Hall’s cavernous lobby. Chairs were arranged in a circle. Rice entered, found a comfortable chair, loaded his pipe, and presided.

[Légende] John Andrew Rice, pipe in hand, teaching students, including Nan Chapin (far left) and Dave Bailey (far right), 1934.

[Légende] When larger issues arose at the college, students, faculty, faculty families, and staff were invited to participate in general meetings, like the one pictured here in the lobby of Lee Hall, where decisions were reached painstakingly through consensus.

On one side of the room, “waving a bible in the shape of Ralph Borsodi’s Flight from the City,” sat Norm, Nan, Nat, and a few others. Starting a farm, the students said, would save the college money and help build community. Others, figuring that the college already had enough on its hands, were violently opposed. Dreier, who had a reputation for name-calling, described the farm critics as “hysterical and ever-excited.”

The “farm question” debate lasted for three weeks. The discussion took in all and sundry and included long conversations about crops, soil, plowing, planting, and manure. At this, English instructor Joe Martin began to sweat. Martin had first learned about the new college through his sister, Mitzi, a Rollins rebel. “I was attracted to the ex-Rollins group not because they were teachers but because they were rebels.” But now this rebellion was becoming too rural for Martin’s liking. He felt a “genuine terror of having to get up at 4 a.m. to tend cows.”

Several faculty members pointed out that the college didn’t have the funds to run a farm. “Who will pay for it?” they asked. “We will,” said the would-be student farmers. Nan announced that the students had started an aggressive fundraising campaign for the farm and they had already raised over $200. Norm, usually reticent about speaking up, offered sheets of paper with neat columns of costs and revenues. Early mornings aside, Martin recalled being swayed by Norm’s “impressive array of statistics on all sorts of agricultural matters.”

Three weeks in, Rice raised questions about logistics. He reminded the student farmers that their lease of Blue Ridge Assembly ran from September to May and that they were expected to vacate the main campus over summer. Nan and Norm, by then going steady and getting serious, said they would take care of the land over the summer and live in the farmhouse. Ted and Bobbie Dreier offered to join them. The debate between optimistic would-be farmers and unenthusiastic detractors raged on in Lee Hall.

A few days before Christmas a parallel development forced a decision. While most BMCers were away for winter break, Parker contacted Dreier asking whether the college would like to extend its lease and whether they wanted access to the farm too. He needed an answer within days. Hastily, members of the Board of Fellows who were still on campus granted Dreier permission to renegotiate the lease.

This was a big deal. It extended Black Mountain College’s lease of Blue Ridge for an additional year, through June 1935, offering the fledgling college a sense of continuity, even stability. It also gave the college access to the 25-acre farm, including the fields currently in cultivation, orchards and vineyards, a barn, a farmhouse, and two storage buildings. In return, Black Mountain College agreed to do four things: reroof the barn and storage sheds, rebuild the farmhouse porch, live in the farmhouse over the summer, and give the Blue Ridge Association half the yield of the orchard and vineyard. Remarkably, students and faculty returned from winter break to discover that their college had a working farm, gained without spending any money.³²

While the would-be farmers immediately got to work, a handful of students and faculty felt robbed. They viewed the signing of the new lease as a betrayal of the democratic decision-making process. Why and how, some asked in hushed tones, did Dreier go ahead and fetch us a farm? As student Sydney Carter recalled, this decision would be a major source of conflict throughout the rest of that school year. It was also not the last time Dreier would get accused of less-than-transparent decision-making.³³

Despite the bad blood, by late winter 1934 Black Mountain College had everything it needed to get started on the farm. They had 25 acres of decent land and basic farm buildings. They had a group of enthusiastic students, a faculty mentor and ally in Dreier, and an orchardist in Bas, the handyman. They had a chicken coop constructed by Nat and a flock of chickens tended by Bailey. They even had a little publicity from the Asheville Citizen-Times, an article titled, “College Students to Operate 25-acre Farm.” But most importantly, they had Norm.³⁴

NORM’S FARM SEMINAR

Throughout that first year Norm spent most of his time learning double-entry bookkeeping from Dreier and quickly became the college’s assistant bookkeeper. “As time went,” Norm recalled, “I became less and less of a student and more of a bookkeeper.” The only class that engaged him was Farm Seminar. The independent study’s goal was ambitious: learn from local farmers and produce a detailed, affordable, and immediately executable five-year farm plan for Black Mountain College. Norm plunged in.³⁵

Starting in November and lasting through spring, Norm would jump into his Buick and drive about eight miles from Blue Ridge to Swannanoa to visit the Asheville Farm School. Established in 1894 by the women of the Presbyterian Church, the Asheville Farm School provided education for poor, rural boys. Because most of the boys had no prior schooling, much of the school’s curriculum was vocational. And because few of the boys could afford to pay tuition, they worked for it, primarily on the school’s farm. In 1933, the Asheville Farm School was run by Dr Henry S. Randolph, a charismatic educator trained in progressive education. Supervising the farm was Bernhard Laursen, who grew up in Denmark, studied agriculture in Germany, and taught farming, gymnastics, and folk dancing. Most times Norm would barrage the patient Laursen with questions about farming. Laursen also visited the BMC farm-to-be, offering all kinds of useful suggestions about soil quality, planting, and livestock.³⁶

Norm made regular visits to the Swannanoa Test Farm as well. Located about ten miles from campus in Oteen, it was one of six such test farms in the state, all run through the North Carolina Department of Agriculture, offering free, professional agricultural advice to locals in order to stimulate the local economy and encourage agricultural productivity. Often joined by Bailey (who pumped “poultry man” Jones with questions about chickens) and Nat (who studied the farm’s buildings and structures), Norm would meet with S. C. Clapp to learn more about day-to-day farming operations. Clapp was an active researcher in soil management, and his lessons focused primarily on building healthy soil with techniques we would now call organic gardening, including crop rotation, companion planting, and cover crops. Clapp encouraged Norm to build up the soil with rye, lespedeza—and, Clapp’s favorite for its regenerative qualities, crimson clover.³⁷

To round out his independent study, Norm took the three-mile drive to Black Mountain to meet with John Gary McGraw Sr, a local farmer, co-proprietor with wife Claudia of McGraw’s Coffee House, and father of Black Mountain College’s only local student, John Gary McGraw Jr. McGraw Sr shared insights with Norm about local farming customs, especially leased land for plowing agreements. It was common, McGraw explained, for farmers to lease free parcels of land to other farmers who, in return, would plow the land at the end of harvest. Plowing brought deep nutrients to the surface, buried weeds and last season’s crop remains, and aerated the fields, leading to richer, more fertile soil. The college, Norm learned, would benefit doubly—with improved farmland (through plowing) and increased goodwill (through free leases). With guidance from McGraw Sr and support from Dreier, Norm established such an agreement with existing tenant Earl Meese to reside in the farmhouse through March in return for plowing six acres of land. He struck another agreement with Robert Morehead, a local farmer, which allowed Morehead to farm ten acres of land in return for plowing about 18 acres. In May, with help from Bas, Norm established an agreement with local farmer Winslow Lytle for the lease of two acres of pasture if he agreed to plow it. This collaboration with Lytle, a Black farmer, represented one of the college’s earliest attempts to challenge North Carolina’s racial segregation. With these agreements in place, Norm had arranged for most of the college’s 25 acres of farmland to be plowed for free. He also set in motion what would become some of the college’s most productive and long-lasting community collaborations.³⁸

After five months of wheeling and dealing, Norm produced his six-page Farm Plan, which included detailed planting schedules, requirements, and instructions for ten separate fields, ranging in size from the Triangle Field (nearly an acre) to the West Orchard and Beyond (four acres). For each field Norm noted what was previously planted, currently planted, and what would be planted in summer and fall. That way, heavy feeders, crops that require a lot of the soil’s nutrients, like tomatoes, could be followed by soil-enhancing crops like soybeans, and cow peas. The plan featured aggressive crop rotation and seasonal cover crops, and an array of vegetables including beans, cabbage, corn, onions, potatoes, pumpkins, and spinach. Corn and oats to feed the chickens had not been forgotten. The detailed document noted how much seed, what kind of fertilizer, and how many students were required to plant each field, and included a budget for a year’s worth of planting, $254.25, which was in line with what the students had already raised. Six months after being swept away by Flight from the City, Black Mountain College’s would-be farmers stopped planning the farm and started planting it.³⁹

As spring arrived and farmer Meese left the farmhouse as agreed, about half the campus community spent a few afternoons banded together to make the farmhouse more livable. Each day, two students hauled the college’s huge cast-iron cauldron from Lee Hall to the farm, filled it with water, and placed it over a huge open fire. Aided by hot water, soap, mops, and brushes, crews of four or five would wash walls and floors, first the upstairs, then the downstairs. The walls were plastered and painted and within a hardworking week, the job was done.⁴⁰

Like the cleaning of the farmhouse, the founding of the farm was a collaborative enterprise. Led by Norm, with Nan and Nat close behind, it involved students, faculty, faculty families, staff, and community partners. The behaviors developed on the farm set the collaborative tempo of work and life at Black Mountain College. Everyone was expected to pitch in. Even when it came to pigs.

CHAPTER 3

PIGS AND PLACE

[Légende] Norm Weston recruiting students to come work on the farm, circa 1936–37.

When the Black Mountain College community returned to campus for its second year in September 1934, their meals featured fresh ingredients grown and raised by the summer student-farmers. Eggs at breakfast from the college’s flock of chickens, which now numbered nearly three dozen. Spinach salad for lunch. At dinner, they feasted on farm-fresh beans, corn, and tomatoes in early fall, and beets, cabbage, and carrots in the winter months. In fall, there was a bumper crop of potatoes, the remainders of which were sold at the Asheville Farmer’s Market, fetching the college a whopping $225, a little less than Norm’s estimated costs for a year’s worth of planting.

Even after giving half of their orchard and vineyard yield to the Blue Ridge Assembly, as per the new lease, the folks at Black Mountain College devoured a steady stream of apples, cherries, and grapes as snacks and in desserts. Leftover fruit was sold at the Asheville market for a handsome profit of $50. Through multiple trips to the Asheville Farm School, Norm, Bailey, and others had learned to kill, pluck, and prepare chickens. Each month or so they proudly delivered one or two of those chickens to Jack and Rubye Lipsey, the college’s cooks. Rabbits raised over summer were enjoyed as fuzzy playthings.⁴¹

The farm’s dramatic growth continued throughout the 1934–35 school year. Having submitted a disorganized mess of economic history for his Senior Division proposal, Norm’s talents, it was understood, lay with the farm and bookkeeping. Soon he was promoted to assistant treasurer and kept the college’s books—which was no small feat. Though the college was not a big institution, with an annual budget of $50,000, it had, Norm recalled, “all the duties and problems of a large college.” On top of paying staff wages, paying for food and coal, and occasionally collecting tuition, Norm managed the farm with aplomb, planting the beds, coordinating with the college cooks, and purchasing seed, manure, and tools.⁴²

Another driver behind the farm’s bounty was Bas Allen. Born and raised in the area, a “son-of-the-woods,” Bas—bespectacled, incredibly patient and gentle, in his 50s with boundless energy—made the place run and served as what we would now call a plant manager for the Blue Ridge Assembly. “Bas kept us warm and dry,” Norm said. Bas had an extremely useful skill set: he was an expert plumber, electrician, steamfitter, carpenter, auto mechanic, orchardist, and farmer. According to multiple memoirs written by students and faculty, Bas could fix anything and everything; he was, music professor John Evarts wrote, “our regular handyman genius.” Bas’s teaching skills were equally impressive. He nurtured confidence in students and faculty, fostering an appreciation for their natural surroundings. As cultural historian Martin Duberman wrote in Black Mountain: An Exploration in Community, Bas led camping trips into the mountains, sharing knowledge about flowers and wildlife, and “many grew to value his quiet, intuitive insights.” Mid-1930s BMC student Stephen Forbes recalled Bas as “a very noble and extraordinary spirit. And anybody who was associated with that man for any length of time came to worship him.”⁴³

Beginning the college’s second year, Bas served as unofficial farm teacher. He taught BMCers how to plow, plant, and harvest, and led pruning workshops, passing on his knowledge through an “each one, teach one” process. Once he joined Dreier and Norm on a trip to town in order to show them how to determine a horse’s age by examining its teeth, and helped negotiate a fair price for Mark, the college’s first horse. Bas’s presence gave the college legitimacy and nurtured trust among locals farmers. Significantly, he lit a fire under members of the college community who too often met pressing tasks with debate and discussion. As the college talked about this or that, Bas would regularly stand up and say, “Let’s stop talking and do some work!”⁴⁴

But perhaps the most important factor spurring the farm’s growth was the college’s emphasis on physical work. From the start Rice, Dreier, and others saw manual labor as a gateway to participation in a democratic society, with cooperation and community as vital by-products. As biographer Reynolds notes in Visions and Vanities: John Andrew Rice of Black Mountain College, “If responsible democratic action was the goal of education, then responsible democratic action should also be experienced within the educational setting.” This could be brought about, Rice and his colleagues reasoned, by requiring all members of the Black Mountain College community—especially the students—to perform manual labor that contributed to campus life. Initially the work was modest and individual, for example, cleaning one’s room, clearing one’s dishes. Within months of the college’s launch, however, regular tasks became more demanding and a lot more collective—small teams chopped wood for fires and hauled coal for the furnaces. Early on, weekday afternoons were left unscheduled, so that members of the BMC community could exercise. This meant visiting the gymnasium to play basketball, badminton, and handball, swimming in the pool (a concrete basin filled by a brook), or taking a hike in the surrounding mountains.

By year two, things began to change. While weekday afternoons remained unscheduled, more and more students, faculty, and faculty families began replacing sports and leisure with farm work. The farm fit the bill in regards to building community spirit through joint physical labor. It offered an array of tasks for men and women, young and old, which required consistent attention and was best accomplished as a group. Beyond the pedagogy, the shift of focus from leisure exercise to physical work was financially motivated. Still deep in the Depression, members of the BMC community were eager to improve the college’s bottom line. They were short on revenue, certainly, but they sure had plenty of free labor. As Duberman wrote: “The severity of the struggle for economic survival helped to knit the community together; they needed each other; it was their little band against the world.”⁴⁵

[Légende] Bascombe “Bas” Allen, plumber, electrician, steamfitter, carpenter, auto mechanic, orchardist, farmer, and Black Mountain College’s “handyman genius,” circa 1935.

There was yet another reason that folks flocked to the farm: it was fun. While often strenuous, farm work was social. When Nan barged into the gym to tell students playing basketball that “it’s more fun on the farm!” she meant it. Indeed, Nan recalled finishing her farm work “bursting with a good feeling and good health.” Even Joe Martin, the instructor who a year earlier had been terrified at the thought of tending cows at an unholy hour, found himself regularly signing up to chop wood. “A falling tree was a new sensation to me and a very pleasant one,” he wrote.⁴⁶

While the farm may have been growing during the 1934–35 school year, Norm wanted more from life. On the one hand, his relationship with Nan was flowering and the couple married in January 1935, in Great Neck. Socially, Norm was blossoming too. He had a cadre of good friends, rewarding responsibilities, and, for the first time in his life, a sense of belonging. On the other hand, when it came to the liberal arts education offered at the college, Norm must have felt isolated, alienated from the big thinking and teeming creativity all around him.

[Légende] Although farm work was often grueling, it was also fun, flirty, and a nice break from studying. Here, faculty, including John Andrew Rice (in white shirt, slumped against truck), Ted Dreier (seated at end of truck), and art professor Xanti Schawinsky (to the right of Dreier) and students, including Norm Weston (in black sweater), take a break.

Surrounded by so many new ideas and new possibilities, Norm wanted his hand in something new too. What about pigs? He raised the question over meals and in the fields, and it was agreed that pigs could help the college financially. For one, selling pigs for slaughter would fetch a handsome price. For another, raising their own pork would bring down the school’s food costs. Norm drove to the test farm in Oteen that winter to see Clapp who drew up a list of requirements for raising pigs. “Pigpen” topped the list and for that Norm turned to Nat.⁴⁷

THE BUILDER

Nathaniel “Nat” Stowers French was born in 1912 in Boston, the first of three children of Eleanor Campbell Fisher and John R. P. French, headmaster of Derby Academy, in Hingham, Massachusetts, and later headmaster at the prestigious and progressive Cambridge Academy, in Weston, Mass. Young Nat was busy. Active in Boy Scouts for years, he achieved the highest rank of Eagle Scout. An athlete, Nat played football and hockey; he swam, sailed, and canoed. In high school he was a leader: president of his class, chairman of student council, and captain of the football team. Academically, he struggled but survived. To make money, he chopped and sold wood.⁴⁸

In 1930, Nat followed his father’s footsteps to Harvard, with a desire to become a headmaster himself. Socially, he excelled. He enjoyed intramural football and hockey and served as manager of the swim team. According to Evarts, Nat was “husky, athletic, one of the boys, fond of dancing—and ready for almost any excitement.” His performance in the classroom was another matter. Academically he struggled mightily and by the middle of his first year at Harvard, he was placed on probation. By the end of his first year, he had failed out. With an aggressive intervention from his father, Harvard reinstated Nat after he passed a summer class.⁴⁹

On his first day as a Harvard sophomore, Nat left Cambridge to cross the Charles River and by the time he reached Boston, he had lost all use of his legs. He “laid flat on his back with infantile paralysis,” as he recalled. He returned home to recover from what he now knew was polio—a common affliction among young people at the time—only to be joined soon after by his younger sister and brother, both of whom contracted a less severe strain of the viral disease. In winter Nat attended a rehabilitation clinic in Warm Springs, Georgia, where he benefited greatly from treatments and good weather. After nearly a year, his doctor reported that he had improved tremendously, both mentally and physically, and in summer 1932, Nat withdrew from Harvard, noting that “cold weather is not good for circulation nor ice good for crutches.” He was accepted to Rollins College, where the weather was warm and the education progressive.⁵⁰

[Légende] Nathaniel “Nat” French, first year at Harvard University, 1930. After contracting polio and extensive recovery, Nat spent one year at Rollins College before becoming a member of the inaugural class of students at Black Mountain College.

Within weeks of arriving at Rollins Nat was elected student body president. Then, when he joined the rebels and transferred to BMC, his leadership skills once again drew attention. During the college’s first all-campus meeting Nat was elected as student moderator. Later that year, he was elected the first student representative on the Board of Fellows. On the farm he organized students and professors into small teams to plow, plant, water, and weed. A most perceptive assessment of Nat came from Rice who noted that he “is by no means brilliant, but has what almost amounts to genius in dealing with people.”⁵¹

Crucially, Nat was also a one-person woodshop for Black Mountain College. Along with the chicken coop, he had built a 45 sq ft cold frame and 55 sq ft hothouse to propagate plants. In spring 1934, he reroofed the barn and storage shed and replaced the farmhouse’s patio and back steps. Building a pigpen would be his most ambitious project yet. It was bigger, more complicated, and involved more people. He was up for the job.⁵²

The first question that needed answering was, “where?”⁵³ The farm was large, yet much of it was already in use. There were fields planted with vegetables for eating, feed for livestock, and cover crops for healthy soil. There was a cluster of buildings and a few abandoned outbuildings, and at the center of it all was the

Nat thought it might help to get the opinion of Josef Albers on where to put the pigpen. Over a year earlier, just a month or so after the launch of Black Mountain College, Rice and Dreier were still looking for an art professor who could fulfill Rice’s goal of creating an arts-centric curriculum. They had reached out to Ethel Dreier, Ted’s mother, who led them to Margaret Lewisohn, whose husband Samuel was on the Board of Trustees of the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), who connected them to Philip Johnson, MoMA’s curator of architecture and the US representative of the Circle of Friends of the Bauhaus. Back in Berlin, in April 1933, just as Rice was fired from Rollins, the Bauhaus school was receiving visits and threats from the Nazis. By summer, the iconic design academy had closed its doors before Hitler could shut it down. Johnson quickly suggested Albers, a former Bauhaus instructor. Through a series of letters, Albers and wife Anni, an expert weaver and former weaving professor at the Bauhaus, agreed to teach at Black Mountain College. With financial support from Edward M. M. Warburg, a trustee at MoMA, and Abby Aldrich Rockefeller, the Albers’ travel costs and first year’s salary were covered. They arrived at the college in late November 1933 and are remembered as two of the school’s first big-name instructors, setting the tone for years of faculty notoriety. Nat had taken Albers’ Werklehre class, described in the 1935–36 college catalog as “the development of the feeling for material and space,” so he knew that Albers had a way with seeing.⁵⁴

What Nat hadn’t known was that Albers grew up in a small village in Westphalia that was surrounded by farmland. He was right at home in a conversation about livestock. Albers excitedly toured the farm and, with extremely limited English and a boundless supply of gestures, articulated a plan for the whole place: pasture here, chickens over there, beans next to the barn. Nat, Norm, and Nan were awed. Before leaving, Albers looked up and down and all around, assessing the placement of the farmhouse, where people lived, and the movement of the wind. He gathered his thoughts, turned to Nat, and sagely proclaimed: “And do not let the house be in the wind-shadow of the pigpen!”⁵⁵

Once they decided to locate the pigpen in the southeast corner of the farm, far away from the farmhouse, the second question Nat needed to answer was, “where would they get the money?” The college was broke and neither it nor the farm could afford the materials required to make a proper pigpen. Nat’s thoughts turned to a significant grove of dead chestnut trees alongside the mountains behind campus. According to Rice, a year or two before the college opened, a blight had killed nearly every tree. Nat thought, couldn’t the chestnut trees, particularly able to withstand wind and weather, be chopped and split into rails?⁵⁶

These same chestnut trees had been chopped into logs for evening fires over the past year. Although no one would mistake Black Mountain College for a community of lumberjacks, maybe, just maybe, there was enough collective will and skill on campus to chop and split the wood required to make 100 rails for a pigpen? So Nat did what he did best: he organized people. For weeks he led afternoon work crews to chop down the trees and split them to construct a split-rail fence. Trees were cut down with an axe and cross-cut saw; the logs were then split using wedges and sledgehammers.⁵⁷

While Nat built the fencing out of chestnut, he fashioned the shelter, or hoghouse, out of salvaged wood from tumbledown structures from a previous era of Blue Ridge. He organized work crews to break down these structures and salvage the lumber. Oftentimes a mountaineer named Owensby stopped by for a chat. Owensby lived near the top of the mountain behind campus where he grew beans which he sold at the market in Black Mountain. One day he witnessed a work crew salvaging lumber. “Whenever we pulled out a rusty nail from the old lumber,” Dreier recalled, Owensby would pick it up. “He saved all the rusty nails to take home to his building while we extravagant Yankees had bought new nails.” As much as the college prided itself on being self-sufficient, seasoned mountain inhabitants like Owensby threw their presumptions into stark relief.⁵⁸

The pigpen was finished on 1 May 1935 for a total cost of $6. Two weeks later, Black Mountain College welcomed its pigpen’s first three inhabitants, Nancie and Eurie, both registered Berkshire thoroughbreds, and Mary, an unregistered thoroughbred, all purchased from the Asheville Farm School. Six months later the college began to breed pigs with the help of B. C. Brown, a pig farmer in Oteen. By April 1936 the college had 25 pigs. By late summer 1937, a little more than two years after the pig project had started, the college had three dozen.⁵⁹

[Légende] From the beginning, students started the farm to save money and build community among each other, with faculty, and with animals.

Raising pigs at Black Mountain College was a success on many levels. Academically, the pigs validated the progressive educational theory of the college; it rose from student interest and grew from student initiative. It gave students an opportunity to go deep into a topic that interested them, required them to research and present, outreach and organize, plan and produce. It brought together nearly the whole community in the effort to maintain the pigs.

The pigs also brought money to the economically unstable college. A fattened hog could fetch between $50 to $60, and because traditional costs like labor (done by students) and feed (grown on the farm) were low, much of this was profit. Further, when cook Jack Lipsey received pork from the farm, rather than purchasing it commercially, the college’s food costs went way down. (The relationship between the kitchen and farm would change over time: first, the kitchen received meat for free, then paid a modest price for it.) Another unforeseen revenue stream were piglets, sold for a handsome profit to local farmers and organizations.⁶⁰

PIGS AND PROGRAMS

That first pig project gave rise to the Build Program and the Work Program, vital components of Black Mountain College. The Build Program was an applied architecture curriculum for which the Black Mountain community worked together to design and make buildings and furniture. Because much of this building took place during the years-long preparation and move from the rented Blue Ridge campus to the owned Lake Eden campus (more on that soon), the origin of the Build Program has been previously pinpointed to either 1937, when the college purchased Lake Eden, or the early 1940s, when the college, under the leadership of architecture professor A. Lawrence “Larry” Kocher, began to design and construct buildings at Lake Eden. But Black Mountain College’s Build Program actually originated in spring 1935, the semester Nat led the building of the pigpen. Though he didn’t intend it, the way he went about designing, sourcing, and building the pigpen established the components that would define the college’s Build Program. These were: 1) Designed in house; 2) Built mostly by inexperienced student labor, with help from faculty and staff; 3) Built to serve the functional needs of the college; 4) Built from campus resources when possible; and 5) Built as inexpensively as possible. Lasting for the next decade and a half, participants of the BMC Build Program applied these five components to design and build barns, cottages, corn silos, music cubicles, and the epic two-story Studies Building. All made possible by Nat’s pigpen.⁶¹

Building the pen and raising pigs also brought shape to what would become the college’s Work Program. At its core, the Work Program was a collectively held, though never codified, assumption that all members of the community—especially students—were expected to perform regular and significant labor toward the upkeep of campus. This usually translated to working two to three hours a day, four to five days a week on projects that benefited BMC, including fixing plumbing (with Bas), maintaining roads (supervised by Josef Albers), helping in the kitchen (with Jack and Rubye Lipsey), and working on the farm. Knowledge, it was hoped, was acquired through the experience of doing and, along the way, a sense of cooperation was created. While early farm work was a precursor to the Work Program, building pigpens and raising pigs and the constant labor they demanded, forced the program into existence.

In many important ways the pig project solidified collaborations with multiple community partners as well. These collaborations challenge some of the long-held beliefs about Black Mountain College. One enduring myth is that BMC functioned in isolation as an exclusive art school that didn’t interact with its neighbors, which Duberman describes as the “‘Bible Belt’ of the nation.”⁶² There is some truth to this. The college was in the South, in conservative Buncombe County. This was Bible Belt country. It was here in North Carolina that Black Mountain resident Robert “Buncombe Bob” Reynolds held the senatorial seat from 1932 to 1945 after getting elected on an anti-immigration platform. It was here, too, that locals expressed hate and horror at hearing stories of the college’s Black cooks living on campus, attending classes, and participating in meetings. It was also here that residents of Black Mountain and Asheville, having witnessed Black Mountain College students walking around downtown in shorts and sandals, concluded that the college was a nudist colony at best, a free-love cult at worst. And it was here that the locals labeled the innovative college as communist—a charged word then in the US as it is today. Certainly there were plenty of reasons for members of Black Mountain College to be wary of engaging with their neighbors, and for their neighbors to keep their distance from this seemingly unconventional group.⁶³

But it’s inaccurate to claim that Black Mountain College did not interact with surrounding communities. Established in fall 1934, the Cottage School was a small elementary school operated by Black Mountain College in conjunction with the local public school system. Staffed by BMC students, it was established to teach the growing number of faculty children, to give classroom experiences to BMC students interested in education, and to serve as a way to connect with the wider community by educating local students. The college also had a weekly radio show in Asheville during the early 1940s and hosted on-campus public events, including a talk by Thornton Wilder (which attracted over 250 guests) and a performance by African American singer and composer Roland Hayes in 1945 that attracted a racially desegregated crowd of over 300 guests. There were evening dances with soldiers recovering at the nearby VA hospital and talks given by Rice and Wunsch in Asheville and Chapel Hill.⁶⁴

But nowhere was this community-campus, town-gown collaboration clearer than on the farm. Norm and Nat’s regular visits to the Asheville Farm School, to familiarize themselves with everything from the daily operations of raising pigs to butchery, often started as early as 4 a.m. Similarly, the two took regular trips to the Mountain Test Farm, where Clapp taught the students how to transform forests into pasture, how to grow enough oats, rye, and corn to feed a growing drift of pigs, and all the while, how to rotate crops and use cover crops to build healthy soil. Later on when the college started to breed pigs, Clapp provided expert advice on feeding and caring for piglets. With these local figures on their side, the college community felt less like a pariah. At the end of his visits to the BMC farm, Clapp would often leave behind names and contacts, “Tell ’em I sent you,” he’d say to Norm and others.⁶⁵

Finally, it is difficult to imagine the farm at Black Mountain College without B. C. Brown and his boars. Twice yearly Nancie, Eurie, and Mary were driven to Oteen to let them be with the boars for a few days. Brown would create a comfortable environment for the visiting hogs, feed them, and, in due course, would write the college, “Your Hog is ready.” By all accounts he was patient and fair, including the time when Nancie arrived and mated “on the spot” and Brown waived the feeding charge. With help from Brown, Black Mountain College increased their hog count quickly.

COMMUNITY FAIR

In mid-September 1937, more than two years after the pig project started, Norm, Nan, and others piled into Norm’s Buick and headed for the Black Mountain Community Fair. Held at a local school, the fair brought together the finest farming and homesteading practices in the township. The BMCers had entered a few of the contests for local produce and livestock, and I imagine there was an air of excitement and pride as they strode into the fairgrounds, abuzz with activity and swarmed with locals of all ages. Surely the BMC group attended the morning address by William Kerr Scott, then North Carolina’s commissioner of agriculture, who spoke about rural electrification and his work with the Resettlement Administration. After the talk, they would have visited the livestock and gardening exhibits, and made their way to the Dairy Services display—some of the students were set on raising cows. They visited the Canned Products exhibit and marveled at the Best Jar of Jam, Best Jar of String Beans, and Best Single Jar of Okra—unaware that within a few years canning would be essential to the college’s survival during World War II. Nan and Norm, expecting, may have made their way to the Better Baby Contest to witness contestants for Best Developed Baby under one year old, Prettiest Baby under two years, and Most Sensibly Dressed Baby under two years.⁶⁶

They would have been excited to see Clapp and his wife, one of four judges for Sewing by Girls Under Eighteen Years of Age. I’m sure it was fun for them to see Claudia Crawford McGraw, mother of student John Gary McGraw, and one of the judges in Home Economics Department—Pantry Supplies. They would have been happy as well to see Bernhard Laursen and B. C. Brown. And I imagine Nan cheering on some of her former Cottage School students, now fiercely competing in the 75-yard sack race for boys and girls or the 75-yard egg race.

At fair’s end, the BMC group jumped back into the Buick and returned to campus. They must have felt reflective, even wistful, looking back on what they’d achieved on the farm in only four years. What had begun with eggs, corn, and apples, had grown into a much bigger operation. They also must have felt proud, for they brought back two second-place ribbons: one for Best Work Horse (Mark) and another for Best Stalk Corn. But what would have made them proudest of all was the ribbon they kept passing around for all passengers to hold: the first place blue ribbon for Best Hog.⁶⁷

CHAPTER 4

MOVING TO LAKE EDEN

[Légende] A portion of Lake Eden’s 667 acres, featuring a human-made lake, foreground, the dining hall and kitchen, left, and student lodges, background.

For the most part, members of Black Mountain College adored the Blue Ridge campus. They loved the way everyone came together in Lee Hall, in pairs and small groups, as classes and committees, and as a campus community. They enjoyed the natural surroundings, the pathways between the buildings, the hikes up and down the surrounding mountains, and the view of the Blue Ridge range. They even learned to tolerate packing up their belongings each summer and scattering away. The problem was their landlord.

Dr Willis Duke Weatherford, born and raised in Black Mountain, was, among many other things, president of the Blue Ridge Assembly and the Blue Ridge campus’s landlord. Although the relationship between Black Mountain College and Weatherford began cordially, it soon soured. When the college inquired about purchasing Blue Ridge, Weatherford was dismissive. Most frustrating was that each year, the college tried to negotiate a long-term lease and each year Weatherford refused. Some years, students and faculty would leave the college at the end of spring term uncertain whether Blue Ridge would be theirs in the fall. In June 1936, the college managed to extract a five-year lease, the longest in its three-year history. But there was a catch. The agreement stipulated that Weatherford could lease or sell Blue Ridge to anyone willing to pay a higher sum. Tired of the uncertainty and distrustful of Weatherford, the college looked to greener pastures and purchased Lake Eden in May 1937.⁶⁸

Located five miles from Blue Ridge, Lake Eden was developed in 1923 by E. W. Grove as a camp for girls. When that venture didn’t work out, it was run as a summer resort. Lake Eden’s 667 acres were graced with a human-made lake, woods, pastures, and farmable land. Scattered about the property were 14 buildings, including two large lodges suitable for student dormitories, a lakeside dining room, a handful of small, two-story cottages suitable for faculty lodgings, a farmhouse, and a bathhouse. To meet the steep purchase price of $35,000 (a $2,000 down payment and $7,000 a year for five years), the college sent a panicked appeal to BMC alumni and supporters. A gift of $12,500 from former student Stephen Forbes and nearly $1,000 from Dreier’s parents allowed the college to purchase Lake Eden in May 1937.⁶⁹

Besides gaining more space, the college community also gained more time—namely, summers. While they’d grown accustomed to vacating Blue Ridge each May, Lake Eden was theirs and they thought they could stay all year. This led, beginning in summer 1944 and lasting through summer 1953, to a series of Summer Institutes, usually organized around art and music. They generally ran for one or two months, were organized around a set of classes, and gave attendees—BMC and visiting students and faculty—time and space to dig deeply into a topic without having to participate in the college’s everyday tasks and tensions. These seasonal sessions attracted some of the famous artists and thinkers that are now synonymous with the college, including John Cage, Merce Cunningham, Buckminster Fuller, and Jacob Lawrence. They were also responsible for some extraordinary artistic developments, including Cage’s “Theater Piece No. 1,” widely recognized as the first “happening”; the formation of the pioneering Merce Cunningham Dance Company; and Fuller’s first attempt (unsuccessful) to erect a large-scale geodesic dome. Less ambitious, but no less important, the college was also able to offer regular classes during summer, usually referred to as summer sessions, allowing existing students to continue their studies while giving new and prospective students an opportunity to test out the innovative college.

Full of potential, Lake Eden had two major problems. First, it had no heating and therefore was uninhabitable in winter (throwing into question the dream of a year-round residency). Second, it had neither classrooms nor studios for students to paint, write, or study. Lake Eden needed work, work required money, and the college had little if any of that. It would take more than four years before the college finally moved to Lake Eden, during which time they stayed at Blue Ridge thanks to that final, longer lease.

While the move took some time, the college still had a presence at the new location. Almost immediately after the purchase was finalized, Dreier, Norm, and Bas began making regular trips to assess current and potential farming operations at Lake Eden. Enlisting once again the good advice of Laursen and Clapp, they were able to assess the soil (poor) and estimate how much labor was required (a lot) to bring the land to working condition. “There was hardly anything here that could be called a farm at all,” wrote woodworking professor Molly Gregory years later. Nevertheless, the college was optimistic and the solution was clear. A few times a week, a work crew piled into the college truck and drove five miles to Lake Eden to clear fields and plant row after row of cover crops to improve the soil. During the 1937–38 school year they managed to plant over ten acres of crimson clover. The next year, they planted nearly 15 acres of crimson clover, 12 acres of soybeans, and seven acres of rye. They also hauled Nat’s pigpen and pigs to the new campus and built a small, modest barn.⁷⁰

The most dramatic farm-related development, however, was the addition of the college’s first farmer, Roscoe “Ross” Penley. A local Black Mountain inhabitant, Penley, a “ruddy, boxy man exuding strength,” joined Black Mountain College in January 1938. Although he lacked managerial skills, Penley had more than enough farming skills (and family help) to maintain the Blue Ridge farm while simultaneously preparing the burgeoning farm at Lake Eden. More importantly, Penley was both good-natured and infinitely patient. “We’d ask him all sorts of questions,” student Ike Nakata recalled. “What kind of tree is that? What’s the name of that wild flower? Is that wild plant good to eat? How do you sharpen a hoe? How do you use a scythe? Did he like the Navy? Did he ever get drunk? Did he think he’d ever amount to anything?”⁷¹

Perhaps best of all for the financially struggling college was the agreement leasing Blue Ridge farm, farmhouse, and farm tools to the Penley family: Ross, wife Stella, and their three sons Max, Jack, and Atlas. In return Ross promised the college one-third of all harvests, including grapes and apples. Further, he agreed to take care of the hogs and horse, with the college supplying feed. At Lake Eden Penley helped clear fields, plant cover crops, and would, in the future, help supervise the college’s growing dairy operations.⁷²

LAKE EDEN INN

Farming was not to be Lake Eden’s only use before BMC moved in fully. At Norm’s behest the college continued to operate the summer resort, called Lake Eden Inn. Guests could rent rooms in one of two large lodges or, for a higher price, rent one of the smaller cottages. Guests could enjoy delicious meals, swim and boat in Lake Eden, and have access to tennis courts, hiking trails, and, for a fee, taxi rides to nearby mountain destinations such as Horseshoe Rock and Blowing Rock. A man with a plan, Norm proposed that he run the resort with support from Wilbur and Alice Miner Devendorf, its previous caretakers. Running the Lake Eden site as a summer travel and leisure business would bring desperately needed revenue to the college and offer seasonal jobs and housing to BMC students and faculty.⁷³

Norm settled easily into running Lake Eden Inn. First, he hired a French chef, John Doxaras, who spent winter seasons cooking at Ridgewood Hotel in Daytona Beach, Florida. Doxaras was comfortable cooking for up to 150 people and reportedly made a mean roasted leg of lamb. His rotating menu featured stylish yet inexpensive meals that integrated fresh produce from the Blue Ridge farm. The paper menus themselves were simple, elegant, and printed in Bodoni typeface, the preferred font of Josef Albers, who also spent his summer at the resort. Next, Norm hired seven BMC students to work in the kitchen and dining room.⁷⁴

In late spring as Norm raced around making final adjustments for Lake Eden Inn, something momentous occurred: the arrival of William Morse Cole and his wife Fanny. To reassure its initial supporters, the college had secured the services of Cole, a professor of accounting at the Harvard Business School, to prepare the college’s annual budget. In return, the Coles would take ten days of vacation on campus and Cole would give a lecture or two on Shakespeare. Cole and Norm got along famously. After auditing books together for four years, the elder of the two exclaimed, “Norm, you are wasting your time here. You ought to go to the Harvard Business School.” In response, Norm pointed out that he did not have a college degree and, come to think of it, wasn’t even close to getting one from Black Mountain College. Cole dismissed the lack of degree as a technicality.⁷⁵

As well as employment and summer housing, the inn brought in $875 (equivalent to about $18,000 in 2024) for the college that year and also managed some community outreach. Throughout the summer 65 boys and girls and one Reverend J. H. Gruver from the Mountain Orphanage swam in Lake Eden in exchange for paying 25 cents per hour to a BMC student-lifeguard, a tradition that would continue for the next five summers. This is all to say that the venture was a huge success, laying the groundwork for prosperous Lake Eden Inn summers to come. Despite this achievement, after more than five years at Black Mountain College, Norm was ready for something new—and so was Nan. Although the college never obtained accreditation (primarily due to not meeting state and national standards on faculty salaries, endowment, number of books in the library, and ample science equipment), it did, on rare occasion, grant degrees to students, which were sometimes recognized by prestigious schools like Harvard and Yale. In 1936, Nan graduated with a degree in history and worked for two years with Wunsch in the drama department. The August 1938 birth of her and Norm’s son Michael led the couple to reassess their priorities. “We cannot stay in this utopian situation forever,” Nan recalled saying to Norm. “We’d better get a little more practical.”⁷⁶

In some ways, it was the season for exits. Although John Andrew Rice had a few good years at Black Mountain College, his old ways soon re-appeared. For his frequent tangles with faculty and students and his consistent embroilment in this or that college controversy, Rice, by fall 1937, had strained relationships with Albers, Dreier, and Wunsch and frequent—and public—rows with wife, Nell. He did not share the students’ love for farming nor Dreier’s spiritual bliss over labor, and he preferred old, staid Lee Hall over the modernist models he saw for Lake Eden. Worse, Rice fancied, flirted with, had relations with, and had sex with a female BMC student. Rumors led to suspicions led to confusion led to anger—on and off campus. Media outlets, including the Christian Science Monitor, which had run more than a half dozen stories about the innovative college, blocked future coverage because of the allegations against Rice, and Colonel Dwight, BMC’s original funder, refused future gifts. In February 1938, the college, led by Dreier, encouraged Rice to take a leave of absence, which he did, at a small beach cottage in Folly Beach, South Carolina, near Charleston, where he sulked, no doubt, but also began to write his autobiography.

[Légende] Black Mountain College students and Lake Eden Inn waitresses, including Hyalie Yamins, far left, summer 1938.

By late spring, Rice was taking visitors and Nan and Norm climbed into the trusty Buick and drove to visit him. Although they had a lovely dinner, things got awkward near the end of the evening when Rice pulled Nan aside and asked to borrow five bucks to buy a good bottle of whiskey. Over the next two years, Rice returned to Black Mountain College, took another leave, and eventually resigned in February 1940. His book, I Came Out of the Eighteenth Century, was published in 1942 and won the prestigious and well-paying 125th Anniversary Harper Book Prize, but was eventually pulled from the shelves due to threats of legal action from none other than Hamilton Holt.⁷⁷

Back on campus, after running Lake Eden Inn and with his conversation with Professor Morse fresh on his mind, Norm came to a decision. In December 1938, the Weston family packed themselves and their belongings into the faithful Buick and drove to Cambridge, Massachusetts, launching Norm on his path to a graduate degree at Harvard Business School. Black Mountain College said goodbye to two of its founding members, significant leaders who had helped establish not only the physical campus with its buildings and plowed land, but the hopeful spirit of BMC itself. Looking back nearly 60 years later, Norm wrote of his time at the school, “I suspect that there is no one on whom BMC had a greater impact.”⁷⁸

LEAVING BLUE RIDGE

Not long after the Westons departed, the unscrupulous landlord Weatherford returned claiming he had landed a ten-year contract with an unnamed local entity. If true, this would mean Black Mountain College would have to vacate Blue Ridge by September 1940. Even though Weatherford was cagey, the college panicked. Soon after, Weatherford withdrew the ultimatum. However any sense of relief was short-lived. In January 1939 the landlord returned to say he had received another offer for a ten-year contract, this one at terms that far exceeded Black Mountain College’s measly budget. Dreier and others believed Weatherford was bluffing. But what if he wasn’t?⁷⁹

Fearing they might be without a home, the college commissioned Walter Gropius, founder of the Bauhaus, chair of architecture at Harvard’s Graduate School of Design, and friend of the college, along with Marcel Breuer, another Bauhaus refugee, to draw up plans for a building complex that could house the college’s main activities and provide living quarters at Lake Eden. The Gropius-Breuer plan was stunning, modern, and very expensive. Dreier estimated the cost at $500,000 (about $9 million today). To amass that large sum, an ambitious fundraising campaign was launched in early January 1940 at New York’s Museum of Modern Art. A model of the Gropius-Breuer plan was showcased, and Albers and Gropius gave talks about the college. A second fundraising campaign followed, but few funds were raised. Monied folk, it seemed, were reluctant to invest in a college that was still unaccredited and lacked a board of trustees. Further, within months, President Franklin D. Roosevelt would request from Congress over $1 billion in war funds. The US economy was rapidly becoming a war economy.⁸⁰

As if on cue Weatherford returned, this time with one last notification. He announced his aim to open a girls’ school at Lee Hall in fall 1941. He returned in April to reiterate his intention and remind the college that they must vacate Blue Ridge by June 1941. This time it was real.⁸¹

In May 1940, Dreier called for a community meeting to discuss the campus crisis. He began with three announcements. First, their fundraising efforts for the Gropius-Breuer plan had failed; they had raised only $14,500 or so of the $75,000 necessary to break ground on the first phase of the Lake Eden project. Second, now that the country was entering the war, securing suitable building materials would be impossible. And third, as a consequence of these points, the Board of Fellows had decided to abandon the Gropius-Breuer plan. Dreier then offered two options beyond the 1940–41 school year: either find another campus to rent or build the Lake Eden campus with their own hands. Following much discussion the Black Mountain College community resolved to build their own campus at Lake Eden. The dream of self-sufficiency, it seems, was becoming a very real necessity.⁸²

In July 1940, BMC commissioned architect A. Lawrence “Larry” Kocher to design a modest and affordable cluster of buildings that could be constructed primarily by students and faculty working with a contractor. Kocher’s plan was sensitive to an unskilled approach to construction, including simple wood frames. Soon after, Kocher joined the college, initially to oversee construction of the Studies Building and eventually as professor of architecture.⁸³

Born in 1885 in San Jose, California, Kocher studied history at Stanford and did graduate work in architecture at MIT. A generalist interested in historic preservation and modern architecture, he proudly claimed three job titles: professor, managing editor, and architect. Starting in 1912, Kocher taught architecture at Penn State (1912–26), University of Virginia (1926–28), the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon, 1938–40), Black Mountain College (1940–43), and the College of William and Mary (1944–59). Kocher’s second job was managing editor of Architectural Record, from 1927–38. During his tenure at this established publication, he redirected the magazine’s focus to showcase projects and practices of pioneering modern architects including Le Corbusier, Gropius, Richard Neutra, and Frank Lloyd Wright. As an architect, Kocher showed a particular interest in low-cost housing, prefabrication, and new materials. He designed the Aluminaire House in New York City (1931, with Albert Frey), the first all-metal house in the US, made in less than two weeks with freely donated materials, and the Plywood House (1939), built for The Town of Tomorrow for the New York World’s Fair. John Burchard, a contributor to Architectural Record, member of Black Mountain College’s Advisory Council, and Kocher’s friend, once opined that while Kocher was “not a genius,” he was “imaginative” and “very serious, he was intelligent, he supported what I would call the right causes.”⁸⁴

[Légende] Cynthia Carr, Black Mountain College student and Lake Eden Inn waitress, summer 1940.

From its start, Black Mountain College intrigued Kocher. He followed the college closely, was fascinated by the pedagogical ideas of Albers, and featured BMC in the September 1936 issue of Architectural Record. Starting in 1940 and over the course of the next three years, Kocher would design and help construct several core buildings at Lake Eden, including the enormous (200 ft long by 28 ft wide), three-story modernist Studies Building (which provided two faculty apartments, 60 student “studies,” or studios, a dozen faculty studies, and a large room used for art classes, weaving workshops, and faculty meetings); the two-story, wood-framed Service Building (a residence for cooks Jack and Rubye Lipsey and other Black staff members); the cozy and chic two-bedroom Jalowetz Cottage, often called the Jalo House (for music professor Heinrich Jalowetz and his wife Johanna, who taught voice and bookbinding); and the iconic barn. Kocher was also involved in “winterizing”, i.e. adding insulation, fireplaces, and chimneys, to the half dozen original two-story, two-bedroom faculty cottages.⁸⁵

[Légende] Roscoe “Ross” Penley, Black Mountain College’s first full-time farmer.

Kocher was Black Mountain College’s only long-term (three-plus years) faculty member in architecture and did more than anyone to foster a campus culture of architectural design and construction. With the Alberses on sabbatical, the art curriculum for the 1940–41 school year shifted from courses in visual arts to construction and architecture, taught by Kocher. Students were instructed using the design and build method, which meant engaging them in the architectural process from conception to construction, with an emphasis on experimentation over replication of historical examples. “Larry was a thoughtful, considerate man who was constantly striving for better architecture,” recalled student Don Page.⁸⁶

CHAPTER 5

BARN RAISING

[Légende] Lawrence “Larry” Kocher, professor, journal editor, and architect, Black Mountain College, April 1942.

The early 1940s were marked by frantic construction at Black Mountain College. The Studies Building and other structures were underway by summer 1941, led by Kocher and local contractor Charlie Godfrey. When the college wasn’t building it was moving furniture, pianos, looms, the college’s press, and an entire heating system—radiators and all—from Blue Ridge to Lake Eden.

In late spring 1941, Kocher turned his attention to the farm. Designing farm-related buildings was not new to him. In 1934, he and Albert Frey had published two designs in Architectural Record—one for a low-cost farmhouse made entirely out of prefabricated materials that required no cutting on the job, the other for a neighborhood of subsistence farmsteads. Following his own advice and building upon a precedent set by Nat’s pigpens, Kocher designed the barn to be built primarily of campus lumber, chestnut poplars, once again. The barn’s overseer was Bob Bliss.⁸⁷ 8”

Robert “Bob” Bliss grew up in Seattle. While his college-bound friends enrolled at the University of Washington, young Bliss wanted something different. He found himself moved by an article Louis Adamic had written about a unique school, Black Mountain College. Originally published in Harper’s Magazine, excerpted in Reader’s Digest, and extended in his book My America, Adamic’s article was an enthusiastic, albeit idealized distillation of the college, its faculty leaders (Rice, Albers, Wunsch), and its involved-in-everything students. The article had reached such a wide audience that for years it served as the college’s single most effective recruitment tool. Bliss was fascinated by the college, especially its communal living and real-world application of knowledge. He also liked the price tag; his family didn’t have much money and the college had a sliding scale for tuition. Despite high hopes, upon arrival he felt intimidated. “I remember feeling very unsophisticated in relation to students that had been to private schools in the east and were very articulate. In fact, I hardly talked for the first year.” Instead he quietly took Dreier’s math classes, which only decreased his interest in the subject.⁸⁸

Things changed his second year when Bliss took Albers’ acclaimed Werklehre class. Another class that had an impact was furniture making with Edward “Ed” DuPuy, a Black Mountain-based photographer, carpenter, and furniture maker. An experienced carpenter himself, Bliss especially looked forward to the days when class took place in DuPuy’s Village Workshop in Black Mountain where students were given access to specialized tools.⁸⁹

Most exciting for Bliss, however, were Kocher’s classes in architecture and building. Partly out of feelings of intellectual inadequacy and also out of a growing interest in architecture, Bliss poured his efforts into several of the early Lake Eden construction projects, and his favorite was the barn. While the other buildings were riddled with complexities, the barn, Bliss recalled, was a satisfyingly simple structure to make.

[Légende] Architecture student and overseer of the barn building Robert “Bob” Bliss working on a model for low-cost housing, 1942.

[Légende] Charles “Charlie” Godfrey, local contractor, builder, and friend of Black Mountain College.

While Kocher designed the barn and Bliss oversaw it, Charlie Godfrey and his crew did much of the building. Charles “Charlie” Godfrey was an experienced Black Mountain-based contractor and builder, who in 1940 had completed a hotel in nearby Montreat. The hotel was unique because of its massive size and the fact that it was built without an architect. Members of Black Mountain College were of two main opinions. First, the hotel, built with conflicting materials and assembled in conflicting styles, was hideous; second, considering its large size, it sure seemed sturdy. Charlie was an appealing option to take on the Lake Eden campus build for several reasons. He had a diverse skill set that included stone and concrete masonry, welding, carpentry, plumbing, and electrical wiring. He had an excellent reputation as a manager of his construction crew, which at the time was comprised of three men, Parley, Hurley, and Pearson Mundy. And, importantly, he expressed excitement at the idea of working with the novice student crew. Throughout March and April 1942, the professional team of four worked seven straight weeks to complete the barn with the help of BMC students. Mundy, an experienced plumber, was a favorite on campus. Student Ike Nakata recalled that he “delighted in telling us what kind of sex lives some of the preachers practiced in private, judging from their stopped plumbing.”⁹⁰

THE WORK PROGRAM

The building of the barn concretized the rhythm of manual labor at BMC. While there had been no formal program to facilitate daily chores at Blue Ridge—chopping wood, hauling coal, and tending to pigs were all voluntary—this would change at Lake Eden. Overnight, the newly focused Work Program altered the very time and space of everyday life at Black Mountain College. Morning classes were scheduled earlier and afternoon/evening classes scheduled later, freeing up five-to-six hour chunks of the day for work. Each weekday and most Saturdays, the BMC community would gather on the porch of Lee Hall, pile into the back of the college truck, and travel to Lake Eden for an afternoon of physical labor. Campus, in other words, became distributed: Students and faculty would discuss ideas and make art at Blue Ridge, learn skills and build structures at Lake Eden, and hopefully fit in some social time while squished together in the back of the truck traveling back and forth between sites.⁹¹

Kocher was in charge of designing the buildings and supervising their erection. Charlie was tasked with construction and supervising students. Bas worked tirelessly wiring and winterizing existing and new buildings. Dreier served as program manager and chaired and coordinated all phases of the Work Program and building project. As always, they relied on key advice from their local network, including Laursen of the Asheville Farm School and Clapp from the Swannanoa Test Farm.⁹²

The secret sauce that would drive the whole operation, Dreier and others hoped, was Dr Richard Gothe. Gothe visited Blue Ridge Assembly for a non-BMC-related matter in July 1940. As he was about to leave, he ended up meeting Dreier and sharing his life story. He told Dreier that he had been born in Berlin, Germany, where he trained to become a master mechanic and toolmaker. After nearly a decade of developing his craft, including in Brazil and the US, Gothe returned home. Although he had neither a high school diploma nor an undergraduate degree, he was admitted to the University of Berlin, and later the University of Kiel, where he earned a PhD in economics. When the Nazis rose to power Gothe fled to the US to study and direct voluntary work camps through the Civilian Conservation Corps, a part of Roosevelt’s New Deal. With this experience he was qualified to run the Work Program, and he could also teach economics. Conveniently, because Gothe was on a fellowship with the Rockefeller Foundation, BMC would not have to pay his salary during the first year. Dreier, one can imagine, was beside himself. The wheeling and dealing treasurer pushed Gothe to remain on campus, organized a few campus talks for him, and suggested the Board of Fellows hire him immediately. They did.⁹³ It wouldn’t take too much time to realize that despite looking great on paper, Gothe was not a popular addition to the faculty or the farm.

For the time, with Gothe at the helm, the BMC community worked incessantly throughout the 1940–41 school year, practically willing the Work Program into full action. As Bliss recalled: “We gathered river boulders and mountain stone, cut trees for pilings, dug foundation trenches, poured concrete, and set masonry. Carpentry, plumbing, wiring, laying oak flooring, and painting completed the work. Everyone took part and all could take pride in what a small group could accomplish given the desire and obvious need.” The work log entry for Thanksgiving 1940 offers a picture.

    Thanksgiving Day: we worked as usual on this beautiful sunny but windy day. It was pretty cold where people were exposed to the wind. Ken Kurtz worked with a group cutting hard wood poles for Mr. Godfrey. A group of five girls with Cynthia Carr as straw boss graded the hillside down to the wall and put the dirt into the cavity between the walls and the hill. Four boys with Rudy Haase worked on the farm ditch. Morris Simon and Tommy Brooks built a bridge across the opening of the fire tower. Four boys worked as carpenters. Hill was the mixer with Eva and Phyllis Josephs as helpers. John Evarts, Chenkin and Roman worked on the wheelbarrows and I had to help them bring the wheelbarrows up for the pouring. Three girls helped on the pouring, and we finished the wall at about 4:30, the time to go home and dress for the Thanksgiving dinner. Bobby Dreier had come over and refreshed us all with hot coffee which was most welcome.⁹⁴

Some individuals, including student Isaac “Ike” Nakata, shone amid all this dusty labor. Born on the island of Oahu, Hawaii, in 1919, Ike was raised in a company house in a company village and, like his father, worked for a corporate sugar plantation, where from a young age he did it all—weeding, fertilizing, irrigating, and tending the fields. But Ike wanted out—from the plantation system, which he hated, and from the unfair expectations of his widowed father. “As the last one, the youngest,” he wrote, “I took the brunt of his self-pity, his accusations of ingratitude.” As eager as he was to leave and not look back, Ike decided he would work three years beyond high school graduation to help his father and save for college. In summer 1940, he read a backpage article in a Honolulu newspaper about Black Mountain College. He was intrigued by the school’s educational experimentation, attracted to its communal living, and drawn in by the accompanying photo showing students playing chess on a sundeck. He arrived in late summer 1940, just as the college began its building spree at Lake Eden.⁹⁵

At first, Ike felt out of place. Still “green from the sugarcane fields of Oahu,” as he said it. The shy and observant but built-like-a-boxer young man was surrounded by students who had been trained at expensive private schools where they had taken college prep classes, which in turn prepared them to thrive as participants in the college’s soaring intellectual discussions. By contrast, Ike had taken required classes in high school (no calculus or algebra, no French or Latin), and had devoted Sundays, his one day off, to correspondence courses in literature and history. His lack of funds made his education feel precarious, but within a year things changed, due in part to classes with sharply observant economist Karl Niebyl, and history, literature, and theater courses with talented professor Eric Bentley. Years later, after the war, Ike returned to the college, this time with GI funding. No longer racing through coursework to save money, he took classes that interested him, traveled to New York and Washington, DC, to expand his horizons, and hitched occasional rides to nearby Asheville, where he enjoyed a T-bone steak or Chinese food.⁹⁶

[Légende] Previous two spreads: Construction of the barn, featuring Pearson Mundy (in white overalls) and Bob Bliss (next to Mundy, in black pants), at Lake Eden campus, spring 1942.

Though he felt more comfortable over time, Ike experienced racism on campus. One day, during an otherwise routine game of touch football, he made a professor look bad, prompting him to call Ike “a damn Jap.” Ike recalled, “I was too dumbfounded to take insult.” Starting in 1944, the college made a concerted effort to racially integrate their academic community, first with Alma Stone (Williams), the first Black student at the college, then with Dr Percy Baker, a biologist from Virginia State College, next with painter Jacob Lawrence, and later with five Black students, including Mary Parks Washington and Ora Marie Williams.

The complex intersections of race, cultural identity, segregation and community among white and Black staff, students and faculty, Japanese-American students like Ike and Ruth Asawa, as well as dozens of immigrant faculty and faculty families, many of them Jewish, plus local residents merits its own book-length treatment. In the meantime, interested readers should look up Bryan Barcena’s contribution to 2015’s Leap before you look: Black Mountain College, and Brenda Danilowitz’s chapter in Black Mountain: An Interdisciplinary Experiment.

Ike challenged his introverted ways by seeking friendship with these new students, but he still felt a bit lost at sea. “I was hardly attuned to the white mainstream,” he wrote, “or the black sidestream. Where was the little creek in which I would swim?”⁹⁷

Ike instead tuned into his natural surroundings, developing a passion for foraging and other outdoor pursuits. He hunted for wild strawberries with fellow student and future wife Alexa McLane, and giant blackberries, which Jack Lipsey baked into pies. Once, he accompanied history professor David Corkran to the backcountry to purchase applejack, the original moonshine, from a subsistence farmer. During his time at Black Mountain College after the war, Ike would walk with math professor and resident ecologist Max Dehn who taught him how to forage mushrooms, wild persimmons, sassafras root, cress, garlic, dandelions, hickory, chestnuts, and walnuts.⁹⁸

But most of all, Ike threw himself into physical labor. As a devoted member of the Work Program, he joined small teams to haul coal, mix concrete, and cut pine and tulip poplar trees—all to help build the barn. He gathered creek rock and rock slabs, chopped trees for lumber and scaffolding, cleared brush, and dug ditches. More than anything, he worked tirelessly in the cornfields. With years of experience on the sugar plantation in Hawaii, Ike knew exactly what to do.⁹⁹

SUMMER WORK CAMPS

As the amount of work needed at Lake Eden became abundantly clear, the college sought ways to increase labor. Some BMC members, including Ted and Bobbie Dreier, looked to the American Friends Service Committee (AFSC), a Quaker organization, and their two decades’ experience running work camps. An early example of what is now called community-engaged learning or, before that, service learning, the work camps invited college and university students to live, work, and participate in areas of economic turmoil—such as the Mississippi cotton delta or the Pennsylvania coalfields—for eight weeks in summer. To learn more, Bobbie Dreier tapped into her family’s social reform network to bring AFSC member J. Olcutt Sanders to campus for guidance. The school’s first summer work camp was initiated in 1940. It enrolled about 20 female and male students who worked for five hours a day, six days a week, for which they were paid $65 plus room and board for eight weeks. Although successful in terms of launching the Lake Eden build program, the summer 1940 work camp, helmed by Gothe, was run haphazardly and often overlapped awkwardly with Lake Eden Inn’s activities. Further, unlike the Friends’ work camps, there was very little integration between the camp and the community. Some work campers said the only thing they remember doing was working.¹⁰⁰

Fortunately the 1941 summer work camp was a vast departure from the previous year. First, Gothe, for whom Dreier had held high hopes, was gone. Although he had successfully established a culture of work and moved building and farm projects forward, Gothe was hated by the students. He barked out instructions, expected the students to have endless skills and energy, and got enraged when they didn’t. “He ran the organization like an army,” recalled Molly Gregory. “He was a fascist,” remembered student Suzanne Noble. Even gentle Bob Bliss called Gothe “the SS man.” By the end of spring 1941, Gothe was asked not to return, prompting a collective sigh of relief.¹⁰¹

That second year, 26 campers—BMC students and alumni as well as students from other universities—joined the work camp. They helped with construction on new buildings and old ones too, excavating under the lodges to install heating systems, constructing chimneys and installing insulation in the cottages, and renovating the bathhouse into a science classroom and dark room. They also worked on the slowly developing farm, helping Penley plant beans, corn, squash, and potatoes.¹⁰²

Unlike the previous year, not only were their bodies put to work, but their sensibilities were stimulated too. Participants enjoyed regular field trips, had access to the college’s library and record collection, engaged with BMC faculty, and attended an impressive series of talks by guest lecturers. Dr Warner F. Brook, from the New School for Social Research, organized a summer course, The World Today, and Dr Jacob Klein, of St. John’s College, gave a series of lectures on Descartes, Galileo, and Hobbes. In a four-part lecture series, Dr Arthur Raper, from the Bureau of Agricultural Economics, discussed economics and race in the American South.¹⁰³

[Légende] Starting in summer 1940, Black Mountain College hosted Summer Work Camps, which attracted both BMC and visiting students to work on campus for room, board, and a modest stipend.

Among the work, campers’ most important task was leveling the ground upon which the barn would be built, on what was then a substantial hillside. Using pickaxes and shovels and the college’s new Farmall H tractor, campers worked alongside Mark the (award-winning) horse to chip away at the hillside to make a straight and stable flatland for the coming structure.¹⁰⁴

By mid-May 1942 the barn was complete. At 54 ft long, 44 ft wide, and two-and-a-half stories high, it was enormous. The main floor was divided into 11 stalls, each with stanchions, feed troughs, and drinking fountains at every other stall. The second floor, constructed from campus pine, had openings on both sides for loading and unloading grain. The loft, also built from campus pine, had a storage capacity of 75 tons of hay. The barn was fully electrified, painted red, and sat on a small hill overlooking the farm; it was stunning from every angle. Today, the barn is still standing but is used for weddings and events, rather than housing livestock.

In late May 1942, Black Mountain College celebrated the completion of the barn with a barn dance. College dances were nothing out of the ordinary. Throughout BMC’s first eight years at Blue Ridge, dancing took place after dinner on Saturday nights. Everyone dressed up and the dancing lasted for hours, with live piano played by music professor John Evarts. For this dance, the college truck hauled a piano from the Round House, the college music library and practice space named after its shape, to the barn so that Evarts could bang out songs. Proud of their accomplishment, the Black Mountain community waltzed, polkaed, and fox-trotted around the pristine, cavernous barn all night long.

CHAPTER 6

MOLLY GREGORY RAISES THE ROOF

[Légende] Mary “Molly” Gregory, faculty of woodworking, woodshop supervisor, one-time farm manager, and the author’s personal MVP of BMC, circa 1945–46.

By all accounts Bill Hanchett was a reliable, hardworking young man. Born into an affluent family in Evanston, Illinois, in 1922, William “Bill” Francis Hanchett Jr struggled academically, but shined as class president. When the Depression wiped out his family’s savings, Bill worked in the school cafeteria during lunch, on a farm after school, and in a factory on weekends to earn money. In summer 1940, during his sister Alice-Anne’s wedding to Robert “Bob” Babcock, Hanchett and Babcock got to talking about college. Hanchett confessed he had neither the grades nor the funds to attend school. Babcock, who taught political science at Black Mountain College, regaled him with stories about students and faculty working together on the farm and constructing campus buildings. He then hooked his new brother-in-law by noting the college’s lack of emphasis on grades and its negotiable tuition. A year later, Bill came to BMC,¹⁰⁵

After a few months on the farm Hanchett found his calling: raising chickens. Having impressed Penley with his farming skills and Dreier with his reliability, Hanchett was granted permission to expand the chicken flock that had been started almost a decade earlier. “Congratulations are in order!” he wrote to his parents in mid-March 1942, after a trip to a stock auction in Asheville to purchase Rhode Island Red chicks. “Your farmer correspondent is the proud guardian of 85 day-old chicks!” To house the chicks, Bill built a large heated brooder, which would find a home in the basement of the two-story wooden South Lodge, one of two student dormitories. When only one of his chicks died during the first ten days, Hanchett’s confidence grew, and he returned to Asheville to pick up 100 more. Two weeks later he purchased another 100, and by mid-April Bill was tending over 250 chicks. Albers was so impressed by his husbandry that he bought eight ducks and gifted them to Hanchett to raise.¹⁰⁶

And then, just like that, it ended. In late spring 1942, Hanchett, like so many other young men at the college and across the country, was drafted. He would be stationed at the US Army Air Force Base in Douglas, Georgia. Within a year all young, American-born male BMC faculty were drafted or enlisted into the war effort. The draft had a dramatic impact on the college’s current male students too. The 1942–43 school year was like an exodus, recalled music student Patsy Lynch. “All of the boys left one by one, you know. Every two weeks or so there’d be a going away party until finally they all left.” By October 1943, a single male student, Sam Brown, who suffered from polio, remained at Black Mountain College.¹⁰⁷

Historically, not much attention has been given to the goings-on at Black Mountain College during the war years. But during this time BMC experienced a flurry of activity performed by the women who remained on campus. Under the leadership of the extraordinary Mary “Molly” Gregory, the college spent much of the war growing all of its food and, against all wartime odds, building an ambitious series of farm structures. Along the way Molly assembled a group of female students who would bring the college closer to self-sufficiency than it ever had been or ever would be. And when the building process was finished, Black Mountain College had established a massive capital investment that would save the college a decade later.

GETTING TO BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE

In 1919 when World War I was over and Molly Gregory was five, her family moved from Long Island, New York, to Framingham, Massachusetts, to buy back the family farm. Growing up, Molly, a scruffy kid with boundless energy, “literally followed the heels of the farmer,” and became obsessed with growing food, raising animals, and building farm structures. From age 13 to 18, she attended Beaver Country Day School, a progressive institution that integrated farming and woodworking into the curriculum. Molly excelled at art and developed into a smart, creative, and well-liked student. Even as a young person, her strong sense of character shone through. Beatrice W. Van Ness, head of the school’s art department said her “personality is vigorous and stimulating, and her work reflects the high quality of her thinking. She has a saving sense of humor and is capable of carrying much responsibility.”¹⁰⁸

In 1932, Molly entered Vermont’s Bennington College, then a women’s college, just as it was established. Like Black Mountain College, Bennington placed visual and performing arts at the center of its curriculum. Unlike BMC, the Vermont school planned its existence for nearly a decade before opening its doors to students. At Bennington Molly took a wide variety of impressive classes, including dance with the illustrious Martha Graham and a semester abroad at the Bermuda Biological Station for Research during her sophomore year. Her focus, however, was studying sculpture with Simon Moselsio, a sculptor, painter, and draftsman. Molly’s roommate and best friend at Bennington was Ruth Bailey, an art major and younger sister of original BMC student and initiator of the chicken flock, Dave Bailey. In 1936, Molly became a member of Bennington’s first graduating class.¹⁰⁹

Upon graduating, Molly, short-haired, attractive, always in flannel tops and blue jeans, and usually holding a cigarette, established what she called her five-year plan. The idea was simple: She would find an appealing job, work there for five years, quit, find another one, and repeat for the rest of her working life. Her first five-year plan was as a teacher at the prestigious Cambridge School, in Kendal Green, Massachusetts, led by headmaster John French, father of Nat. While hired to teach sculpture, Molly also taught ceramics, painting, and life drawing, and oversaw athletics and directed the woodshop. She particularly enjoyed her time with shop teacher Alfred Hulst, who helped Molly refine the woodworking and building skills she had learned on the family farm.

During her third year at the Cambridge School Molly was reunited with Ruth Bailey, who arrived to teach art following a one-year apprenticeship at Black Mountain College. Ruth talked nonstop about BMC, especially about Josef Albers. When Ruth visited the campus in spring 1940, Molly tagged along. During her visit Molly attended classes, led a work day on the farm, and made an impression on both students and faculty.¹¹⁰

Dreier in particular was taken by Molly’s enthusiastic know-how, and over the next year he sent her long letters describing recent progress on the farm. In spring 1941, Molly and Ruth visited the college again, but this visit was more like an interview for Molly. She met with students (who adored her), faculty (who were impressed by her), and staff (all fans). She also sat in on classes, attended a general meeting, and visited the modest but full-of-potential farm. Clearly the college wanted Molly, and Molly wanted the college. But in what capacity? Eventually she was invited to become a teaching apprentice for Josef Albers, with her modest pay supported by Ethel Forbes, who, along with husband Mac, was one of the college’s original funders. Molly was scheduled to start in her role in fall 1941. She was so excited she arrived that summer.¹¹¹

MOLLY GREGORY AND THE WOODSHOP

Like so many others at Black Mountain College, Molly found Albers’ classes to be exhilarating. “He asked us to look and look,” Molly recalled, “but, in looking, to trust and to use our own perceptions creatively and neatly.” Not being a novice, however, she ultimately felt inhibited in Albers’ classroom and experienced creative blocks. The solution for this industrious young woman, it seemed, was to take fewer classes with Albers and teach more of her own. By spring semester of her first year at BMC, Molly was teaching popular classes in sculpture and woodworking.¹¹²

From the start, Black Mountain College had provided modest opportunities in woodworking, but Molly completely transformed these opportunities. Shocked at the lack of tools provided to students—“They just let them practically use their front teeth”—she gathered tools lazily spread across Lake Eden, reclaimed tools left behind at Blue Ridge, and generously made available her own extensive collection (“a rather shocking amount of tools”). Molly established regular shop hours six days a week during which any member of the community could learn woodworking skills and work on projects. Most importantly, Molly transformed the woodshop from a space where somewhat random individual works were produced to an organized shop with a mission: to build much-needed items for Black Mountain College.¹¹³

[Légende] Another busy day at Black Mountain College’s woodshop, featuring, left to right, student Jane “Slats” Slater, Molly Gregory, unidentified student, and Horace McGuire “Mac” Wood, summer 1944.

The shop produced tables for the dining room, cupboards for the kitchen, bookshelves for the library, drafting tables for architecture classes, stools and carving benches for the art room, shelves for the music room, paper storage racks for the bookbinding shop, benches for the lobbies inside both the North and South Lodges, and much more. In November 1942, just over a year after Molly’s arrival, the woodshop was humming with do-it-yourself activity. Barbara Heller, a student from New York, was constructing five benches for the weaving department. Claude Stoller, an architecture student from the Bronx, was busy making a milk rack for the kitchen. Lou Bernard Voight, professor of landscape architecture and botany, was constructing cabinets and a table for his study. And art student Ati Gropius was preparing for a room party by “making impromptu table settings: grapefruit plates, shallow circles cut on the lathe into six-inch mahogany squares, a set of delicate three-pronged forks, and little butter knives from the wood of an old walnut tree—lathe-shaped, sanded, and oiled.” Countless other students built rudimentary furniture—chairs, desks, bookshelves, easels—for their rooms.¹¹⁴

With Molly at the helm, the woodshop trained a new generation of students how to hammer, saw, and drill—how to make things. Molly also shared her extensive leather skills, gained during her Bennington days, teaching students how to make their own sandals, among other things. Within a year, Molly and the woodshop sustained a campus-wide culture of making, a collective behavior that would help the college through the lean war years and beyond. “It was asked from the start,” Molly recalled, “that everyone who joined the community be a contributor, not a purchaser.”¹¹⁵

VICTORY FARM

Though much of the land had been cleared and the new barn was impressive, ambitions to actually develop the farmland at Lake Eden were coming along slower than expected. The only other structures were a hastily built small barn and a derelict, unheated farmhouse, and the only field under cultivation was a single plot near the farmhouse, which grew more weeds than veggies. Only one of the pastures was usable because, until then, the main focus had been soil health. For the last four years Penley had planted and plowed under acres of cover crops, hoping to reclaim the fields and pastures, with only a small amount of edible crops planted. But without Norm to lead, scheduling across the multiple farms fell largely to Dreier who couldn’t keep up. When it came to delegating the farm work to other people, Dreier proved equally ineffectual.

[Légende] Previous spread: Farmer Ross Penley (center, in hat) and two students prepare corn for harvest with the Studies Building in the background, 1943.

He had a reputation for exhorting students to work for some kind of spiritual enlightenment, micromanaging them when they did show up, and publicly shaming them when they didn’t. Too often the plots were planted and tended in fits and bursts and sometimes yielded more consternation than food.¹¹⁶

Although the college would have preferred a slow and steady buildup of healthy soil and farmland, the country’s involvement in the war dramatically sped up the process. Led by Dreier, members of the college began to make contingency plans for inevitable shortages. A shortage of meat meant raising their own, which would be difficult since there was also a growing shortage of available livestock. Further, growing more food and raising more animals required the construction of structures for storage and shelter, a daunting task due to shortages of building materials and male labor, not to mention the college’s perennial cash flow issues. Like the rest of the country, BMC was facing difficult times.

To Dreier, the solution was clear. Black Mountain College could and must boost self-sufficiency by growing more food, raising more animals, and building more farm structures—as quickly and inexpensively as possible. A farm, he would announce to anyone within earshot, could profoundly help the college during wartime by supplying the college with dairy, meat, and vegetables and, like the victory gardens sprouting up across the country that bolstered morale while supplying produce, the farm would be a patriotic contribution to the war effort. Beyond survival and patriotism, the farm could become a strategic capital investment, a rainy day fund if difficult times got worse. This was particularly important since the college had neither a well-developed alumni-donor class nor a wealthy board of trustees to whom they could turn during rough stretches.¹¹⁷

Although Dreier loved dreaming about the farm, building it was overwhelming. Enter Molly Gregory, who immersed herself in the farm plan and enjoyed figuring out multi-year planting schedules. She relished balancing forests, fields, and pastures, and celebrated when things worked, like when the dairy herd spent a day grazing the new barley pasture and each cow responded by producing two more pounds of milk than usual. Molly also gave Penley the support he needed. Unlike Dreier, who had assigned the farmer endless tasks that all seemed to need immediate attention, Molly, an experienced farmer, was able to think holistically and plan and prioritize, gaining Penley’s trust and respect. Within a year of living at Black Mountain College, Molly was managing the farm, and within a year under her leadership, the farm was humming, producing beans, beets, cabbage, carrots, chard, collards, cucumbers, onions, peas, potatoes, radishes, squash, and tomatoes—amounting to most of the produce the community ate.¹¹⁸

In fact, the farm produced so much food that Black Mountain College had a surplus. So once a week, Molly gathered up sacks of vegetables, hopped into the college truck, and drove to Swannanoa and the Asheville Farm School to use their canning machinery. During two weeks in July 1943, Molly and students canned 100 gallons of beets and 85 gallons of beans and put 50 gallons of kraut into crocks, increasing the college’s winter stores. Though the operation strengthened the ties between Black Mountain College and the Asheville Farm School, there was one notable exception. After Molly was caught smoking in the cannery, in violation of the school’s smoking ban, the school’s superintendent Henry S. Randolph angrily called her into his office, had her take a seat, and glared at her from behind his desk. Molly apologized profusely, placed a can of beets on his desk, and slowly moved it in his direction. Randolph accepted both her apology and the beets.¹¹⁹

At Lake Eden, acres of field corn, soybean hay, vetch hay, barley, and rye were grown for animal feed. At the same time, although the college had fully vacated from Lee Hall and the old campus, they continued to rent fields for cultivation at the Blue Ridge farm. When the harvests from Lake Eden and Blue Ridge were not sufficient to feed their growing—in girth and number—live stock, the college rented plots from neighboring farmers. Juggling multiple plots was difficult and harvest time was arduous; it often took Penley and teams of ten or more students three days to harvest all their corn. The work was hard but rewarding. Ruth Lyford Sussler, a summer session student in 1944, recalled the process of cutting cornstalks for silage:

    The flow of energy that bristled from this gathering of souls was powerful. The day’s work swallowed you up, carried you along in the rhythm of swinging machetes. The whole cornstalk was cut down and then tossed onto piles which were gathered and fed into a grinder, sucked up and blown into the top of the silo. The noise and dust created in this process was enormous. The excitement and energy released in this ritual of harvesting the corn gave magic to the hard physical labor taking place in the hot sun, making the work seem effortless. The importance of community manual labor as a balance to solitary thinking and artistic effort could be well understood.¹²⁰

Fortunately, with Molly’s outreach, the college received much-needed outside help to harvest the silage. In late summer 1944, the nearby orphanage let the college borrow their silage cutter. In return, Max Penley, the farmer’s son, spent three days helping the orphanage take in their silage. Also that summer they enlisted the brawn of seven soldiers from the 134th Field Artillery who were convalescing at nearby Moore General Hospital. The soldiers volunteered on the farm at Black Mountain College for two straight weeks and, as Molly recalled, “really saved our lives.”¹²¹

WHEN THE COLLEGE WAS FEMALE

Always hatching a new scheme for financial betterment, Dreier, immediately following the move from Blue Ridge to Lake Eden, became an “earnest, eager, and enthusiastic” advocate for developing a dairy herd. When Marshall Field III, a banker, philanthropist, and heir to the Marshall Field department store fortune, declined to donate money to the college, Dreier convinced Field to instead give them a bull. “King’s Regent of Dellwood,” the bull, came to campus in fall 1941. A few months later the college purchased two thoroughbred Guernsey cows and bought Penley’s wife Stella’s cow as well. By this time Dreier was so cow-crazy, he began asking donors and foundations for cattle rather than cash. When that didn’t work out, eager to add a beef herd to the college’s growing dairy herd, Dreier spent his own money ($815) on five Angus cattle from Phoenixville, Pennsylvania, and had them transported down to Black Mountain.¹²²

Prompted in part by Dreier’s acquisition sprees, and under Molly’s careful leadership, Black Mountain College’s livestock operation grew significantly. By the end of summer 1943 the college was raising 35 cows (eight dairy cows, 25 beef cows and calves, and two bulls), 33 pigs, and over 100 chickens, mostly from Hanchett’s original flock. The college was also home to three horses (Lady and Pearl, mother and daughter mares, and Derek, a colt), a flock of nine ducks, and one pet goat raised and tended by the Dreier’s eldest son, Ted Dreier Jr. With the farm providing the college with all its milk and butter and most of its meat and poultry, the animals fed the BMC community and provided much-needed money. Throughout the war, the college sold calves, cows, young pigs, and hogs to neighbors and nearby farms. Like Nan, Norm, and the original student farmers, Molly stressed the farm as an exciting source of community building, a space to bring people together, to fulfill one’s responsibility, a place to have some fun. Accordingly, students clamored to work with her—in the classroom, in the woodshop, and on the farm.¹²³

One of these students was Janet Heling. Born on Long Island, New York, in 1925, Heling learned about Black Mountain College through a magazine article that featured photographs of young women and men doing farm work together. She arrived at the college to study social science in the fall of 1943. Mentored by Molly, Janet worked as the farm sub-coordinator, scheduling teams of students to weed and water veggie beds, harvest apples and grapes, and hoe the cornfields. She also helped record the farm’s food production and write up brief entries for the Black Mountain College Community Bulletin, a periodically published newsletter for campus and alumni.¹²⁴

During Molly’s time as farm manager, work campers turned full-time students Jagna Braunthal and Ati Gropius had a chance to run the college’s milking operations. Born in Germany in 1927, Braunthal and her family fled the Nazi Germany to Austria, then Belgium, and finally to New York in 1936. Shy but exceptionally intelligent, with an interest in physics and chemistry, Braunthal graduated high school early, participated in two American Friends (Quaker) work camps, and learned how to lay stone, mix concrete, harvest hay, and milk cows along the way. She spent a year at Queens College, in New York, but found it devoid of the cooperative spirit she enjoyed at the work camps. When she learned about Black Mountain College’s program she quickly applied. As leader of milking operations, each morning and evening at five, Braunthal walked ten minutes from campus to the farm to milk the college’s cows in the summer of 1944.¹²⁵

From there, Beate “Ati” Gropius took over. The adopted daughter of Bauhaus founder and friend-of-the-college, Walter Gropius and his wife, Ise, Ati, whose long, curly hair, dark eyebrows, and ample confidence gave her a regal look, was a difficult teenager. She recalled her youth as excruciating for her and her parents, so when they announced in 1943 that they were sending her to Black Mountain College work camp, she feared the worst. “I had only seen pictures of men and women bare armed or bare chested, working away with shovels and hammering things, and I said, ‘Oh my God, it’s a prison reform school!” The reality, however, was much different. Within 36 hours of arriving she vowed never to leave.¹²⁶

Ati was quickly put in charge of cooling the milk. Each morning at 7:30, she collected the five-gallon pails of fresh milk, poured them into smaller cans, and placed some of the cans in the pantry for breakfast, some in the kitchen for the cooks, and some in a refrigerator for BMCers, including for families with young children. Each night at 6:15 she repeated the process and also skimmed the milk for cream and, later, butter.¹²⁷

One evening Elsa Kahl, who taught eukinetics, a course on body conditioning and dramatic movement, entered the kitchen and asked Ati for some skimmed milk fat to make fresh butter. Kahl’s butter was an instant sensation. Everyone who tried it wanted more. “The rhythm of it seems to appeal to her eukinetic self,” literature professor Kenneth Kurtz waxed. But there was a catch. To get more butter, they would have to make less cream. And so, like most decisions at Black Mountain College, it was settled at a general meeting.

A small group of two or three argued against skimming the milk for fat to make butter, preferring whole milk for their morning coffee. Practically everyone else at the college was on board for more butter, resting their case, it seems, entirely on the quality of the stuff that Kahl had whipped up. In what may have been the fastest reached consensus in Black Mountain College history, it was decided “to have partially skimmed milk and homemade butter instead of full milk and no butter.” Within a few weeks Black Mountain College was producing 15 lb of butter a week. Unfortunately, not all of it had been whipped by Kahl’s magic hands,¹²⁸

MOLLY GREGORY

BUILDS

Molly Gregory spent much of the war years building a new farm, beginning with a milking house. As the college became serious about developing a dairy herd, Molly visited the Swannanoa Test Farm, surveyed their milking operations, and returned with a basic plan (a “government pamphlet type of thing”) for a milking house. She tasked Charlie and Parley to build it and asked student Gisela Kronenberg to wire it.¹²⁹

Born in Germany in 1922, Gisela and her family fled the Nazis in 1935 and settled in Cincinnati, Ohio. Drawn to Black Mountain College because she liked that students could select their own classes, Gisela arrived in fall 1940, studied psychology, and graduated within three years. A sharp, diligent student, she loved working with Bas Allen and threw herself into installing electricity, putting in subflooring, building up walls, and doing masonry work. After working extensively on the Studies Building, wiring the milking house was easy; she finished within a few days.¹³⁰

In the summer of 1942, as both the college’s livestock and their need for feed grew, Molly and Morton “Mort” Steinau, the college’s assistant treasurer, researched various corn silos and their costs. They worked closely with Albers, by this time the college’s unofficial landscape architect, who surveyed the farm and declared the best location for the silos. Molly delegated the building of the silos to Charlie, Mundy, and two students—Nancy West, a drama student from Hartford, Connecticut, and Zoya Sandomirsky, a summer student.¹³¹

A year earlier, Zoya and her family had escaped war-torn Europe and landed in Chicago. Traumatized by the war, she felt like an outsider, “a total social and emotional misfit.” Zoya enrolled in a high school but quickly dropped out. Without a high school diploma, she managed to be admitted to the University of Chicago but was reluctant to attend. A friend of her mother’s suggested Black Mountain College and convinced her to try it out during a summer session. Surrounded by other refugees, including the Alberses, musicologist Heinrich Jalowetz, and especially Frances de Graaff, who taught languages and Russian history and regularly took time to check in with the young transplant, Zoya began to heal. She particularly relished building the silos, which “helped to satisfy my longing for a communal, socialist experience.” During the final stages of the project, H. G. Moffat, from the Pomona Terra Cotta Company, joined the college to direct the tiling of the silos. Moffat was so impressed with Black Mountain College’s can-do spirit that he donated $5 on his way out.¹³²

On a bit of a building streak, Molly turned her attention to their farm machinery, which, by the early 1940s, included a Hough Loader tractor, flatbed farm truck, Fresno scraper, disk plow, wheat drill, and bush and bog harrow. To house all of this a machinery shed was built—and still exists—which is 20 ft wide by 60 ft long. It was constructed entirely out of campus lumber and could store equipment and machinery on one side and up to 1,200 bushels of corn on the other.¹³³

The summer of 1943 saw Molly design and build three more farm structures. After Guernsey bull King’s Regent of Dellwood and Black Burgess, a registered Angus bull, got into a nasty fight, separate pens and strong fences were immediately required. She and Bas hastily designed a new bullpen and enlisted work campers to chop endless wood to make over 300 stakes for the pen’s fence. Bas and Pearson Mundy, who by now was working for the college rather than for Charlie, handled the construction.

Within two weeks a giant pen fit to separate a pair of warring bulls was built. “As solid as the Rock of Gibraltar,” is how Molly described it, “and Mundy and Bas are frightfully proud.” The only casualty of the bullpen’s construction was Molly’s good standing with the Board of Fellows. Opting for swift action after the fight, she’d gone ahead without asking their permission. “I got in the doghouse with the Board of Fellows,” she recalled, a flareup that would escalate then defuse over the next year.¹³⁴

With the bullpen complete, Molly focused on building a new hogpen, a task the college had been avoiding for years. For nearly a decade Nat’s pens—built at Blue Ridge in 1934 and carted, piece by piece, to Lake Eden in the late 1930s—had suited the college’s porcine needs. But as the hogs grew in size and number, a new pen was needed. Construction began during the first week of August 1943, with students cutting, splitting, and hauling 200 stakes for the fence. The next week a work crew dug holes, set the stakes, and strung wire to form the fence. Molly designed the hoghouse and built it entirely in the woodshop, constructing six-foot prefabricated sections, lugging the pieces to the farm, and assembling them in situ. By the end of the month, the new hogpen was complete. To celebrate, work campers and summer students joined Molly and Penley for a Friday-night hayride in the farm wagon, driven by Lady and Pearl. Leisurely they rode from Lake Eden to downtown Black Mountain, square danced in the town square, and returned to campus by midnight. While all the humans were relieved the hogpen was complete, the hogs were positively thrilled. “The pigs are happily rooting and snorting,” wrote Molly in a Community Bulletin. “They have been waiting for this experience for two years.”¹³⁵

The third farm structure built during the summer of 1943 was the henhouse. When Bill Hanchett left the college for the Air Force in spring 1942, it was unclear who would tend to the 250 chicks, or “Bill’s babies” as they were known. For the next year the chickens had been housed in “a rather questionable shanty,” according to Molly, behind the barn. Their plight improved, sort of, during the summer of 1943 thanks to work camper Harold Yanagi. Born in Honolulu, Hawaii, in 1920, Yanagi studied architecture at the Universities of Hawaii, Michigan, and at Cornell, before graduating from the Illinois Institute of Technology. Looking to continue his design studies and get hands-on experience, Yanagi came to BMC hoping to study with Kocher. When he arrived at Lake Eden, he learned that the beloved architecture professor had taken a leave of absence, followed by his resignation. Disappointed, Yanagi rallied and got involved in the building of the bullpen, then jumped at the chance to build the henhouse. Like the hoghouse, the henhouse was constructed entirely in the woodshop using 6 ft prefab components, some of which were made with wood salvaged from Nat’s old pigpen. Though appreciative of Yanagi’s efforts, Molly was less than pleased with her own design. The chickens, it seems, weren’t thrilled either. Most of them refused to live in it, electing instead to sleep and brood in the nearby trees. Stung by the chickens’ ingratitude and disappointed in their paltry egg production, the college began eating the chickens, reducing the flock to about 50.¹³⁶

The final and most controversial farm building constructed during the wartime summer of 1943 was the beef shed. By then the college’s beef herd had grown considerably, resulting in cramped quarters in the barn. That fall Molly tasked Bas and Mundy with building a beef shed directly attached to one side of the barn. Work crews cut down campus trees, chopped them into logs, and hauled them to the nearby Morris Saw Mill to make lumber. By February 1944, the Board of Fellows, learning that the beef shed was over budget, and still smarting from Molly’s unauthorized bullpen project, halted the project and hauled Molly into a meeting. When, they wanted to know, did we turn into a farm school?¹³⁷

To settle the bad blood, Molly organized a field trip for board members to the farm where she reviewed two years’ worth of building. Next, she led a general meeting for the larger campus community to explain the past, present, and future of the farm. A peacemaker extraordinaire, Molly reminded the community of its goal of self-sufficiency, highlighted the vital contributions of so many BMC members, and reassured everyone that the beef shed would be the last new farm building until the war was over. Her strategy worked: the board acquiesced and construction resumed.¹³⁸

RAISING THE ROOF

Beginning in 1944, the previously reliable Penley became less and less so. When he suddenly left to work at the Morgan Manufacturing Company, the college hired Roger Banks, a 19-year-old farmer from Warren Wilson Junior College (formerly the Asheville Farm School). It was a disaster. Molly persuaded Penley to return; Penley and Banks didn’t get along; Banks left. Penley remained, semi-committed and semi-sober. George Zabriskie, who taught writing and poetry at BMC during the 1944–45 school year, recalled Penley drunk on moonshine, “plow[ing] fields at night, wasting precious gasoline during wartime, and keeping people awake.” By March 1946, after Penley had left, returned, and left again, the college had had enough. Molly—already a full-time faculty member, woodshop supervisor, and farm manager—became head farmer. She could swing it, she said, but not for long.¹³⁹

Truth be told, Molly was exhausted. She loved Black Mountain College but was growing increasingly weary of the constant faculty fights. As Duberman wrote: “Slice Black Mountain open at almost any point in its history and a comparable set of grievances, complaints, and animosities can be found.” The battles were often about policy, more often personal, and always about power. They simmered for a year or two and boiled over every three or four years. Although the battlelines were seldom clearly drawn, they usually included older, long-term faculty like Josef Albers and Ted Dreier on one side, and younger, newer faculty on the other. The outcome was always the same: a handful of faculty and a dozen or so loyal students would angrily leave or resign, plunging the college into deeper instability and, worse, depriving it of a new generation of ideas. In 1944, for example, five professors and 20 students left the college, over the speed—or lack thereof—of the college’s racial integration efforts, the misogynist treatment by Dreier and Albers of two female students unfairly jailed while hitchhiking in Tennessee, and differing opinions of what constituted “work,” with Eric Bentley, professor of English and drama, arguing that the plays he directed for campus were as important as the fields Dreier planted with corn. By 1946, a new battle was emerging between a young psychology professor named John Wallen—who sought to make the Work Program more efficient by drawing up detailed diagrams, lists, and tables and scheduling meetings, committees, and more committees—and Albers and Dreier, who wanted to keep things as they were. The battles were brutal and traumatic.¹⁴⁰

For two months Molly reached out to her broad network to find a suitable farmer for the college. Ultimately, at Dreier’s behest, Molly contacted Raymond “Ray” Trayer, who was working on a state farm, and whose wife Dorothy was a former student. As it happened, not only were Ray and Dorothy Trayer interested, but so was Ray’s farmer friend, Clifford “Cliff” Moles, and his wife Shirley. While the college was generally excited to land not one but two farmer families, they faced a daunting question: Where will they live? The farmhouse, empty since Penley’s sudden departure, was only built for a single family.¹⁴¹

The solution, Molly decided, was to raise the roof. She would take the farmhouse’s sloping roof and raise it 45 degrees, creating full-sized rooms where previously there was only attic space. She enlisted help from her woodworking students, including two future bright lights of the art world, Ruth Asawa and Ray Johnson. Though Molly was most helped by student Miriam “Mim” Sihvonen. Born in 1919 on Long Island and raised on a poultry farm in Connecticut, Mim studied nursing after high school and enlisted in the army. While stationed in Paris, she read Adamic’s “Education on a Mountain” in Reader’s Digest and was attracted to the college’s communal living. She arrived on campus in the spring of 1947. Although Mim intended to study writing, she immediately gravitated toward the Work Program and eventually took charge of it. An experienced amateur carpenter, she spent most of spring semester working with Molly to raise the roof of the farmhouse.¹⁴²

“A dream of spacious, well-planned comfort,” was how Evarts described the new farmhouse. While functional, it was also original, even fanciful. “It was a very funny project,” recalled Ilya Bolotowsky, an abstract painter who taught art at the college from 1946 to 1948, and who often visited the farm to witness progress on the roof raising. “I’m sure it was never done before—improvised very well. It looks as it might as well take off and fly—whoop!”¹⁴³

LEAVING LAKE EDEN

Having trained supervisors for the woodshop having found not one but two new farmers, and having transformed the farmhouse, Molly Gregory was ready to leave Black Mountain College, which she did in the summer of 1947. For her next five-year plan she traveled north to Woodstock, Vermont, to become manager and designer at Woodstock Enterprises, owned by original BMC student Dave Bailey. Here Molly oversaw a woodworking shop that first produced custom furniture and later built private homes. By 1953 she was managing two all-male crews of ten housebuilders. She left Woodstock in 1954, and her next five-year plan lasted for 34 years. From 1954 to 1988, when she retired at age 74, Molly ran her own custom woodworking shop in Lexington, then Lincoln, Massachusetts, specializing in design and construction of homes, kitchens, furniture, and an occasional henhouse. “T wanted to do every job myself, all of it,” she told Edward S. Cooke Jt, a professor of American decorative arts at Yale, “savoring the appropriateness or uniqueness of each assignment.” During this time, she also taught woodworking, painting, scenery making, and other crafts at Concord Academy.¹⁴⁴

One of Molly’s last Black Mountain College-related activities was at a small college reunion in Lenox, Massachusetts, in 1972. Organized by student Larry Fox and inspired by the release of Duberman’s Black Mountain, the first published history of the college, the reunion included about 30 BMC alumni brought together for two days and one night at a Jesuit monastery. On the first day, many attendees, including Ati Gropius, expressed disappointment that Molly wasn’t there. So at around six in the evening during cocktail hour, Ati called Molly.

    Ati: Are you coming?
    Molly: I have to plant my peas.
    Ati: Are you coming?
    Molly: I have to get a cow sitter.
    Ati: Coward.
    Molly: I’m coming.

And she did. After finding a cow and chicken sitter, Molly hopped in her car with her dog Grisselda and drove from Lincoln to Lenox. She arrived at midnight to a near fully awake reunion shrieking “Molly!”¹⁴⁵

CHAPTER 7

THE QUAKERS

[Légende] Quaker, conscientious objector, and farmer Raymond “Ray” Trayer prepares feed for cattle, 1948.

From an early age Raymond “Ray” Trayer considered himself a pacifist. Born in 1916 in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, he was raised as a member of the Fourth Reformed Church of Christ but was repeatedly drawn to the Quakers and their long history of pacifism. In high school Ray participated in two American Friends Service Committee work camps, including one on a coop farm in the Mississippi Delta. After high school he studied philosophy and religion at Franklin & Marshall College in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, and graduated Phi Beta Kappa in 1940. Shortly after marrying Dorothy “Dottie” Coldren, Ray was drafted. Citing his religion and pacifism, he declared himself a conscientious objector. After bouncing around multiple Civilian Public Service (CPS) camps, Ray eventually landed for two years in Ames at the Agricultural Experiment Station of Iowa State College. There he studied basic farming and conducted rudimentary experiments in soils and agronomy, often under the supervision of experienced farmer Clifford “Cliff” Moles.¹⁴⁶

Cliff Moles was born in 1920 and raised on a farm near Shenandoah, Iowa. Kind and hardworking, Moles and his father operated two well-respected local farms. 1942 was a big year for Moles. He married Shirley McWhorter, got drafted, and, citing his religion (Methodist) and pacificism, declared himself a conscientious objector. Moles was stationed at a CPS camp in Elmira, New York, moved on to manage the farm for a mental hospital in Concord, New Hampshire, and finally settled for two and a half years at the Agricultural Experiment Station (AES). A keen farmer and organizer, Moles was named farm manager of the Howard County Experimental Farm, an AES satellite in the northeast corner of Iowa, where he enjoyed freedom to carry out experimental agricultural techniques.¹⁴⁷

With Ray and Cliff finding common ground while at work in the fields, in the evenings of 1944 the Trayers and the Moleses often met for dinner. There was plenty of talk about farming and pacificism, but invariably the highlight was Dottie’s stories about her nine months at a small, experimental school called Black Mountain College. Dottie had graduated high school first in her class at age 16 and then trained as a lab technician. Now she worked in bacteriology at Iowa State College’s Veterinary Hygiene Department. But before that she had attended BMC to study piano under Frederic “Fritz” Cohen, composer, music professor, and husband to Elsa Kahl, butter master. Within months Dottie had joined the staff, first as college registrar and later as assistant treasurer. Over dinner in Ames she regaled the three others with stories about BMC community living, about general meetings that stretched well into the night, and about then-new woodworking professor Molly Gregory. She was gleeful when she relived a Saturday night campus concert where she played three pieces by Schubert. On the fields and over dinner, the Moleses and Trayers “became quite close friends,” recalled Cliff; he “hoped for some time we would work together for a common purpose.”¹⁴⁸

Fortunately, Ray had a plan for the four of them. From as early as November 1943, he began exchanging letters with Dreier. Ray kept Dreier abreast of his training at the Agricultural Experimental Station and his classes in crop production and welding taken at Iowa State College. Strategically, Ray appealed to Dreier’s professed decade-long dream to hire a farmer who could teach classes by littering his letters with casual mentions of agricultural-related articles and books, including agronomist Edward H. Faulkner’s 1943 book Plowman’s Folly. Finally, he regularly enclosed a modest donation check of $5 to the college.¹⁴⁹

In early July 1946, Cliff, Shirley, and their two-year-old daughter Diane arrived at Black Mountain College. Molly was positively gleeful about Cliff and “his quiet and really sound idealism.” Two months later, Ray, Dottie, and one-year-old daughter Susan arrived. With Molly fixing to leave, and these two families ready to take over the farm, a new era was set to begin.¹⁵⁰

THE MOLES-TRAYER REGIME

Under Cliff Moles and Ray Trayer, the farm at Black Mountain College became more orderly and organic. Moles took inventory of anything he could count, categorize, or measure, including farm buildings, machinery, tools, livestock, silage, and seed. He took endless soil samples across multiple fields. He pored through past and present farm budgets and studied the costs and returns of the livestock. Later, he and Trayer suggested the farm reduce the beef cattle, increase the dairy herd, and pause the pig population until further housing could be built and additional feed could be grown. Within a year of their arrival the farm exuded order. “They practically iron the fields after planting,” wrote Molly—who had stuck around long enough to witness this development—in the Black Mountain College Bulletin. “They are a mechanized pair and pretty gay with their tractor.¹⁵¹

The farm under Moles and Trayer followed a set of practices that have come to define organic farming. The focus was on building healthy soil, so they planted endless acres of cover crops. They established a large-scale composting operation that combined food scraps from the kitchen, greens from the garden, and manure from the farm to make rich, healthy compost to add to garden beds, fields, and orchards. Molly described the massive composting operation as “a whole village street of rectangle piles,” which Moles and Trayer examined “with almost morbid solemnity.” Finally, they initiated a series of soil conservation practices like contouring, ter-racing, and, influenced by Faulkner’s Plowman’s Folly—a foundational text for natural farming methods in the US—judicious use of the plow.¹⁵²

[Légende] Black Mountain College farmer Clifford “Cliff” Moles on tractor, 1948.

Moles and Trayer were aided in their efforts by a number of experienced and capable student-farmers, starting with William “Bill” Treichler. Born in 1924 to a homesteading family living in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, Treichler’s family grew their own food, raised and slaughtered their own animals, and baked their own bread out of homegrown wheat. When the war came Bill joined the Army Air Force and was stationed in England. While there, he read The Living Soil by Eve Balfour, a British farmer and pioneer of the emerging organic food and farming movement, and discovered that her experimental farm was within cycling distance from his airbase. He became a regular visitor and Balfour ordered books for the eager young farmer. Upon returning to Iowa, Treichler found his family obsessed with “decentralism,” a rejection of industrialism, urbanism, and consumerism and embrace of self-sufficiency, family-scale economies, and community cooperation. Together the family attended homesteading conferences organized by decentralist writer Mildred Loomis, and it was in Milton Wend’s How to Live in the Country Without Farming that Treichler’s mom found Black Mountain College in a list of colleges to which homesteading parents should send their children.¹⁵³

[Légende] Students Martha Rittenhouse and William “Bill” Treichler team up on the tractor to mow grass at Black Mountain College, spring 1948.

Arriving at Black Mountain College with his parents in late summer 1947, Treichler, who had high standards when it came to landscaping, was horrified by the lack of campus upkeep. While the farm had been taken over by trusted hands, the Work Program was in disarray and the campus had begun to flounder—a sign of things to come. “The school entrance looked weedy and the grounds were grown up with long grass,” Treichler recalled. “The property looked ignored and neglected.” After a full day of walking around campus, looking into buildings, touring the farm, and meeting with new writing professor, lauded poet and, later, potter M. C. (Mary Caroline) Richards, Treichler remained stunned by the school’s appearance and wanted to drive back to Iowa. “You should give the place a try,” his parents said reassuringly. “Maybe you can cut the grass and weeds.” Treichler relented.¹⁵⁴

And cut grass and weeds he did. Within days, Treichler connected with Moles and Trayer who gave him access to the tractor to use all over the campus, especially along its roads and around its buildings. Albers was impressed by Treichler’s efforts and, from his unofficial role as campus landscaper, gave him carte blanche on landscaping matters. Within weeks Treichler was entrusted to lead multiple farming operations and within months he became the college’s Work Program coordinator. During his time at BMC he would deepen his understanding of organic farming, in part due to the teachings of chemistry and physics professor Natasha Goldowski, a world expert on corrosive chemistry. Born in Moscow and trained in Paris, Goldowski had been a member of the US government’s top-secret Manhattan Project, which created the first atomic bomb.¹⁵⁵

During the first week of his second year at BMC, Treichler met Martha Rittenhouse, a new student from a farm in Maryland, who shared his interest in homesteading. Under the supportive watch of librarian Nell Rice, Bill courted Martha in the college library. He invited her to read and discuss—who else?—Ralph Borsodi. They began with Borsodi’s first book, Education and Living, continued with Flight from the City, and by the time they got to his most recent book, This Ugly Civilization, they were going steady. Later that year the duo invited Borsodi to visit Black Mountain College and give a talk. As Treichler recalled, the recent crop Of st dents, unlike their forebearers, were put off by what they perceives to be the isolation and drudgery of decentralism and rural living.¹⁵⁶

Another essential student-farmer was Ruth Aiko Asawa. Born in 1926 in Norwalk, California, Asawa came from a farming family. Because of California’s discriminatory laws against “aliens” including those of Asian descent, Asawa’s father, Umakichi, and mother, Haru, were unable to own land. Instead they leased a whom ping 85 acres spread across three parcels where they and their seven children grew vegetables and fruit, loaded it up on a truck. and sold it at markets in nearby Los Angeles. As a child, Asawa worked before and after school on the family farm, all year round. growing cabbage, cauliflower, and parsnips in the winter, carrots and strawberries in the spring, and melons and tomatoes in the summer. The work was grueling for Asawa and her siblings and only ceased on Saturdays, when they attended Japanese school and where Asawa learned calligraphy, and for a few hours on Sundays, when they went to the Quaker church, the only house of worship in the area that welcomed Japanese Americans.¹⁵⁷

With the bombing of Pearl Harbor in December 194], the Asawa family’s hard life grew harder. Within two months, without warning, FBI agents appeared at the farm and took Umakichi away to an undisclosed location. Asawa and her family would not see him for the next six years. Two months after her father had been arrested, Asawa, her mother and five of her siblings were told to report to the Santa Anita racetrack where they would live in horse stables for the next five months. Conditions were hideous: horsehair covered the walls; the smell of horse was constant and, especially on hot days, overbearing; their bath, toilet, and laundry were public.

Despite this Asawa blossomed as an artist, taking makeshift painting classes with fellow internees Tom Okamoto, Ben Tanaka, and Chris Ishii, artists and illustrators from Disney Studios. “He got us drawing,” she recalled about Okamoto, “and we posed for each other.” After five months the Asawas were transferred to Rohwer Relocation Center in Arkansas, where they supplemented their miserable meals with fresh vegetables planted near their “apartment” (Block 13–11-A) from seeds Haru saved from the farm. Through all of this, Asawa found solace in painting and drawing classes, and in leading a letter-writing campaign with her siblings and Edythe Byars, a Quaker from Norwalk, to request the release of her father.

Although the war wouldn’t end until 1945, the US government established prewar releases for internees who were high school graduates—if they signed a loyalty statement, enrolled in a Midwest college (the coasts being military zones), and secured a sponsor. Asawa graduated high school in 1943. Her brother George and sisters Lois and Chiyo left to attend William Penn College, a Quaker school in Iowa. Ruth selected Milwaukee State Teachers College. “It was the cheapest thing in the catalog,” she remembered. All of this was made possible by the American Friends Service Committee, which provided free room, board, and tuition for Asawa’s siblings and, with help from a Quaker named Ethel R. Potts, awarded Ruth a $100 scholarship.¹⁵⁸

In the summer of 1945, Asawa’s eldest sister planned a trip to Mexico City and invited Ruth to join her. Mexico was magical. When Asawa returned to Milwaukee she reconnected with Schmitt who could not stop talking about her summer experiences at BMC, especially the teaching of Josef Albers. In summer 1946, with a $200 loan from the Church of the Crossroads, a progressive Protestant congregation in Hawaii, and using funds from the National Japanese American Student Relocation Council, Asawa packed her belongings into a small trunk and took the train to Black Mountain. When it became clear that her savings would not cover costs, Dreier managed to secure funding for her tuition and fees.¹⁵⁹

Asawa, who usually dressed in casual clothes with straight black hair and blunt bangs, thrived at BMC. Her first assignment was to make butter, which she quickly mastered, followed by buttermilk, a drink much loved by Albers, Asawa’s favorite professor. Her butter and buttermilk operations became so ambitious that she often borrowed the school’s double-clutch, four-wheel-drive truck to haul larger amounts of milk from the farm to the kitchen. But it wasn’t all dairy-based glory. Asawa set tables, washed dishes, swept floors, and picked up trash. Once, during a particularly star-studded Summer Institute, she managed the laundry, checked out sheets and towels, and prepared rooms and beds for guest faculty including Elaine and Willem de Kooning, Buckminster Fuller, John Cage, and Merce Cunningham.

Never stopping, Asawa helped Molly raise the roof of the farmhouse, repaired broken furniture, mended mattresses, and designed and created the bell used in the college’s production of Erik Satie’s The Ruse of Medusa. On the farm she cleared brush, fertilized crops, harvested vegetables, and, according to Albers, “cultivated mad little gardens” across campus. One summer Asawa volunteered to be college barber to raise money for a proposed expansion of the farm’s milkhouse. Instead of charging for haircuts, she asked her clients to donate to the fund. By the end of summer Asawa had raised over $800. “She just kept that place going,” remarked Buckminster Fuller.¹⁶⁰

Asawa’s most notable work at Black Mountain College was one that eventually garnered her international attention. In summer 1947 she returned to Mexico, this time with a group of young Quaker women, sponsored by the American Friends Service Committee. They traveled to Toluca, a town outside Mexico City, where Asawa taught children to draw. The summer was filled with adventures, including multiple visits from Josef and Anni Albers, who drove Asawa around to see Rivera murals. But the most influential event was when she witnessed villagers in Toluca looping wire baskets designed to hold eggs. Asawa spent much of her free time learning the technique from a local craftsman. When she returned to Black Mountain College she immediately started making the baskets, first to transport eggs from farm to kitchen and later from campus to the farmers’ market in Asheville. One day, according to fellow student Mervin Lane, Asawa enclosed the basket entirely, transforming the utilitarian form into an elegant wire sculpture. On a whim she brought it to the Asheville farmers’ market and three people wanted to buy it. Asawa continued making looped-wire baskets for the remainder of that year and for the majority of the rest of her life.¹⁶¹

TEACHING AND TENSIONS

After spending fall semester getting familiar with their new home, Moles and Trayer developed Black Mountain College’s first farm-related course in spring 1947. Although teaching had occurred on the farm from the start, including Bas Allen’s pruning lessons and student-led workshops, the lessons were always informal and separate from the college’s official curriculum. This changed with Moles and Trayer’s Getting Acquainted with Agriculture (universally referred to as Farming), a hybrid class, part seminar, part practice, which offered students “first-hand knowledge and experience of the operations of a small general farm.” Students read books like Plowman’s Folly and J. I. Rodale’s Pay Dirt: Farming and Gardening with Composts. These and current farming topics were discussed with a passionate Trayer and a more reluctant Moles during once-a-week evening seminars. Each student was expected to maintain a farm project, like milking cows, raising rabbits, starting a beehive, or repairing the tractor. The class attracted a modest seven students. Two of them, Verna Raattama and Katherine “Sue” Schauffler, excelled. The rest flailed. One student, Richard “Dick” Sherman, worked hard on the farm but “never worked up a tremendous enthusiasm for the course.” Another, Howard Rondthaler, son of faculty member Theodore Rondthaler, participated regularly in the seminar, but never once set foot on the farm.¹⁶²

The seminar exposed a tension between the farmers. Moles found Trayer’s knowledge of the issues at hand to be intellectual but inexperienced and found his professorial tone pompous. He was also turned off by Trayer’s ambitious nature. For example, in spring 1947 when the college found itself short on faculty and lacking a quorum, the board of faculty invited Moles and Trayer to join. Trayer, perhaps seeing it as a way to advance, accepted immediately. Moles mulled it over. It was not that Moles was detached from the college. He taught, ate meals in the dining room, and attended college parties with his family. Further, he was often relied on in general meetings as a de-escalating mediator when things got hot. But Moles did not consider himself a teacher, much less a college professor. After a couple of weeks of consternation, Moles reluctantly accepted.¹⁶³

Discord also rose from the farmers’ dissimilar farm experience. While Moles was born and raised on a farm and had managed farms throughout his conscientious objector service, Trayer was new to it, a farming student to be more accurate. “He didn’t have any previous farming experience,” Shirley Moles recalled. “He had read about it and wanted to do it.” Oftentimes, Moles would suggest a routine task to Trayer and instead of asking questions, Trayer would flounder and, feeling insecure, get upset. Despite this and to Moles’s great frustration, Trayer had stealthily assumed the role of farm manager to Moles’ assistant farm manager. He participated more actively with the faculty, which it seems had led some members of the community to consider him more knowledgeable. Acrimony further flared, no doubt, with both farmers and their families living under one roof.¹⁶⁴

By spring 1948, perhaps as a way to cool tempers, the farmers decided to teach two separate classes. Moles taught Farming, while Trayer developed a new class called Soil and Steel. The aggrandized course summary gives weight to Moles’s accusation of Trayer as high-minded. Soil and Steel was “a study of the farmer and the industrial worker; nature of the social and economic setting; physical and psychological wants and needs of both groups; background of the labor movement; major problems of labor; modern agricultural patterns; the interdependence of the farmer and wage earner; techniques toward unity. Two hours weekly.” The highlight of the course was a two-week-long field trip to visit various government dams built across North Carolina and Tennessee. Despite initial student interest—25 students signed up—the class tanked. Only one student, Albert Lanier, managed to complete a final research paper. Trayer’s brutal assessment of the students did not help the situation. He accused them of limited intelligence, poor analytical skills, and over-emotionalism. One unfortunate student, Wilma Fuerstenberg was said by Trayer to do “little more, seemingly, than occupy space.”¹⁶⁵

By summer 1948 patience had worn thin. That summer, neighboring cattle trespassed through subpar fencing to munch on the college’s pastures. Trayer was furious. Aggressively, he rounded up the offending bovine, hauled them home, and yelled threats at the next-door farmer. The second time the cattle invaded, Moles took a different approach. With plenty of calm, he and a student collected the cows and herded them home. While still at the neighbor’s, Moles struck up a conversation with the farmer, compared crops, and discussed possible fixes to the fence.¹⁶⁶

When Trayer learned about Moles’s non-confrontational tactics, he grew furious. In no uncertain terms, Trayer demanded that Moles tell him the next time foreign cattle made their way on campus. “I don’t care if it’s my weekend off,” Trayer hollered, “if those cattle get out on our land again, I want to know!” The cattle did, Moles didn’t, and Trayer fired Moles. Calmly Moles put down his tools, walked to the farmhouse, and asked his wife, “How soon can you be ready to go?” Without hesitation, Shirley replied, “Well, by tomorrow.” The next day the Moleses left the college to return to farm in Shenandoah, leaving an underqualified Trayer in sole charge of the farm at Black Mountain College.¹⁶⁷

FEUDING CAMPUS

While the college was no stranger to protracted power struggles, the biggest of them all began in summer 1948 and peaked a year later. On one side was Theodore “Ted” Rondthaler (history, college treasurer from 1945–48), Albert William “Bill” Levi (social sciences, current rector), and M. C. Richards (English, married to but not in lockstep with Levi). It is difficult to think of a more different BMC couple than M. C. and Levi. Levi was intellectual, erudite, and pedantic. He taught sociology at Dartmouth College and the University of Chicago before coming, with M. C., both in their late twenties, to Black Mountain College to teach philosophy in 1945. As a teacher, Levi, bald, serious, and a bit chilly, was formal, especially for informal BMC, and, as Duberman wrote, “tended to lecture and, even off the platform, to pontificate—to hand down truths.” As a person he was cagey. Few faculty trusted him. As rector, which he took over from Dreier in spring 1947, his focus was on building up the humanities and social sciences, fields that Albers had been hostile toward, widening the rift between the two.¹⁶⁸

While M. C. held many similar beliefs, especially regarding the primacy of the humanities and social sciences within the curriculum, she carried herself in an altogether different way. Young, warm, attractive, and friendly, M. C., now considered one of the shining lights of Black Mountain College, came from the University of Chicago and University of California, Berkeley, in search of a less standardized pedagogy. She taught a half dozen classes, all categorized under “Reading” or “Writing,” and ran them the way John Andrew Rice had done (minus the bullying). In class, students and professor would spend hours getting to the bottom of a text as well as their individual beliefs. “M. C. wanted you to find yourself,” said poetry student Joel Oppenheimer. She embraced the communal spirit of the college and quickly became, with a few exceptions, one of BMC’s favorite teachers and members of the community. Beyond the classroom, M. C. and a reluctant Levi often hosted students at their cottage where they would read poetry, discuss literature, and feast on M. C.’s homemade blackberry pie.¹⁶⁹

[Légende] Albert William “Bill” Levi, professor of social sciences, and Mary Caroline “M. C.” Richards, professor of English, walk from the Studies Building to the Dining Hall.

On the other side, once again, were Albers and Dreier. Tired of new professors offering ideas on how to run the college and, in Albers’ words, “this constant over-democratic nonsense,” he and Dreier proposed hiring new faculty on a one-year visiting basis, with neither voting rights nor admittance to the Board of Fellows. Levi, M. C., and Rondthaler were appalled. What’s more, perhaps feeling underappreciated for their contributions to the college over the past decade and a half, Albers and Dreier proposed changing faculty salaries from need-based to seniority-based, which would benefit them, the college’s most senior faculty. Levi et al. were aghast. In a move that would have shocked Rice in his founding vow to avoid administrative strangleholds, Albers and Dreier proposed decreasing the power of the faculty, increasing the power of the rector, and creating an outside board of trustees. Levi, M. C., and Rondthaler were horrified.¹⁷⁰

Sadly, students were not immune to the power grab. First, Albers sought to change the nomination process for student representatives; nominations were to come from faculty instead of the students themselves. Second, Albers demanded eliminating the practice of students teaching courses. A year earlier while he and Anni were on sabbatical in Mexico, the college allowed three advanced students—Harry Holl (sculpture), Arthur Penn (acting), and Jimmie Tite (printing)—to develop courses. By the time Albers had returned and fall semester was underway, Holl had repurposed an old tool shed into a forging and sculpture shed, created an introductory sculpture studio class, and was currently teaching eight students.¹⁷¹

Albers was livid. His fury was aimed at Holl in particular, who, a year earlier, had dropped Albers’ course after only a few sessions. One day, Albers tracked Holl down on campus and demanded he stop teaching. The class, explained Albers, should have been approved by him, the head of art. Holl thought it over and replied, “I am going to go on with the course.”

Stunned, Albers eventually recovered, smiled, and invited Holl to tea at his faculty cottage. Over tea and pumpernickel, brought in from New York, the professor told the student the reasons why he should not, could not, and would not teach this class. Holl, older than most students, having spent three years in the service, was neither intimidated nor impressed. “I’m teaching the class.”

    Albers, “a little shaky and angry,” said he wouldn’t allow it. Holl, who was on the GI Bill, which funded returning

servicepeople in education, explained that if he must stop teaching the course, then he would need to report to Veteran Affairs in Asheville that his recently approved student-teacher status had to be changed to full-time student status; thereby disrupting his year-long course of study, which meant the college would need to return Holl’s GI funds, and that would trigger an automatic reevaluation of Black Mountain College’s accreditation as a GI Bill institution. GI Bill money was steady income for the college and the older professor knew it. Checkmated, Albers seethed and proceeded to ignore Holl throughout the remainder of the student’s stay at the college.¹⁷²

Predictably, faculty meetings were toxic. Camps formed and conversations quickly descended into name calling. Old schoolers Albers and Dreier labeled newer faculty M. C. and Levi as transients, unable or unwilling to appreciate the college’s history. In turn, new guard M. C. and Levi called Albers and Dreier stayers, accusing them of clinging to the past to retain power. To some, Albers and Dreier represented European standards, while Idaho-born and UC Berkeley-trained M. C. became a symbol of modern degeneracy, or what one BMC faculty called, “romantic anarchy.” Albers was called a fascist; M. C. was labeled a communist. “Meetings had become so stressful,” recalled Holl, “that some faculty members took powerful tranquilizers—a rather new thing—prior to the faculty meetings.”¹⁷³

[Légende] Student-teacher Harry Holl’s Sculpture class, including, left to right, students Dorothy Albers, Stanley Hebel, Mirande “Randy” Geissbuhler, Sheila Oline, Marion Rothman, Zucha Dickson, and Gregory Masurovsky, spring 1948.

For Levi and M. C. the final straw was a particularly nasty Board of Fellows meeting near the beginning of summer 1948. For perhaps the first time in its history, Black Mountain College had some remaining funds that needed to be spent. Albers demanded the money go toward an ambitious set of fences—neat, angular, white—built across campus. Rector Levi argued for one or two new pianos. Discussion, argument, vote: pianos win. Levi called for a short recess, during which he, M. C., and others left the room for some fresh air. Student-moderator Charles “Chick” Perrow recalled what happened next: Albers, who was second in command, reconvened the meeting with Levi and M. C. still out of the room, and amid cries of foul, led a new vote. By the time Levi and M. C. had returned, the Board of Fellows had voted to use the funds for fences. From there, things moved quickly. Levi and M. C. were granted a leave of absence. Levi resigned as rector. To no one’s excitement, Dreier took over as interim rector. By summer’s end Albers, despite his disdain for administration, agreed to serve as rector and loudly transmitted that a dramatic reorganization of Black Mountain College was in store.¹⁷⁴

Low on faculty due to leaves and sabbaticals, Albers invited Trayer, and new instructor Charlotte “Bimbus” Schlesinger (music harmony and composition) to serve on the board to ensure a quorum could be reached. Fall semester’s list of course offerings was so slim, especially in non-art classes, that Albers asked Trayer to develop a new course. The Farm in Society explored “the farmer’s pivotal position in our society with a view to gaining knowledge of his problems, conflicts, and role.”¹⁷⁵

Having launched fall semester, Albers turned to the reorganization. First, he shared with faculty and funders his vision to transform the liberal arts college into an arts school, Black Mountain College for the Arts, where the specific focus would be arts and music—no more history or math—supplemented by introductory courses in the humanities and social sciences taught by visiting (i.e. no voting privileges) faculty. Second, in mid-December he invited a handful of allies, including photography scholar Beaumont Newhall; John Burchard, the first Dean of MIT’s School of Humanities and Social Science; and former BMC student Alex Reed, a surrogate son of sorts to the Alberses, to Black Mountain College to join a select committee (comprised of allies Dreier and Schlesinger) to discuss the formation of a board of trustees. A second meeting to this end took place in mid-January 1949, this time in New York City.¹⁷⁶

Back on campus, Trayer convened a meeting of his own. This time, Dreier was under fire. Four other Board of Fellows members—Erwin Bodky, Goldowski, Frank Rice, and Nell Rice—joined Trayer in lambasting Dreier, bringing into serious question the longstanding faculty member’s fitness. Although Dreier had always raised eyebrows for his barreling nature, idealization of physical labor, and chaotic reasoning, this attack was different. It was vicious. Librarian Rice was furious with Dreier for his support of hiring new faculty without voting privileges. Goldowski was livid at Dreier for his recent public shaming of a student couple who were caught spending the night together, and his attempt to expel the female student. Trayer’s list of grievances was long. He railed against Dreier’s habit of visiting the farm with a list of “urgent” tasks only to change the list the next day. He complained about Dreier’s impulsive decisions, like when he bought a litter of piglets for the farm with no consideration of where they would be housed, how they would be fed, and who would care for them. He fulminated against Dreier’s support of an outside board of trustees, and reintroduced a stale rumor that Dreier was trying to sell the college to the University of North Carolina. Worse, Trayer concluded, Dreier was having an open affair with a faculty member, bringing shame to both Bobbie and the college and bringing pain to him personally as a Quaker. By the end of the meeting the five members, constituting a bare quorum, voted to demand that Dreier resign from Black Mountain College.¹⁷⁷

When Dreier returned from a trip to New York in late January 1949, Nell Rice, acting as spokesperson for the Board of Fellows, informed him that they had decided he must resign from the college. Dreier was stunned. He asked Nell for the decision in writing and walked off in a daze. Never a popular teacher, Dreier was presented with a petition later that day signed by more than half of the students—perhaps seizing the moment—asking him to resign as a teacher of mathematics. That night Dreier made his way to the barn, no doubt looking for peace. There, “right into the midst of the herd of beef cattle in the jet black night,” he wrote in his unpublished memoir, “I just stood there among them for half to three quarters of an hour, leaning against them, resting.” Here, Dreier found calm. “They did not answer back.”¹⁷⁸

[Légende] Students Francine du Plessix and Joel Oppenheimer pose mischievously at Black Mountain College, 1952.

[Légende] Student Joel Oppenheimer, dubbed by professor Paul Goodman as “the wild bull of campus.”

Dreier agreed to resign on the proviso that the college, as a means to ensure steadiness, hire as an administrator N. O. Pittenger, retired treasurer at Swarthmore and a Quaker. Although Pittenger was interested, he had some questions about how things were run, delaying his appointment—and Dreier’s resignation—for three months. Sensing a power vacuum, the remaining faculty met to determine how to continue spring semester, how to salvage the forthcoming Summer Institute, and whether the college would even exist come the fall. When no one showed any interest in assuming the role of rector, Trayer agreed to the interim job, making him the only farmer to serve as rector of Black Mountain College.¹⁷⁹

By April Pittenger took over, Dreier immediately resigned, and the Dreiers left Black Mountain College within days. Out of protest against what they perceived to be unfair treatment of Dreier, a stampede of resignations followed. First, Anni and Josef Albers (he also resigned as rector), then Trude Guermonprez (weaving), and Schlesinger—basically the college’s art department. Walter Gropius resigned from the advisory council.¹⁸⁰ But the storm that had lasted nearly two years and swept away a good chunk of the faculty wasn’t over just yet.

FACULTY MEETING FIGHT

During his three-month stint as interim rector, Trayer kept a few balls in the air, including helping visiting faculty member Buckminster Fuller organize the forthcoming Summer Institute, corresponding with more than 50 students who had signed up for the Institute, and retaining poet-pedagogue Charles Olson, who would become a key BMC figure in the coming years.¹⁸¹

Over the 1949–50 school year, within a climate of suspicion and indecision, the college faculty and other leaders did their best to keep heads above water. Without Josef Albers to protest, the college hired two new social science professors, Paul Leser (anthropology) and Flola Shepard (linguistics); three advanced students (Hazel Larsen Archer, Joseph Fiore, and Pete Jennerjahn) to teach visual arts; and one student (Betty Jennerjahn) to teach dance. The college also hired a handful of Quakers, including Robert Turner to begin a ceramics program, John McCandless to run the printshop, Vollmer Hetherington to teach music appreciation, Louise Hetherington to teach piano, and Don Warrington to serve as treasurer. Following a train accident, Pittenger resigned as rector, and was replaced by Levi.¹⁸²

Although the college was functional, the atmosphere was far from amicable, and soon another intra-faculty battle began to brew. While the battlelines were fluid, they roughly fell into two camps: the Quakers argued for a more conventional and conservative college, while Levi, M. C., and most of the students advocated for a more bohemian one, in line with the college’s past. This rift was especially apparent when the faculty discussed visiting professor Paul Goodman.¹⁸³

BMC was still attracting acclaimed names, and Goodman arrived at Black Mountain College as part of a diverse group of visiting professors that included sculptor Leo Amino, critic Clement Greenberg, dancer Katherine Litz, and artist Theodoros Stamos. A writer, social critic, psychoanalyst, and, in the decade that followed, guru to the counterculture, Goodman was hired to teach writing but brought so much more. “The guy was loaded with guns,” student Fielding “Fee” Dawson wrote much later. “He came down with psychotherapy (his own), literature, history, community planning, and sex.” While few questioned his teaching skills, some members of the community were disturbed (or threatened) by his open bisexuality. Goodman’s flirtations didn’t help. As Mary Emma Harris wrote, he was “aggressive in his pursuit of young men, and students who had been pursued by Goodman were tired of having to deal constantly with his advances.”¹⁸⁴

When Goodman requested to stay longer to teach, a hiring process kicked in that included a series of meetings. At one, Trayer, acting as the Quakers’ spokesperson, expressed fear that Goodman would sexually assault the faculty’s children. “Why does everyone think Paul wants your 13-year-old boy,” a meeting attendee asked sarcastically, “when there are all these groovy 20-year-olds around?” Later, Trayer accused Goodman of public indecency when, during a campus softball game, he urinated behind a tree in the outfield rather than walk a quarter mile to the bathroom. Joel Oppenheimer, a poetry student from New York, retorted: “Everyone has peed behind that tree!”¹⁸⁵

Four years after his arrival, pacifist Trayer had become hostile and violent, leading many to doubt his emotional stability. One student, Edward Stanley “Stan” Vanderbeek, had a particularly disturbing relationship with Trayer. Initially Vanderbeek was eager to work with the antiwar farmer. “I was very seriously involved in [being a] conscientious objector and the belief in it, and all those struggles you do for moral value:” But Trayer disappointed Vanderbeek with his violent behavior. He would “beat up a cow any chance he had. I mean he was just furious at them; if they disobeyed him, he was just an absolute militarist.” Another student, Harry Weitzer, recalled a day on the farm when Trayer was bitten by Pearl, the horse. “Ray was furious,” wrote Weitzer. “He pulled that horse’s head down and took hold of her ear in his own mouth. The horse stood still as Lot’s wife. When the battle was clearly over, Ray straightened up and spit a neat semicircle of ear.”¹⁸⁶

The pros and cons of hiring Paul Goodman continued to be heatedly debated, culminating in a Board of Fellows meeting held in the hot and sticky classroom of the Studies Building late in summer. While most of these meetings were attended by the board’s six or seven members, for this one about 15 members of the BMC community sat in chairs around the large wooden table that Molly Gregory had built, eager to see and hear what went down. After a half hour of discussion, anti-Goodmanite Trayer turned up the heat by shouting at pro-Goodmanite Levi something to the effect of, “The college cannot afford another scandal!” To which Levi replied, “The college cannot afford a farm that keeps losing money!” In a flash, Trayer leaped out of his seat, lunged across the table, and started slugging Levi. Trayer “just trashed him,” recalled Vanderbeek. “He was a big, strong guy and he just beat up this poor philosophy teacher right in sight of everybody.”¹⁸⁷

From the other side of the room Oppenheimer, who was a Goodman supporter, sprung out of his chair, onto the table, and then pounced onto the back of Trayer. As he wrestled the farmer to the ground, Oppenheimer screamed, “You fucking bastard! You fucking bastard!” While roughly half of the room was horrified, the other half was bloodthirsty. “Ja, Joel, Ja!” yelled bookbinding instructor Johanna Jalowetz. “You tell him! You tell him!” For his heroics, Goodman dubbed Oppenheimer “the wild bull of the campus.”¹⁸⁸

The next day Trayer slunk away from campus, while wife Dottie remained behind. “Because he was a Quaker,” writing student Mark Hedden recalled, “I think he was sort of demoralized.” Having left the farm at BMC with his tail between his legs, six months later Trayer had taken on the role of farm manager of Earlham College, a Quaker school in Indiana.

CHAPTER 8

“NO MORE OF THIS COMMUNITY HORSESHIT!”

[Légende] Charles Olson, poet, pedagogue, reluctant rector, and hater of Work Program, circa 1951.

When Ted Dreier was ousted from Black Mountain College in 1949, SO Was any remaining sense of financial stability. From the college’s inception, Dreier, with all his shortcomings, had served masterfully as treasurer, bookkeeper, and chief fundraiser. As the latter, he endlessly tapped into his and wife Bobbie’s network of wealthy family and friends to fund initiatives and stave off foreclosure. When he left, the funders left too. So did nearly all the financial support from Stephen Forbes, a student at the college from 1935–38 and again from 1940–42 and one of the college’s biggest donors. Making matters worse, beginning in the early 1950s, student tuition, the college’s main stream of revenue, often went uncollected. Each year a small horde of non-paying students, alumni, and Summer Institute participants simply squatted at the college. “They would move into a room and attend classes and eat in the dining room,” recalled Daniel Rhodes, a visiting pottery faculty during the summer of 1953. Soon the freeloaders made the paying students feel like suckers and many of them up and left, depriving the college of additional tuition. Carolyn Dunn recalled that her husband Joe Dunn and their mutual friend John Wieners, both writing students, arrived at the college with a work scholarship but neither of them ever worked nor paid tuition. “I don’t think anyone paid any tuition,” recalled Betty Baker, an administrative secretary. “The students weren’t contributing financially to the college. It was just a terrible situation.” The school that never seemed to have any money suddenly had even less.¹⁸⁹

In fall 1953, Black Mountain College had about 35 students; a year later, there were 20; by fall 1955, it was down to seven or eight. With so few students, it barely felt like a college. “The spirit was lacking,” noted Baker, “because there weren’t enough people there to have spirit.” Further, the college had no sustained program for recruiting new students. By now poet Charles Olson had firmly established himself as the campus leader and with few to no senior faculty left to guide, took over what remained of student recruitment. For a while, Connie Olson served as registrar but when the Olsons’ marriage collapsed, Connie left the college and student Eloise Mixon took over. Mixon was shocked to find a desk full of unopened letters, many a year or two old, from students inquiring about admission.¹⁹⁰

The only thing that saved the college, temporarily, was the GI Bill. As an unaccredited college, the first attempt to secure GI Bill eligibility failed. They tried again in 1945, and their proposal was approved four days before the fall semester began. The impact was immediate. By spring 1946, enrollment soared to 75 students and the next year it increased to 90 students, over half of whom were male.¹⁹¹

While the college benefitted from the GI Bill throughout the mid-to-late 1940s, by 1953 BMC’s GI status was precarious and Olson, who managed the GI Bill funds for the college, was summoned multiple times to Raleigh to explain the curriculum and clarify the college’s untraditional teaching practices to the Veteran’s Administration (VA), which ran the initiative. The VA wanted vets to take particular classes in particular sequences during particular times. Olson, however, often taught during his “optimum hours,” which were between one and four in the morning and usually ended class when he felt the class needed to end. Rober: “Bob” Creeley, professor of poetry, recalled one of Olson’s classes lasting “from seven or eight in the evening until one o’clock the next afternoon.” Miraculously, each time the VA summoned Olson to Raleigh he managed to convince them of the college’s eligibility. With the renewal secured, seven student vets were able to attend Black Mountain College, bringing sorely needed income. “Most of the oil painters were there on the GI Bill,” recalled Carolyn Dunn, “so their tuition, paid by the government, was enough to squeeze out a few dollars for us hangers-on.”¹⁹²

With revenue evaporated, the Work Program eroded. Perhaps this was due to the departures of Dreier, the head cheerleader for the program, and Albers, indefatigable keeper of campus roads and walkways. Maybe it was the result of the ineffectiveness of Leonard “Len” Billing, a former BMC student who returned during the 1951–52 school year to direct the Work Program. Perhaps it was the college’s failure to replace the irreplaceable Bas Allen. Subsequent handyman Ben Sneed was more often drunk than sober on the job. After that, Mr. Merrit was an adequate handyman until a horrific acid-related accident rendered him bandaged up on a rocking chair all day, too injured to work, too poor to quit. Regardless of the reasons why, few students and fewer professors showed up for their weekly shifts. Some students resented the program altogether and refused to participate. Art student John Chamberlain, for example, “loathed the community scene,” recalled Creeley, calling it “some great banal boy scout scene.”¹⁹³

Once in a while, a meeting was called to drum up participation. “Come on, get out there with your scythe!” Baker remembered faculty imploring. “So you’re bright, you still have to have some physical exercise!” Besides not wanting to work, few even knew how to do the work required—the “each one, teach one” pedagogical practice that had thrived at the college throughout the 1930s and 40s was gone. When it came to basic campus maintenance, “no one knew how to do it,” recalled Mixon, and “no one was the least bit interested in doing it.”¹⁹⁴

[Légende] Math student Trueman MacHenry takes a break from farm duties, Black Mountain College, 1951.

[Légende] Students Joan Heller and Edward Stanley “Stan” Vanderbeek bring in the hay.

On a rare chance that a work crew was assembled, the results were comical. Once, Olson managed to put together a half dozen students to work a Saturday afternoon on the farm. Shortly after they had started, however, Olson and art student Dan Rice got into a heated debate that quickly escalated into a nasty argument. “Enough!” Rice announced. He turned to the crew and offered to buy all of them, minus Olson, a beer. In sync the students dropped their tools, about-faced, and started walking the four miles to Ma Peak’s Tavern, a bar located outside the town limits of Black Mountain. Abandoned, Olson yelled, “a fine lot of fish!” to the refreshment-seeking deserters.¹⁹⁵

Evidence of the Work Program’s collapse was everywhere. The lawns and fields that Treichler devotedly plowed and the roads and pathways Albers and student crews diligently maintained were nowhere to be found. Weeds were neck high. When a rare visitor showed up, they had to be literally guided through campus. “Grass and shrubs had not been cut for months,” recalled Robert Moore, a local high school student in 1954, “so we followed the students as they wound their way along paths through the trees and deep grass.” Helene Dorn, who accompanied her husband Ed, a writing student, and their young children to BMC, observed, “It was a weird sight and easy to understand how large settlements can be buried alive by the jungle.”¹⁹⁶

Campus buildings were unkempt and battered. The Studies Building, Black Mountain College’s greatest architectural achievement, was a disaster. The foundations were shaky, the coalbin had caved in, and broken windows were replaced with cardboard and tape. Poet Robert Duncan, who taught at the college in spring 1956, remembered the structure as “a derelict piece of modernism,” adding, “nothing looks more run-down than an art moderne building ten years later.” Inside, students stacked their trash—drafts of papers, uneaten food, cigarette butts—in mounds that grew 6 ft tall. When the mounds took over their studies, the students simply moved to a new study. The library, once a jewel, was shot—its front door no longer closed, its books were moldy and often pilfered. When faculty and students left, “they just stole the damned books,” recalled secretary Baker, “just walked away with them, volumes, collector’s items and art books from Europe, you know, magnificent things.”¹⁹⁷

Other buildings were closed or abandoned. The pot shop was padlocked. The woodshop was leased to student Don Mixon, who seldom used it and never paid rent. The printshop was barely functional and “leaves littered the type cases,” wrote Olson biographer Tom Clark. One faculty cottage was relinquished as it slowly slid off its foundations and down the mountain. The glass-and-metal Science Building was abandoned. Duncan recalled finding “deserted laboratories with broken glass, and splendid kiln equipment which had just gone to ruin.” Kudzu, a fast-growing vine, grew up, around, and over North and South Lodges and all the faculty cottages. The farmhouse was vacant. In the Round House, a once “fantastic collection of records” was scattered across the floor; some unsleeved LPs rested on tables through the hot summer until they melted, Dali-esque, down the edges. By 1955, “Black Mountain was only about ideas,” wrote student Martha King. “Almost everything else had been abandoned, lost, broken, fallen in.”¹⁹⁸

While the plant eroded, campus services became practically non-existent. Gone were the days when earnest, hardworking crews loaded coal from the train station in Black Mountain and hauled it back to campus. Instead, a small heap of damp, soft coal was occasionally delivered to the doorsteps of some faculty cottages. Lumber was sometimes cut, rarely delivered, and never ample. Mildred Harding, a nearly finished graduate student in English at Columbia University, wife to anthropology professor John Adams, and mother of a baby and toddler, recalled a horrific winter during the 1951–52 school year. “I remember 2 a.m. bottle feedings at just above zero indoors, huddling in layers of blankets, the baby in my arms, buried in there, only his nostrils and lips visible.” Although meals were still served, they peaked at the beginning of the month, when cooks Cornelia Williams and Malrey Few worked within their tiny monthly budget. As the month went on and the budget dwindled, the meals worsened.¹⁹⁹

Even laundry services disappeared. During summer 1953, a German museum director visited Black Mountain College for a few days. “After he left,” Rhodes remembered, “it was discovered that no one had put sheets on his bed or given him towels, so he had just been sleeping on a dirty old mattress in his room. It wasn’t anybody’s business. And that’s the way it went.” By 1955, the college’s last remaining washing machine, the one that had been faltering for years, finally collapsed. Above the machine, someone posted a sign that read: FUCKT.²⁰⁰

OLSON’S UNIVERSITY

Like so many others, Charles Olson first learned about Black Mountain College by reading Louis Adamic’s 1936 article in Harper’s. The college’s rebellious roots and Rice’s pursuit of a new kind of college deeply resonated with him. As Tom Clark wrote, Olson was especially impressed with “Black Mountain’s downplaying of traditional educational ‘solidities’ (like buildings, credits, grades) in favor of intangible spiritual and intellectual values.” The college’s agrarianism and communalism, however, repelled Olson. When he read Adamic’s account of the student-run farm, he recalled saying to himself, “Oh shit!”²⁰¹

[Légende] Art student Dan Rice and writing professor Robert “Bob” Creeley under the Studies Building at Black Mountain College, 1955.

In fall 1948, BMC hired writer Edward Dahlberg to replace M. C. Richards, who, along with husband Levi, took a year-long leave of absence. Within two weeks, Dahlberg, frustrated with the college’s foliage, students, and uncivility, angrily left and suggested Olson as a replacement. Olson was living in Washington, DC, but agreed to teach intensive seminars on writing and literature, commuting to the college for a week every month throughout the 1948–49 school year. In summer 1951, looking for some sense of home for himself and his pregnant, common-law wife Constance “Connie” Olson, Olson returned to the college where he remained, more or less, until it closed in 1956, and through the next year to sell off anything worth something, pay off debts, and close accounts. His classes were legendary, his intellectual scope—especially in terms of history, mythology, literature, and poetry—was extraordinary, and he quickly gained followers. By all accounts, Olson was huge—in size (approximately 6 ft 8 in. and around 280 Ib), presence, confidence, and ego. “Charles Olson!” wrote Harding, “he seemed, even then, at 42, half mythic: bard, guru, titan.”²⁰²

Although Olson showed up in the classroom, he was a constant no-show when it came to the Work Program. He resented being expected to participate. As a student of the college’s history, Olson was well aware that many of BMC’s fights and flights, or what he called, “rump revolts and jumps away,” were centered on issues of community and physical labor. His perspective on this type of involvement was unequivocal; “No more of this community horseshit!” Olson wrote in 1953.²⁰³

Initiated under Levi’s rectorship and institutionalized during Olson’s reign, contingency salaries for faculty were intended to help the college remain afloat. These were essentially 1OUs. Faculty were paid half the amount of their previously agreed upon salary with a promise that the other half would be paid once the college was more financially sound. Later, as things got more desperate, faculty were offered food from the farm, which by this point was being run by Doyle Jones, a local farmer who had taken over where the Trayers had left off. Faculty would get part of a pig or a basket of beans in place of an IOU. With their hunger increasing and expectation to be paid decreasing, most faculty reluctantly accepted the food. Each year, these contingency salaries accrued and accumulated and their fate was ultimately settled in court.²⁰⁴

SAVING THE COLLEGE

While the college was surely crumbling, it was not collapsing. Classes continued, meals were served, and modest buildings were (slowly) built. This was due in part to farmer Doyle Jones.

A “lean, handsome man with the bluest eyes,” as described by writing student Michael Rumaker, Jones had spent most of his life farming the Black Mountain region and specialized in raising livestock, especially hogs and beef cattle. He also had building skills he had picked up through brief stints at Essex Chair Company and the Morgan Manufacturing Company, where he worked alongside Ross Penley, Black Mountain College’s first farmer. In early June 1951, right after the Trayers left the college, Doyle was hired as the new farmer. The job gave him use of the fully furnished farmhouse and access to a large garden plot, maintained mostly by Doyle’s wife, Claire, or “Clara,” a member of the Cherokee Nation, and their 14-year-old son, Ben.²⁰⁵

[Légende] Doyle Jones, Black Mountain College’s last farmer, spring 1952.

Doyle arrived to high expectations. After Trayer’s quick retreat, his wife Dottie had run the farm. When that didn’t work, the college temporarily hired art student Vanderbeek, who “hardly knew one end of the cow from another.” By comparison Doyle knew what he was doing with the land. He also served on college committees, procured moonshine for students, and drove folks to Asheville for errands. Doyle took control of the dairy herd, trained writing and art student Terence Burns to assist him, and began providing the college with all its milk, eggs, and butter, once again. He regularly slaughtered members of the beef herd until one year later, they were no more. While Doyle was experienced with livestock, he was less adroit when it came to growing vegetables, so although food came from the farm, it didn’t exactly flow. With little income to count on and a reliance on food-in-kind, Harding recalled: “As winter settled in and less and less food trickled from farm to dining room—for one long stretch, almost nothing but milk, eggs, and red beans—I pictured my children withered and deformed with beriberi and scurvy.”²⁰⁶

But Doyle’s underwhelming food production was arguably impressive given the lack of resources. In the past, the farm had a budget to purchase needed equipment, repair old tools, and initiate new projects. Now, Doyle had such a small budget he couldn’t afford fertilizer. In the past, the farm had an agricultural program, which introduced basic farming skills to the wider BMC community. Now, Doyle had none of that. In the past, the college had a functional Work Program, a generally agreed-upon collection of practices where all members of the college worked with staff in service to the campus and farm. Now, Doyle had, if lucky, a handful of students show up on the farm on Saturday afternoons. In the past, expert community partners had been on hand to help. Now, the only community partners Doyle had were his farming friends and drinking buddies, who sometimes came to campus to help harvest silage but often spent their time taunting students.²⁰⁷

One such instance involved writing student Michael Rumaker. In late 1951, Rumaker was living precariously in Philadelphia, having recently been kicked out of his family home for being queer. By chance, he and a friend attended a lively talk at the Philadelphia Museum of Art by painter Ben Shahn who spoke rapturously about an unconventional place called Black Mountain College and its writing teacher Charles Olson. Intrigued, Rumaker visited Mary Reed, who taught painting at the Moore Institute of Art, and asked her thoughts. “Oh, yes, Black Mountain,” Reed answered, “I hear it’s a hotbed of communism and homosexuals.” Immediately Rumaker wrote a letter of inquiry to the college, noting his poverty, and quickly received a reply from Connie Olson, then BMC registrar, who touted the college’s work scholarships and invited him to visit. Rumaker visited in the summer of 1952, was smitten, and arrived as a student in fall 1952.²⁰⁸

Upon arrival Rumaker joined a crew assisting Doyle to complete the last stage of a weeks-long process of turning corn into silage. Although Rumaker didn’t mind the intensive work nor the oppressive heat, he felt demoralized by the rude snickering from the local farmers who called the students “city slickers” and mocked them—especially Rumaker—as unmasculine, with Doyle adding his own two cents. The next day, Rumaker requested a transfer to the kitchen team.²⁰⁹

While Doyle fed the college, Paul Williams funded it. Born in Chicago in 1925, Paul Francis Williams Jr was raised by mother, Gretchen, a smart, independent farm girl, and father, Paul, an electrical engineer and inventor who amassed a huge fortune through his company, G & W Electric Specialty Co. From kindergarten through high school Paul Jr attended the prestigious North Shore Country Day School, where he enjoyed woodworking and student government and took classes from former BMC student Nat French and future BMC history professor David Corkran. As Mark Davenport, a music historian at Regis College, wrote, “despite coming from a family of wealth—or perhaps as a reaction against it—Williams developed a disdain for pretension in all its forms.” Following high school, he enlisted in the military and spent two years on a B-29 bomber. After completing his service Williams, was accepted to Black Mountain College, arriving in January 1946.²¹⁰

[Légende] Paul Williams, initially a BMC student of architecture, then architect in residence, and lastly Black Mountain College’s final funder, circa 1952–53.

Williams, like many other BMC students, yearned for architecture classes. With no architect-in-residence since Kocher’s departure three years prior, Williams took summer classes with Walter Gropius and later with Buckminster Fuller. Wanting more, in the fall of 1947 Williams recruited a group of students to join a weekly architecture seminar. With modest funding ($1,000) from his mother, he and the seminar students designed and built the Minimum House, a modern 400 sq ft home made of aluminum siding, wood, glass, and rocks gathered from campus. Constructed to prove that good design didn’t have to be pricey, the Minimum House’s south-facing side was made primarily of glass, taking advantage of solar heat and offering a dramatic view across a meandering stream and into a sumptuous glen of dogwood trees. It was completed in summer 1948 and its first occupants were Williams and his wife, BMC student Vera Baker.²¹¹

For the next few years Williams bounced between Black Mountain and Boston, where he studied architecture at Harvard. Along with the college’s first potter, Robert Turner, Williams designed a concrete and wood pot shop, inaugurating an exciting era of pottery at the college led by Turner, potter Karen Karnes and her husband, potter and sculptor David Weinrib, and M. C.²¹²

In early 1952 Williams’s father died, leaving his son a nearly $1 million inheritance. Williams decided to donate or loan much of it to people and projects involved in art, architecture, and community building. This included Black Mountain College, to which he loaned $5,000 to build up a beef herd of 50 heifers, $5,000 for scholarship fees, and $5,000 for operational expenses. Williams also gave $5,000 to John Cage, whom he had met at the college, to continue his work with David Tudor on the Project for Magnetic Tape, which began in New York and came with Cage when he taught at Black Mountain College in the summer of 1952.²¹³

Williams’s biggest gift to Black Mountain College was also his last. In early 1953, he offered the college a $25,000 loan, to be paid at $5,000 a year, interest-free. Sweetening an already sweet deal, payment was not to begin until 1 September 1956. In return, the college would offer the farm as collateral against the loan. Dreier’s wartime decision to build up the farm as a strategic capital investment was proving prescient. Once the deal was signed, the college, which had been sitting at the precipice of ruin, crawled along.²¹⁴

In June 1953, Olson, perhaps in a bid to secure future funding, persuaded Williams to return to Black Mountain College, this time to teach a course in architecture and serve as architect-in-residence. Williams accepted and he, Vera, and their two young children traveled to Black Mountain. They were shocked. As Davenport wrote, “They quickly became alarmed with the school’s state of disarray, the small number of students, and the lack of administrative leadership.” No one, it seemed to Williams, was in charge. Before the end of the 1953–54 school year, Williams and his family left the college to establish an artists’ coop, or intentional community, called the Gate Hill Cooperative with former Black Mountain College faculty and students including Cage, Karnes, M. C., experimental pianist and composer David Tudor, Vanderbeek, and David Weinrib.²¹⁵

While Doyle and Williams worked heroically to save Black Mountain College, the real hero was the land itself. Although the farm was nowhere near peak production, by 1953 it was still providing the college with over half of the vegetables and fruit BMCers ate and all of their meat, eggs, and butter. Lucky for the current inhabitants, they were able to benefit from nearly two decades’ worth of labor.

Black Mountain College began living off its land in a different manner—namely, by selling it. Throughout the early 1950s, the college enacted a series of entrepreneurial failures, each one worse than the last. First, they sold off 70 acres of campus to a local gravel company, who, as math student Trueman MacHenry recalled, “engaged in scooping the heart out of the entire valley.” Upon selling the parcel, the college learned they would have received the same amount of money by merely leasing the land. Next, students Carl Kent and Ron Robertson, under Doyle’s supervision, chopped and trimmed pine trees from a stand near the farm hoping to sell the lumber to local mills. Using only rudimentary axes they managed to produce less than expected lumber and much more than expected exhaustion. When additional funds produced two chainsaws, the venture seemed viable. But soon one of the chainsaws broke, and Kent and Robertson decided to leave the college and return to New York, “stronger and healthier and also sadder because we could sense we were seeing the last of a most significant and wonderful learning community.” After that, writing student Fee Dawson and painting student Jack Rice decided the college could profit mightily by growing tobacco. With help from Doyle, they planted acre after acre of the stuff. Next, they built a tobacco barn. But these entrepreneurial students were inexperienced and built the barn out of pine, a difficult wood to use in this capacity, meaning construction lasted months longer than expected. Although the tobacco crop grew strong, it was harvested too early; and while the college eventually brought it to market, tobacco prices were at their lowest. Overall, more money was lost than made.²¹⁶

[Légende] Unidentified campus guest (left) and art student Robert Rauschenberg (in white sweater), working at the campus saw mill, 1952.

THE LAST THANKSGIVING

At the break of dawn on 25 November 1954, Terence Burns, student and recently appointed interim farm manager, woke, rose, and left his digs at two-story stone cottage Meadows Inn for early morning milking duties on the farm. Within minutes housemate Michael Rumaker rose, looked in on Terence’s room, winced at the color of the sheets (“the shade of a good strong brew of coffee”), and tactfully closed the bedroom door, hoping to minimize the “barnyard odor” that came from his housemate’s quarters. Rumaker woke up their third housemate, Tom Field, an impeccable painter, whe dashed out of bed, fired up his large Coleman stove, put two huge Dutch ovens on it, and excitedly placed one large, plump turkey into each. For the rest of the day a “gut-gurgling aroma” emanatet out of Meadows Inn and into the nostrils of nearly every remainize member of Black Mountain College, which at the time numbereé no more than 15. It was Thanksgiving.²¹⁷

Throughout the morning Rumaker and Tom cleaned, scrubbed, and polished their rooms and the common room. They even washed the windows. During the afternoon Tom tended to the turkeys and prepared multiple side dishes. Meanwhile, Rumaker borrowed chairs and tables from around campus and sez the cozy common room for a dozen dinner guests. He borrowed goblets from Mary Fiore, a writing student married to painting professor Joseph “Joe” Fiore, foraged herbs from the farmhouse garden, and gathered enough wood to keep Meadows Inn’s huge fieldstone fireplace roaring all night.

When guests arrived in early evening, they were offered a goblet of red wine and led to a table filled with hors d’oeuvres, some of them prepared by guests, most made by Tom. Charles Olson stayed near, next to, blocking, posting up in, or eating from the hors d’oeuvres table all night long. At some point, Tom carried in the last hors d’oeuvres, a tray of hot chili peppers, one of his favorite dishes Immediately, Hilda Morley Wolpe leaped out of her chair.

Hilda first learned about Black Mountain College through the Eighth Street Club, an informal gathering space for painters, sculptors, and thinkers in New York City, where former BMCers like Cage, the de Koonings, and abstract expressionist painter Franz Kline charmed her and husband Stefan Wolpe, an avant-garde composer and music teacher, with stories of the college. Despite initial enthusiasm, Hilda’s experience at the college had been problematic. On the one hand, she loved the natural beauty (“the quiet, the space, the sound of birds”) and enjoyed teaching classes in metaphysical poetry and James Joyce. On the other hand, the college’s strain of male chauvinism, attributable to Olson’s presence, was tiresome. “I remember the discomfort I felt in most of my contacts with ‘Olson’s boys,” she wrote, “and even at the way they stared at me on my way to and from the dining hall, as they sat in a group on the wall with expressions on their faces which seemed to say that I was a questionable character because I’d never been seen in any of Charles’s classes.” Tonight, however, she felt part of the scene and excitedly took a hot chili pepper.²¹⁸

Tom turned next to Stefan Wolpe and offered him a pepper. He had arrived with Hilda in June 1952 when the college had around 50 students. The Wolpes thought the move would be temporary and “decided to stay as long as our money lasts.” They remained at Black Mountain College through the 1952–53 school year, when the student population dwindled to 35, through 1953–54, with 20 students, all the way to spring 1956, when the college hosted about ten students. Although he had few students for his music classes, Wolpe loved the college’s everything-goes atmosphere, spent days reading deeply from the library, and was engaged with the administrative decision-making. “I very much like hot chili peppers, yes?” said Wolpe, in his strong German accent, to Tom, waving off a pepper. “But in the mawning they burn my asshole.”²¹⁹

As the wine flowed and things got loose, Wolpe was on a roll. He shared a story from his past, when he was “a young, impoverished but dandified student in 1920s Berlin.” He had been sleeping with multiple women, none of whom knew about the others. Until they did. As a response, “they each took turns shitting in a shoebox,” he explained, and “wrapped and tied it up nicely with ribbon to look like a gift.” The women placed the box outside the dirty door of his dingy apartment. When he saw the waiting gift young Wolpe was excited, then horrified, then, upon reading the enclosed card signed by all the women, embarrassed. Game’s up, he thought. Older Wolpe thought the story was hilarious, as did Hilda, as did all the Thanksgiving dinner guests, and more wine was poured,²²⁰

[Légende] Stefan Wolpe, avant-garde composer and faculty of music, Black Mountain College, 1952–56.

When everyone finally sat down on borrowed chairs and scooted themselves toward the borrowed tables in the lobby of Meadows Inn, they feasted on Tom’s roasted turkey and creamed onions. It must have been the best meal that most of them had eaten in the last year. For dessert there were multiple fruit pies, baked by Tom and Mary Fiore, with fruit foraged from the all-but-forgotten campus orchards. Mary also baked a batch of her beloved butter cookies, the ones she had always made in the past when the college community came together for teatime, a longstanding tradition now long gone. “The last carefree, festive gathering at the college that I remember,” Rumaker wrote, “where everyone felt high-spirited and congenial.”²²¹

It was a lovely evening, made lovelier by its uncommonness. By late 1954, Black Mountain College was a precarious place—financially, functionally, spatially. To stay afloat, the college realized they had to either lease or sell not just the land, as they had started doing, but the campus itself. They entertained two or three offers and eventually settled with George Pickering, a local businessman who wanted to start a religious summer camp for boys. When the college signed a lease-to-purchase agreement with Pickering, they received $7,000 up front, with more to be paid in annual installments. In return, Black Mountain College gave Pickering full use of the lower campus, which included the North and South Lodges, the largely student dormitories, the Round House, the Quiet House—a meditative space and later a queer hook-up spot—the softball field, and, perhaps most importantly, the kitchen and dining hall.²²²

Communal meals had been a steadfast tradition at the college since its inception, which would no longer be possible without a dining hall. But even more significant was the loss of the BMC cooks. Born and raised in nearby Swannanoa, Malrey Few joined Black Mountain College’s kitchen staff in 1942 and became its full-time cook in 1944. Since assuming that role, Malrey fed the college grits and eggs, French toast with cinnamon, and piles of pancakes for breakfast, soups and salads for lunch, and roasted chicken and farm-fresh potatoes for dinner. She participated in community meetings, taught students and an occasional faculty member how to cook, and together with Rumaker danced the night away in summer 1953 at the college’s Black Arts Ball.²²³

The other cook, Cornelia Williams, was born in South Carolina, traveled to Asheville in 1927, and loved the weather so much she never left. For years she worked as an assistant dietician at the Highland Hospital until she heard about an opening for a “salad and dessert girl” at Black Mountain College. Cornelia, who took music classes at the college and participated in school concerts, was known for many delicacies, including apple pie, fried potatoes, and super strong coffee. She worked closely with students, who adored her and would volunteer to do extra work if Cornelia made special desserts like Brown Betty. “They were very fond of meat and sweets,” she recalled. She also made homemade peach brandy, which would get offered to county inspectors by Malrey while Cornelia raced to make sure the kitchen was up to code. Cornelia’s favorite pastime was music and she took music classes with Edward Lowinsky, spent much of her free time practicing piano, and sang lead in “Tennessee Waltz” at a college concert.²²⁴

By all accounts, Thanksgiving 1954 was a smashing success. The food was terrific, the wine flowed, and the spirits were high. “There was real good fellowship in our Thanksgiving celebration,” wrote Hilda, despite not always feeling comfortable at the college. As nice as it was, it must have also felt, consciously and unconsciously, unbearably sad. Tom’s delicious dinner must have reminded attendees of Malrey’s roasted chicken. Mary Fiore’s butter cookies must have made them nostalgic for teatime. The entire evening must have reminded them of communal meals, until recently an utterly routine experience. As much as they enjoyed themselves that evening, they must have known nothing like this would happen again. Late at night, after all the guests left Meadows Inn, Rumaker washed the dishes in a reflective mood. “Thanksgiving 1954 was the last gathering with the sense of the old convivial, self-sustaining Black Mountain spirit,” he thought, “where we made magic out of next to nothing.”²²⁵

WINTER CANCELLED

The steady collapse of the college became increasingly dangerous as winter approached. Lake Eden had originally been built as a summer resort. Although the college had tried to winterize the student lodges and faculty cabins by adding furnaces and insulating the walls, halls, and ceilings, lack of resources limited these efforts. There were a few attempts to re-establish crews to chop wood for fuel, but by then both chainsaws were broken and few members of the community had any skill or desire to do so. On one Saturday in early December, a half dozen students and faculty gallantly hopped on the college’s flat-bed truck and drove to one of their remaining oak groves. They worked all day but returned dejected with a paltry stack of wood. Wolpe took most of the logs to feed the furnace in the downstairs unit of two-story Black Dwarf, a faculty cottage he shared with wife Hilda and with students who lived upstairs. The furnace quickly ate up the day’s work and provided only a few hours of mediocre heat for the two. Unfortunately, it had no effect whatsoever on the shivering students above.²²⁶

[Légende] Merce Cunningham and dancers, Black Mountain College, summer 1953. From left to right, Viola Farmer, Merce Cunningham, Carolyn Brown, Paul Taylor, Remy Charlip, and Debbie Moskowitz. “At Black Mountain,” Carolyn Brown wrote, “We were fed good plain fare from the college’s farm.”

With no heat, it was finally decided to suspend all winter classes. Defeated, the faculty assembled the remaining students to share the news that they would have to vacate campus from Christmas to mid-March. They also encouraged the students to learn a new skill, make some money, or both. Stunned, the students nevertheless made do: art student Jerry van de Wiele departed for Chicago where he continued painting; Tom relocated to Chicago to work first at a restaurant and then in an advertising studio; Rumaker moved to New York City, where he landed a job at IBM World Trade Corporation, an amazing feat for a self-described “scruffy-haired writer from radical ‘commie-homo’ Black Mountain.” In mid-March the students returned—some with new skills, a few with new savings, and all feeling much less cold.²²⁷

COLLAPSE

It was summer 1954 and Elizabeth “Betty” Baker felt restless. She had been working in Raleigh for years as an assistant editor at the North Carolina Historical Review and had gained significant experience in news and publicity. But she wanted more. She often reminisced about her time, over a decade earlier, at the Woman’s College of the University of North Carolina (now UNC Greensboro). She missed the special seminars, which always attracted a handful of students from Black Mountain College. Baker loved everything about the BMC group—their rigorous thinking, original ideas, and creative works, not to mention their hip-without-trying style. “My tongue would hang out,” she recalled. Most of all Baker was moved by the students’ tales of communal living, including how they had purchased Lake Eden, started a new farm, and built the Studies Building.²²⁸

On a whim in July 1954, Baker sent her resume to Black Mountain College with a note that read, “There must be something I can do.” Olson responded immediately by hitchhiking to Raleigh to recruit Baker. Her job, he explained, would be “to help save the college.” More specifically, she would create “publicity and propaganda in order to get money in.” Before accepting the job, Baker visited the college with her curious mother and conservative father. They showed up on a Sunday, Baker remembered, “and were met by Olson who had a great big patch in the rear of his pants, you know. His shorts were showing through one side of them.” A tour revealed a campus in ruins. Meals were neither offered nor witnessed. “They were dying financially,” Baker recalled. Her mother was enchanted, her father horrified, and Baker took the job.²²⁹

Arriving in August, Baker dove right in. She spearheaded grant and gift proposals to the Guggenheim Museum, Duke Foundation, Mellon Foundation, and the John Hay Whitney Foundation, all of which were rejected. She worked tirelessly with the Council of Southern Universities and an informal consortium of small liberal arts colleges on various grants, but nothing materialized. Her biggest success was landing an article about Black Mountain College in the New York Times, which resulted in a few visits from prospective students. Unfortunately, upon seeing the state of the campus, they immediately fled.²³⁰

When it came to fundraising, the faculty fared no better than Baker. Olson took multiple trips to New York, Washington, DC, Boston, and Chicago looking, without success, for new donors. Loan applications to the Bank of New York, Farmers Federation Bank, and Northwestern Bank of Black Mountain were rejected. The college established an Advisory Council, which included such notables as Albert Einstein and William Carlos Williams, that boosted credibility but did nothing to boost credit. A faculty committee comprised of Wolpe and Robert Hellman, a writer who taught languages, was established to explore ways to develop the campus lake into a financial asset. A week later committee members reported back with precisely zero ideas. A week after that the committee collapsed. Olson wrote a passionate letter to alumni in hopes of raising $25,000. It yielded $250. For weeks Wolpe worked with Robert Creeley, a young American poet who had been living with his wife and children in Mallorca, Spain, publishing The Black Mountain Review with guidance and blessings from Olson, and eventually invited by Olson to teach at BMC, to write personal letters to Wolpe’s expansive network of artists and musicians begging for money. They received nothing. “Our actual progress,” recalled Creeley, “was one long slow plunge into despair.”²³¹

Within four months Baker had had enough. By fall 1954, the budget was so bleak that faculty and staff, having received “contingency salaries” since 1951, no longer drew any salary at all. Instead, they were given free lodging, a food allotment of $27 a month, and permission to forage from the farm. “The financial thing,” Baker recalled, “I just couldn’t swing anymore. I’d used up all my savings.” Worse was the emotional toll. Black Mountain College had become desperate and depressing. The community spirit that mesmerized Baker over a decade ago was nowhere to be seen. Instead, the new reality was “parasitic,” with students and faculty taking what they could get without contributing back.²³²

In early December Baker submitted her letter of resignation. The Board of Fellows rejected it. She submitted a second one. “Detailed reasons for this decision are known, to the point of extreme boredom, by the majority of the Faculty-Board members,” Baker wrote. “Suffice it to say that I feel incompatible with Black Mountain today, and with those few plans I have heard for Black Mountain tomorrow.” When Baker departed, so did the last vestiges of fundraising at Black Mountain College.²³³

THE FALL OF THE FARM

By fall 1954, farmer Doyle was fixing to leave Black Mountain College. He felt betrayed by the college’s use of contingency salaries, by which he received half of his salary (a measly $40) and an IOU promising the other half when the college achieved financial stability. He felt disrespected when the college offered to pay him some of the IOU with food, including meat he raised and vegetables he grew. He felt underappreciated, like the day when Doyle slaughtered the farm’s remaining chickens, delivered them to campus, and watched as no faculty were willing to pluck them for dinner. Barbara Abrams, a student from New York, tried to pluck one but instead threw up all over it. Eventually, Mary Fiore, who was born in Ohio and arrived to the college on GI funding, stepped up and plucked them all. When the campus community offered toasts to celebrate the student who plucked the chickens, not the farmer who raised them, Doyle felt sour. “The farmer felt he was doing all the work, and the faculty was just sitting there eating his stuff,” recalled Baker, “which it was.”²³⁴

Making matters worse was Doyle’s relationship with Olson. During the previous spring, Doyle and Olson were friendly. The farmer invited the professor and his students to the farmhouse to watch the Army-McCarthy hearings on Doyle’s television, the only set on campus and the surrounding region. As a sign of appreciation, Olson and his students washed the windows of the farmhouse. After that, however, something changed. Maybe it was because Doyle did most of the farm work and Olson did none. Perhaps it was their differing sizes—Olson, a giant, “King of the Mountain,” Doyle, diminutive, “a short, leathery little man.” Whatever it was, their “blood hatred” for one another climaxed in November 1954. Fee Dawson witnessed it all: “I saw them pass in the hallway that led from the kitchen to the back door of the dining hall; words (animal) were exchanged; I followed Olson outside as he strode, mind just able to control body, electrified with rage, and with nowhere to go sat on the steps of North Lodge. Dan Rice was there. Olson on the very brink opened and closed empty hands (around Doyle’s neck), body shuddered, voice broke, snarled ‘I can’t FIGHT that little bastard!” Shortly after that, Doyle had had enough. In mid-November 1954, Doyle, “angry and sensing the end,” packed up his family and belongings, including his television, and left Black Mountain College for good.²³⁵

The temporary solution was to put student Terence Burns in charge of the farm. Raised in Providence, Rhode Island, Burns had attended Rhode Island School of Design for three months before becoming disillusioned and dropping out. After finding a brochure about Black Mountain College at a local bookstore, he joined the 1952 summer session for two weeks. Mesmerized by Cunningham and his dancers and Cage’s nightly lectures on Zen Buddhism, Burns was hooked. For the next three years he studied writing with Olson and painting with Joe Fiore. In place of tuition he worked on the farm. “I was the milk boy,” Burns recalled. “I used to milk a dozen milk cows twice a day.”²³⁶

FOOD CRISIS

When Black Mountain College leased its kitchen and dining room and laid off its cooks, its members were responsible for procuring food. Then when Doyle left and the farm collapsed, a full-blown food crisis ensued. One by one, members of the college became increasingly hungry, unhealthy, and desperate. As Creeley recalled, “It was a very meager scene.” For an entire quarter, student Barbara Abrams ate cinnamon toast for breakfast, lunch, and dinner; Carolyn Dunn heard of students surviving off “hot-dog soup,” prepared by adding milk from the farm and herbs from the garden to the water in which the hot dogs were boiled; and for a while, writing student John Wieners remembered being “penniless, living on crackers and secondhand tea bags.” During the summer of 1955, Wieners made an inventory of his food items that he hoped would last him for one month; they included: “I quarter box of pancakes, 1 box of Hominy grits, % can of corn, 2 frankforts, and 20 crackers.” Indeed, “people just sort of subsisted,” recalled Clemens Kalischer, a photographer who visited campus in summer 1954, “I don’t know on what.”²³⁷

On top of all of this, the remaining members of Black Mountain College had to feed Olson. As Tom Clark noted, when Connie left Olson, “a sort of round-robin standing invitation system was established, with the honors of hosting the hungry but helpless rector shared around the college community.” Olson spent many nights mooching meals from Wesley “Wes” Huss, theater professor and college treasurer, and his wife Beatrice “Bea” Huss, as well as from Robert and Helen Hellman, especially around pay day, when the two couples had enough money to buy decent ingredients. “He appreciated my French dinners,” recalled Helen, but was often a distracted conversationalist, “almost as if I/we were out of his great orbit.” Sometimes he ate with Wolpe and Morley in their cottage. This came to an end one night when Olson found a trace of a Brillo pad in his paella, complained bitterly, and stomped out. When faculty were out of food, Olson would seek out students, including the Mixons. Eloise remembered one night in winter when Olson, dressed in a half dozen layers of trousers and shirts, each of them littered with rips and tears, appeared. He strolled into their cottage, pulled off his extra layers of clothes, planted himself next to the fire and asked, “What have you got to eat?”²³⁸

The food crisis was met in multiple ways by the BMC community. They shopped smartly and bought eggs and milk from local farmers. They also stole food. This was nothing new. Members of Black Mountain College had been stealing food for years—including from the school itself. The college had to place locks on the kitchen’s two walk-in refrigerators due to constant pilfering. The theft of food was so common it turned into a sport called “poker” that, according to summer painting professor Esteban Vicente, included “the picking of the ice box locker to perhaps find something to eat—sometimes even a steak, or what passed for steak.” Although poker was frowned upon by all, it was rarely punished since one of the main players was none other than Olson. When the kitchen and dining room were leased, the college transferred its remaining meat from the campus walk-ins to a coop storage locker in Black Mountain. Within weeks, the locker was pokered and the last of the college’s meat was stolen.²³⁹

A much more ambitious operation took place at the A&P in Black Mountain. Remy Charlip, a founding member of the Merce Cunningham Dance Company, got things started when he first visited the college for Thanksgiving 1951. A dancer, choreographer, artist, and costume and set designer, Remy was also an expert thief. His main tool was an oversized ankle-length army surplus coat that, as described by fellow dancer Carolyn Brown, “sported custom-made pockets that opened into cavernous inner linings ready to receive cartloads of produce from local grocery stores.” Remy, who once lifted an 18 lb turkey from a crowded Manhattan grocery store, taught the craft to various BMCers.²⁴⁰

But the college’s champion stealer of food was student Dan Rice. Born in 1927, Rice excelled at trumpet at an early age and spent many of his high school nights playing gigs with such jazz greats as Benny Goodman, Woody Herman, and Artie Shaw. During World War II he served in the navy. When he returned, he enrolled at UC Berkeley and later transferred to Black Mountain College to study music and then painting. Dan was known to be a highly principled thief who followed three rules. First, he only stole food from the big chain stores, like A&P. Second, he never personally ate the food he stole. Third, he always stole “right under the nose of a clerk, or someone putting up food,” recalled Eloise Mixon, “to give them the opportunity to see him.” With staple goods like butter, cream, and anchovies, Rice, a gourmet cook, made delicious and hearty sauces and soups for everyone.²⁴¹

Black Mountain College students stole so much food from the A&P that store managers began to track them, especially when they wore heavy coats on hot days, and even moved some items to prevent further heists. “They stole so much instant coffee,” remembered Martha King, a writing student during the summer of 1955, “that management moved the instant coffee to behind the counter where they grind the fresh coffee, so you had to ask somebody for it.” Fortunately for the college, they had an ally on the inside. A young worker—“A mountain girl, local girl,” Martha recalled, “long hair hanging down”—tacitly supported the students’ endeavors by looking the other way. Student Basil King, who was taught how to steal steaks by fellow student Eric Weinberger, once brazenly approached the friendly cashier with an 8 lb ham in plain sight. “Had it under my arm and I never said a word,” Basil recalled, “and that girl let me through.”²⁴²

The BMC community also foraged for food on the farm, which continued to produce a meager amount of edibles after Doyle had left, including perennial vegetables like broccoli, spinach, and sweet potatoes. Others foraged seasonal herbs from the farmhouse’s kitchen garden and fruit from the orchards, which had become overgrown. Students and faculty also received milk from the remaining dairy cows. Some members occasionally lent a hand to the campus community garden, newly established by Bea Huss, and left with a meal’s worth of carrots, onions, and potatoes. Finally, some students foraged in campus forests and mountainsides for wild berries, nuts, and edible flowers.

Perhaps most effectively, BMCers formed small food co-ops. Each included three or four (mostly) students who pooled their money and cooking experience to produce regular meals. The members of the most impressive of these food co-ops were students Rumaker, Burns, and Tom Field, a true cook. Tom was born in 1930, to alcoholic parents who ran a bar in Fort Wayne, Indiana. He enjoyed painting from a young age and eventually took classes at Fort Wayne Art School, where he learned about Black Mountain College. In 1951, he was drafted to the Korean War and was trained as a cook, often in charge of meals for 2,000 sailors. Two years later, with GI funding in hand, Tom came to Black Mountain College where he studied painting with Joe Fiore. With help from Rumaker and Burns, Tom produced delicious, inexpensive soups and stews. He was also an expert shopper and would purchase soup bones and “pieces of meat that nobody else would buy.”²⁴³

When they were especially short on cash, Tom and Rumaker would go frog hunting in the marshy areas near the lake or behind the barn. In Black Mountain Days, Rumaker recalled Tom “squatting down, intensely looking, listening, himself becom[ing] some wild animal on the scent, then abruptly plunging his hand deep into the muck and each time hauling out, despite their winter’s fast, an enormous, plump-thighed bullfrog buried there, for frog legs for our supper.” Tom stabbed each frog in the brain, handed them to Rumaker, and together they hauled their dinner back to the student cottage they shared. Tom chopped off the frogs’ legs, dipped them in flour, eggs, and breadcrumbs, sprinkled them with fresh herbs Rumaker had gathered from the farm, and fried them in a big iron skillet over a portable gas stove. “I was a little leery, these being my first frog legs,” Rumaker wrote, “but they turned out to be as tasty, I suspected, as in any high-class French restaurant, and nourishing, too, a delicacy that didn’t cost us one red cent.”²⁴⁴

Wes Huss described the college during these times as “a community of subsistence dwellers.” One night in Black Dwarf, Wolpe and Morley finished their not-enough dinner and prepared to sleep. But above their heads, like so many other nights, Rice, Creeley, and Anthony “Tony” Landreau, who taught weaving, were partying away—drinking, singing, dancing, and rolling empty beer bottles across the floor. Wolpe had had enough. He raced outside and

BURYING A COW

One night in December 1954, Black Mountain College’s 38 Hereford cows disappeared. When acting farmer Terence Burns rose early to milk the dairy cows, he discovered that the entire beef herd was missing. While he was only in charge of the dozen dairy cows—which provided the college with milk, cream, and occasionally some subpar butter—the beef herd was under the domain of the Board of Fellows, led by Olson. Certainly Olson talked like he was in charge of the Herefords. “And here I was,” he was often heard saying after becoming rector, “left with the total creation of 600 acres and 13 buildings, and windows and cows and stuff, roadways and everything!” In letters and in conversations, he often referred to “my 38 Herefords.” Once, Olson boorishly compared himself to an enslaver. “I used to watch my herd of Herefords,” he remarked, “like a great plantation owner up there in the sun where we left them all spring, from spring to fall.” While possessing grand visions, Olson possessed neither skill nor interest in the cows.²⁴⁶

Burns urgently woke up the college and a groggy motley crew of professors—a patient Huss, a frantic Wolpe, a cranky Olson, and an amused Landreau—was deputized to collect the college’s cows. Some of the bovine escapees were lazy, made little ground, and were quickly rounded up. Others were more ambitious, traveled significant lengths, and were clearly a match for the wheezing faculty wranglers. “A sight to see, that was,” Landreau later recounted in “The Cow,” a short story he wrote about the incident, “running around out of breath, clumsy faculty members trying to chase cattle.” The worst were the ones who had been lured into nearby farms. When the panting professors asked their neighbors for their animals back, the farmers, already suspicious of the city slickers and their radical college, said, “What cows?” Only with intervention from a local farmer and BMC friend did the farmers reluctantly give back the escapees. By nightfall all of the college’s cows had been captured and shoved into the school’s barn. This time, the barn door was bolted shut.²⁴⁷

A few days later Burns decided to check in with the cows. He was aghast. Both the barn and the animals were a mess. The barn was covered in a blanket of manure, with some stalls 3 ft deep in it. The cows were hungry, swollen, and furious. The worst was found in a stall at the back of the barn. There, an unfortunate cow had slipped and drowned in its own shit. Not knowing what to do, Burns consulted with faculty, who had zero ideas. Eventually Burns called the friendly farmer, who called the local animal health inspector, who came to assess the situation, and ordered the college to dig a hole at least 4 ft deep to “bury the poor creature.” Perhaps feeling guilty for bringing the inspector’s rules into the rule-free college, the friendly farmer stuck around and offered to clean the barn.²⁴⁸

A team of nine was assembled to dig the internment. “It was precisely New Year’s morning, 1955,” wrote Landreau, “light snow falling on a small hillock behind the barn.” The ground was stony and frozen. They struggled. It took hours. The only saving grace was the jokes, mostly cow-related, told by the diggers, especially Olson who, characteristically, talked more than he dug. Finally, the inspector looked at the not-yet 4 ft deep hole and, taking pity, declared it “deep enough.” Using the college’s tractor, the friendly farmer unceremoniously hauled the dead cow from the barn into its grave and the nine diggers shoveled dirt upon it. Someone, probably Olson, offered a brief eulogy.²⁴⁹

Unsurprisingly, after this incident the college had had enough of the beef herd and organized an auction on campus. Two days later a half dozen local farmers, some bearing guns, scattered themselves around the periphery of the barnyard, “licking their chops over the prospects of skinning the city slicker.” Olson served as auctioneer. With his trademark Mexican heavy wool serape draped over his shoulders, he “enthroned himself in a wooden chair in the middle of the barnyard.” Helping Olson was Huss, who trafficked messages between the auctioneer and individual farmers, and Burns and Landreau, who, on Olson’s cue, trotted out this or that cow. Channeling his prodigious skill at debating, Olson sustained a bidding war between two farmers. Back and forth they went, egged on by Olson, resulting in a higher-than-expected final bid of $1,725.²⁵⁰

That night someone drove to Ma Peak’s to bring back a few six-packs of Ballentine beer—a massive luxury—and a raucous party ensued. At the center of the celebration was Olson, who energetically recounted the bidding war, blow by blow. The night “was an exhilarated triumph for Olson,” wrote Landreau, “at the top of his form.” At some point Olson announced that the final bid would allow the college to continue to operate through spring 1955, and a new round of drinks were distributed. But not all was festive. Late in the night Olson turned to Burns and asked, “Burns, where in the hell were you when we buried the cow?” Burns replied, “Where in the hell were you when I milked the cows?” Arguably the more important question—If we bought the beef herd for $5,000 and sold it for $1,700, why are we partying?—went unasked.²⁵¹

The widespread psychological despair that swept through the college during its final few years also played a part in its collapse. Weeks away from graduating, Rumaker attempted suicide; he accepted his diploma with bandaged wrists. Writing student Louis Marberry, son of a wealthy Louisiana family, drank so much green Créme de menthe his gums turned green. He jumped out of a second-story window and eventually committed himself to a mental hospital in Asheville. When student Tim LaFarge began to, in the words of Basil King, regularly “flip out,” his wealthy father flew down to place him into the mental institution in Asheville. Three weeks later, he took him out and returned LaFarge to the college. Anita Landreau, wife of Tony, suffered multiple nervous breakdowns, especially during her pregnancy, which, according to Betty Baker, was exacerbated by Tony spending more time at Ma Peak’s than with his wife.²⁵²

The despair affected faculty too. By all accounts Creeley was a mess. He spent much of his stay at Black Mountain College high on weed (brought to the college from New York by Dan Rice and from Mexico by John Chamberlain) or drunk at Ma Peak’s, commiserating with anyone who would listen about his collapsing marriage. “People started ducking him when they saw him coming,” Rumaker recalled, “not wanting to endure listening to yet another hour or so harangue of accusation and self-pity.” Oftentimes, student Cynthia Homire had to drive the college truck to Ma Peak’s to retrieve the poet and return him to campus to sit in his classroom: “We’re here to be taught!” Olson, trying unsuccessfully to manage between Connie and their child, Kate, and his new girlfriend, Black Mountain College student Elizabeth “Betty” Kaiser and their baby, Charles Peter, holed up in the Jalo House in what Creeley called an “inchoate state.” Days after writing professor Robert Hellman and wife Helen’s marriage dissolved, Robert survived an overdose from sleeping pills. The situation was so unstable that Jerry van de Wiele, who lived in Last Chance, the last cottage on the road to the farm, would hear certain students coming to visit and would quickly hide “the axe he used for cutting firewood in a safe place, out of sight and reach.”²⁵³

And then it was the night of the car crash, perhaps the most disturbing example of the college’s precarious collective mental state. On that hot summer night in 1955, art students Jorge Fick, Tom Field, and Dan Rice and professors Creeley and Hellman loaded into Tom’s 1950 Buick Roadster and headed to Ma Peak’s, Although none of the remaining BMC community members had any money, a GI check could fuel a fun night of drinking at the local watering hole. But this particular night wasn’t fun. The party, led by Creeley, needled Tom incessantly. Some recalled Creeley bullying Tom for being too passive, for his penchant to play the fool. Others remembered Rice accusing Tom of hiding his skill as a painter behind an act of incompetence. According to King, a friend of Tom’s, he was “a brilliant painter,” and also “Black Mountain’s village idiot,” chided for his “doughy body, drawling Midwestern speech, keeping his dawdling manner and androgynous persona among all the testosterone addicted zip gunners of Black Mountain College.” Quiet but kind, queer but closeted, Tom held, according to Ekbert Faas, “the lowest rung in the college’s merciless macho pecking order.” For over three hours and many rounds of ale, the others continued to bully Tom and wouldn’t let up as they drunkenly piled back into his Buick and drove home.²⁵⁴

Back on campus Tom stopped in front of Black Dwarf and Hellman barreled out of the Buick. As Rice made his way out of the car, he barked one last cutting insult. That was it. Without warning Tom stomped on the accelerator, making the tires spin, and forcing Rice—half in, half out—to dive back into the car. With fierce focus, Tom continued to accelerate faster and faster, straight toward Meadows Inn—his home. Rumaker was sitting on the steps outside the cottage, staring directly at the Buick and wondering why it was bounding toward him at such an alarming speed. Rumaker heard Tom yell: “If this is what you want, you’re getting it!” “Whatta ya doing, man?!” screamed Fick, riding shotgun, seeing the cottage approaching closer and closer, “Hey, Tom!” “Aw, Tom, come on!” shouted Dan Rice.“Tom!” yelled Creeley, “Tom!”

Rumaker dashed off the steps just as the Buick crashed full speed directly into Meadows Inn’s stone chimney. Rumaker recalled the sound as “a sickening punch of metal tangling against stone.” The Buick, totaled, was rammed up against the chimney “like a crushed accordion.” The cottage shook like crazy, then settled. Groans emanated from within the car.

[Légende] Meadows Inn, a faculty-turned-student cottage where the college’s last Thanksgiving took place and whose fieldstone chimney turned Tom Field’s Buick into an accordion.

Minutes later, the entire college community, which by 1955 numbered no more than 15, appeared and frantically helped to pull out the occupants. Miraculously Fick was OK, save for a small cut on his head. Creeley had a severely dislocated shoulder that would hurt for months. Rice flew from the back seat to the front, fractured a vertebra, wrenched his back, and had to wear a back brace for six months. Tom, who Rumaker found trapped under the steering wheel muttering to himself, “I told them to stop—I told them I’d do it—I told them,” broke his leg, dislocated his hip, and fractured some ribs. A week or so later the painter Ephraim Doner, an old friend of Olson’s, made a surprise visit to the college. It was grim, Doner reported back, and “everybody in the place was on crutches.”

GI CHARADES

In February 1956, a Veterans’ Administration representative paid a surprise visit to Black Mountain College. Like the year before, the college could not heat its occupants, so the decision was made to cancel winter classes and temporarily expel all residents. When the representative arrived, he found Huss and no one else—no students, no classes, no curriculum. The punishment was swift: the college would lose its GI eligibility at the end of March. Aware that the loss of this funding would mean the loss of BMC’s last revenue stream, Olson pleaded his case. Astonishingly, the VA agreed to send a representative to campus in mid-March to perform one final assessment.²⁵⁵

When students returned from winter break, the college immediately got to work. Because there was no curriculum and no classes that started and ended at a certain time, the remaining members of Black Mountain College performed one. They rehearsed a day of classes like it was a play. When the VA rep arrived, he attended Olson’s writing class with eight or nine students. When class was over, Olson escorted the rep to the next class, taking the longest possible route between two points. This gave students enough time to change clothes and receive haircuts before their next class, perhaps Huss’s drama class or Wolpe’s music class. And so the day went: The VA rep was ushered through winding pathways between neck-high weeds to attend a new class with a different professor who taught the same, albeit differently dressed and groomed, students. As the VA rep left campus at the day’s end, the remaining members of Black Mountain College celebrated their theatrical success, a celebration that lasted until a few days later when a letter arrived informing the college that their ineligibility was upheld. Game over.²⁵⁶

THE LAST DAYS OF

BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE

In summer 1956, as he was finishing his time in the army, Ebbe Borregaard began to consider college. Raised in East Islip, Long Island, by a creative family—his father was a vaudevillianturned-advertising man, his mother, a writer—Borregaard enjoyed writing in high school. While stationed at Fort Eustis Army Base in Newport News, Virginia, he wrote to Black Mountain College to inquire about admissions. Unlike so many other letters to the college, Borregaard’s was both opened and answered, by Eloise Mixon. She included the faux brochure Olson had created for the VA department. “It was a marvelous thing,” he recalled, “a list of courses and how they were supposed to work.” Borregaard packed his meager belongings, hitchhiked from Virginia to Black Mountain, and arrived at the college in late September 1956.²⁵⁷

Although fall classes had not yet begun, Borregaard got involved immediately with college activities. On his first full day he accompanied some students to town where they taught him how to steal food from the A&P. On his first night, he joined students and faculty at Ma Peak’s. He enjoyed meeting his peers, despite the constant hazing he received from two of them, Basil King and Eric Weinberger, who peppered Borregaard with questions to determine if he “was suitable enough for Black Mountain.” He participated in multiple conversations about theater and attended the college’s production of Medea, written and directed by Robert Duncan. Because the cast included nearly the entire college community, Borregaard was one of the few audience members. Compared to his years in the army, BMC, with its utter lack of rules and regulations, felt like freedom. “The place,” he remembered, “was such a mind-blower.”²⁵⁸

The place, Borregaard discovered, was also in ruins. “It was overgrown,” he recalled, “and it was falling apart.” He made his way to the library, which by then “had been ransacked,” and the Round House, where he shuffled through the mostly melted records. It was unclear to Borregaard where classes took place, where meals were served, and where he would sleep. Worse, across campus there was a stench, a smell of decomposition, “Everything smelled damp, like damp wood, and moldy.”²⁵⁹

On day five or six of Borregaard’s first—and only—week at Black Mountain College, he, along with all other students, faculty, and hangers-on, was summoned to Olson’s cottage. It was nighttime and Jalo House was lit with candles, since by then the electricity had been turned off due to unpaid bills. Tea, most likely made with recycled tea bags, was served. Eventually, Olson stood up and announced: “The college is closing.”²⁶⁰

Olson took in the shocked faces in the candlelit room. “We need students,” he explained, most likely to a silent room full of staring eyes. “We need paying students,” Olson clarified. The students remained silent. “We can’t pay ourselves,” Olson went on, adding with a poet’s precision: “We’ve got no money, no money’s coming in, nobody’ll send us money.” One student and then another offered to “write to grandma” to secure some money. With a wave of his hand Olson dismissed such suggestions. “The college is closing.”²⁶¹

Beer soon appeared and a low-key party began. Olson offered a few fuzzy details. In order to pay off debts and contingency salaries, the farm and upper campus would be sold. To prepare the campus for sale, Olson would remain to clean up the place (an offer that no doubt raised some unbelieving eyebrows). Huss shared his plans to move to San Francisco to join Duncan and others to expand Medea into a trilogy. Some discussed moving to New York; others talked about relocating to Chicago. At some point, Dita Frauenglass, who was married to writing student Harvey Frauenglass, took a photograph and then another one, showing the last remaining members of Black Mountain College, emaciated, drained, defeated. As it got late, one by one the students and faculty returned to their cottages. By night’s end, the college had closed.²⁶²

The following day or perhaps the day after that, Borregaard decided to join forces with the San Francisco crew. The idea was to drive to California where they would connect with Duncan, his partner and artist Jess, and BMC students and graduates like Paul Alexander, Tom Field, Basil King, and Michael Rumaker, as well as Beat figures like Allen Ginsberg and Jack Kerouac, whose work was featured in the seventh and final issue of The Black Mountain Review. Borregaard packed his meager belongings, threw them in the trunk, and jumped into the backseat of one of the cars heading west. As they departed, he turned around to see Olson, on all fours, frantically pulling weeds from the road that once led to Black Mountain College.²⁶³
