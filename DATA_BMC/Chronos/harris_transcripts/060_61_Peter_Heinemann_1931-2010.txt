MEH: Peter, you were at Black Mountain one year?
PH: I was in Black Mountain one year – September '48 to the end of May '49.
MEH: How did you come to be there?
PH: It was recommended to me by Jim and Frannie Hall, who had been there and
taught at a school that I went to called Windsor Mountain School.
MEH: They just had a reunion?
PH: They just had a reunion, and I went to that although I didn't go to the reunion at
Black Mountain the year before. But I did go to the high school reunion,
because I really wanted to see some of those people I went to high school with.
MEH: What type of school, just generally, was Windsor Mountain?
PH: It was very much like Black Mountain, only it was younger. It was run by two
German people who fled Germany when Hitler got there, or shortly there –
They had a school. They were both doctors. One was a doctor of Latin and the
other was a doctor of psychology. They started a school, tiny school, in New
Hampshire, and then it moved to Manchester, Vermont. It was Windsor, New
Hampshire, and then at Manchester, Vermont. Then they bought this estate in

MEH: About how many kids were at Windsor Mountain? How big was it?
PH: Well, it was about the same size as Black Mountain – about sixty – when I went
there. Then when I got to Black Mountain there was [SIC] about sixty students.

business in '75. Black Mountain didn't over-extend. I guess it just actually
petered out over a long period of time. I know that the population went from
sixty the year I was there to thirty the year subsequent, and then smaller and
smaller. I was surprised to hear that it went on to like '56.
MEH: Did you have any particular interest, when you went to Black Mountain?
PH: Yes, I did. I was always interested in painting, and I took only two courses from
Albers – Color and Drawing. I took Music Appreciation from a man named
Erwin Bodky. I sat in on Charles Olson's seminars, but I didn't take his course.

PH: My father paid five hundred dollars for the first year and then no one ever heard

MEH; Did you live in the area, or did you – ?

MEH: Okay, back to Black Mountain, what do you remember about Albers's classes?
PH: They were short and intense. They were two hours long. It was a two-hour – I
don't remember exactly whether there was a two-hour drawing class on a
Tuesday and a two-hour color class on a Thursday, or maybe they were butted
in together. They were very intense. I sensed that he was very secure in what
he knew. He had a body of knowledge, he taught it, and it was easy to think
about. I felt very positive. I was a figurative painter, and I don't think what I did
pleased him at all. But he pleased me. I liked him a lot. He liked sycophants
better, but I mean, you know, he's not the only one.
MEH: That's interesting, because a lot of the men students had real problems in
dealing with Albers.
PH: I had no problem. I really enjoyed him. I had no problem with him at all. It's just
that I was committed to doing something else. That's all. But I really liked the
information that he put out and the ideas. I thought about it, and I tried all the
things. It's just that I was – I had spent – When I went there, I was seventeen. I
had spent a year in New York trying to survive, at sixteen, which is pretty hard if
you don't have any support system. My mother had given us the five hundred
dollars. My mother lost a watch and got insurance for it. She said, "Well, if you
can go to college for five hundred dollars, I'll give you the five hundred dollars."

MEH: What do you remember about Olson?
PH: Olson was very big, a huge man. I think he was at least six-six. He might have

would travel and stop and see Pound in the pound, as it were, and then he
would come down – I mean it was quite amazing. Whether he was driving or
what, I don't know. He was fun to talk with. He was not – I knew a lot of the stuff

Wolfe. So, I knew all these things. I had them backwards and forwards. There
were a lot of things that I could talk with him about. The only problem I had with
him is that I hated his poetry, and I despised his reading of his poetry. I just
thought it was the most embarrassing glop I'd ever heard. I'm really surprised to
hear that it's such big stuff now, because it was really just – I just would cringe.
It seemed so unformed and self-involved. But he was a good guy. There were a
lot of good things about him. He was big and, you know, welcoming or
nurturing. He was a good guy. I just didn't like his poetry.
