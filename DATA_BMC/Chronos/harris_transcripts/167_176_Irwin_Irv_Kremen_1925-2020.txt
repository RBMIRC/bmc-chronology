MEH: You're from Chicago?
IK: Born and bred.
MEH: How did you hear about this small college in North Carolina?
IK: You mean Black Mountain College. I had left Chicago. I had been at
Northwestern University for three years but about that time had become
disenchanted with campus life—classes, textbooks, student politics, college
social life. But that disaffection was but a strand of a huge developmental
change that had begun to alter the tenor of my life. It was a turning point; I was
seeking another way to be in the world than that of my upbringing and its
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 2 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
circumstances. Had that turning point not already begun, I may not have
learned more about Black Mountain College nor gone there at all.
MEH: How then did you learn about it?
IK: I had come to New York and had started to take a couple of courses at the
New School. It was still more like Northwestern. My good friend Mort Nasatir
had heard about Black Mountain College and spoke highly of it as being
"progressive, experimental." What really lit me up was an article I chanced on
in a little magazine of the time, the Partisan Review, called a "Report from the
Academy" by Eric Russell Bentley. I had to go take a look. I hopped a train and
went down to Black Mountain, and it was really interesting.
MEH: Do you remember what you found interesting on that visit? What might have
grabbed you?
IK: Probably things tangential to the college. Look, I'm a Midwestern boy. I grew
up in Chicago. I lived near a park. I would go into the park. I would take the
branches, fashion them into bows, make arrows, go fantasy hunting in the
park. I was imbued with all kinds of images of an American past from my
reading as a boy—not only in the Far West but also in the movement across
the Appalachians into Tennessee and Kentucky, starting with Daniel Boone.
Things I would find in the library when I was a boy of circa—how old are you
when you're in the third grade? Eight? There was a certain series of books—I
would read about scouts in the great forests on the eastern side of the
Mississippi, from Kentucky north into Ohio country.
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 3 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
Well, coming down on the train to Black Mountain in the early
morning mist—I, of course, took a coach, and so I was up as the light began to
break—you could see these little log cabins and smoke coming up in the
mountains where Black Mountain is on Lake Eden. I could see myself there. I
could go to Black Mountain College! There were these roads and back roads,
wooden lodges and the log cabins. Good! Take me! I didn't care about
Gropius' building [A. Lawrence Kocher was the architect]. I went back to New
York and shortly afterwards to Chicago and convinced my family to at least
stake me to one semester there.
MEH: What was it l like to be there in 1945?
IK: The student body was quite small in the forties, just after the War, mostly
young women, only a few guys. Oh, yes, and this impressed me much. At that
time quite a large proportion of the students and the faculty were European,
refugees from the Nazis, most of Jewish origin. That that was so in North
Carolina struck me as amazing!
MEH: How did you spend your time at Black Mountain?
IK: I liked logging, I liked chasing cattle up on the ridge, and I even liked husking
corn. I didn't do that often, but it was good. This feels like I'm back—I'm not
simply a Chicagoan pounding concrete. The other thing I liked was that I could
go there and do what I wanted. Do what I wanted. Seriously. I did not know
that they kept records and grades. You'd have to sign up for courses, so I
signed up for courses. I never went to the courses. I signed up for Albers'
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 4 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
course and after a week I stopped going. I was horrified to find, when I was
applying to Harvard, that Charles Olson wrote me and said, "Do you want your
record sent to Harvard?" I said, "I don't know. Send me the record." So, he
sent me a copy. There was some damn grade from Albers like D-minus or
something like that. I went once or twice to the class. Albers was interested in
all the young women around him and how he was teaching them. He didn't
want to show me anything about drawing. I was just starting. What a waste,
because of all the people in the class I'd turn out to be the artist!
All right. I didn't go to the class. I had to sign up for a certain
number of classes. I didn't go to them. I went to M.C.'s poetry class because
there I was writing. I wasn't doing art. I was writing. So, I would write all week,
and I would put something into the poetry folder. We'd meet on Saturday
mornings out on the deck overlooking the lake—the deck of the Study
Building—and talk about what was in the folder. That class was fine.
But the next class I took with M.C. had a lasting influence upon
me. It wasn't because of M.C. It was because of what we did in that class. We
read Ulysses. There is no doubt in my mind that I would have read Ulysses,
whether I was in M.C.'s class or not, sooner or later. It happens this was the
context in which it began. I never got over that.
Later on in New York I read Finnegan's Wake with M.C. and
Barbara, my wife. That is how I courted Barbara. I met her at a concert at the
Cherry Lane Theatre. David Tudor played the Music of Changes by Cage and a
176IRWINKREMEN

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
work by Stefan Wolpe. Barbara was a friend of Wolpe; I was a friend of Cage. I
took her home from the concert. Once I say her library, I developed this plan.
“M.C., let’s read Finnegan’s Wake,” I said. “I’ll get the pretty one to join us.”
(LAUGHS) So, we started reading at my apartment one Friday night, and then
we began to read every Saturday for the next year-and-a-half.” Finnegan’s
Wake out loud. It was marvelous to read it out loud that way, and that’s how I
courted Barbara.
MEH: About what year was this? When you were reading Finnegan's Wake, when
you were living in New York?
IK: That started in '52, ended some time in '53. We'd read on Saturdays and then
we'd often go out to dinner—Tudor, Cage, and sometimes Merce. Not always.
More often Tudor, Cage, M.C., Barbara, and I.
MEH: Back up a bit. When you left Black Mountain, did you come to New York?
IK: I first went to Chapel Hill for a brief period, and then came New York. I was
trying to write then. The business of reading Finnegan's Wake was not
fortuitous. It was serious reading, and I was seriously involved. The
reading—both reading Ulysses and reading Finnegan’s Wake-—and all my
reading of Joyce has influenced my stance as an artist. My stance, my inner
stance, as an artist. The greatest influence upon me ever has been Joyce.
MEH: You read Ulysses out loud in M.C.’s class?
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 6 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
IK: No, we didn't read Ulysses out loud. We read Ulysses, I suppose, chapter by
chapter. I don't remember exactly how we did it. Then we discussed it. It was
interesting. I forget who else was in that class, except one person I know. Bill
Levi sat in on that class so he was there as well as M.C.
MEH: What do you remember about Bill Levi? What was he like?
IK: I always had the greatest respect for Bill Levi. My deep respect for him stems
from my recognition at the time, early, without actually articulating it just in this
way. Bill Levi was a true intellectual, and I'm an intellectual, although I didn't
know it at the time. That intellectuality—and when I use that word, I'm not
using it as a cold, dry kind of concept—intellectual activity can be very vibrant
and life-enhancing for a true intellectual. Bill's intellectuality, which is not
simply a matter of "If P then Q" kind of cold reasoning, and scholarship,
informed almost everything he did or said. I recognized this and enjoyed it. I
enjoy a man who really has depth of knowing. That's why I always liked Bill.
Besides he was sophisticated and worldly. You could learn things. The first
zabaglione I ever had, Bill Levi introduced me to. Things of that sort. But I did
not simply value him for that. I valued him for the way he thought, how he
could think. He was a thinker.
MEH: What did you do on the Work Program at Black Mountain?
IK: I lumbered. We used to cut trees. I loved that.
MEH: What were you cutting the trees for?
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 7 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
IK: Hell if I know. I mean, they were cutting trees. I chopped trees down. Take an
axe and hit a tree. It's marvelous. Cut a big gash and then boom, it goes:
Timber! I liked that. I liked chasing cows up the mountain or down the
mountain. I worked on the farm a couple of times. It was gathering, the
husking of the corn. What else did I work at? I don't know. Riding around on a
truck. Sort of macho type things. I'm a Chicago boy, and I was still a macho
type guy then—or at least those kind of things, cowboys, frontiersmen. I don't
know. I had to get that out of my system. So, those were the kinds of things I
would do.
MEH: Did you ever leave the campus to go into the surrounding areas?
IK: Well, we'd go to that bar there on the road. What was the name of that place?
MEH: Ma Peek's?
IK: Ma Peek's. I went a couple—once or twice to Asheville. I think I went with Paul
Williams once on a liquor run to Hendersonville, down in South Carolina. I'm
not sure if I went on that run or not. But I didn't have much to do with the
town.

MEH: How did Black Mountain people view themselves as individuals or a creative
community?,
IK: They did sort of tend to think they were in a place different from other places,
but whether they thought of themselves as different, I'm not sure, unless they
already had a sense of difference before they ever came there. There are many
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 8 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
ways people can have a sense of difference from their age peers as they grow
up. I suppose difference for me went along the lines of intellectuality from a
young time. Finally, I felt it enough to sort of break and go off to a place that
was different. While I was there, I suppose I was aware that I was in a place
different from other kinds of places, but it wasn't a daily kind of congratulatory
matter that it would be if you were to say daily, "I am a creative person" or "I'm
in a creative community.” I was in a place that's different and I wanted it to be
different and that's fine.
MEH: How would you characterize the college at the time?
IK: Physically. It was very beautiful there. For me it was very beautiful to be in the
mountains although I sometimes had great yearnings to be in a city. New York
or Chicago, a city of that sort. I do love city. I've been in places that have been
more beautiful or more grand, but the mountains there and the lake—even
though it was just a pond—it was fine.
MEH: Was the library adequate?
IK: I was there not to be a student, not to use the library, but to write. That's what I
did. I would start writing late at night and go to bed maybe three, four in the
morning. All my classes were in the morning. I never went to class except on
Saturday morning when I'd go to M. C.'s class. I came with a batch of my own
books, and I could borrow books from people. I can't remember when I was
lacking a book that I needed. But then I was not there to be a scholar, so I didn't
feel that limitation.
MEH: What did you do for entertainment or recreation at the college?
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 9 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
IK: Well, we'd have the dances every Saturday night. I was one of the few guys
who could really dance the jitterbug. So, I would dance. I had a very good
partner in the second semester. Jeanne Belcher was a black girl from Atlanta.
We would put on a show. Occasionally people would play records. I remember
Vera Williams once played a lot of records—folk songs. I had not known folk
songs before. Who sings that marvelous rendition of "I Wonder as I Wander"?
That great guy with the tenor voice. He came down—John Jacob Niles. I
listened to folk singing thereafter for many, many years and had a fair collection
of records into the '80s.
MEH: You went from Black Mountain to Chapel Hill? How long did you stay there?
IK: About a semester. I had married a Black Mountain student. We left Black
Mountain for New York.
MEH: You knew M.C. from Black Mountain, but you didn't meet Cage, Cunningham,
or any of the rest of that circle until in New York?
IK: I met Cage and Cunningham here when M.C. came to New York. I had heard
that John and Merce had been down there. I kept in touch with the Black
Mountain people for a number of years. Dave Resnick and Liz Gellhorn lived
three blocks away from here on Charles and Bleecker. I lived on West 11th.
M.C. and Bill would come through, and I'd always see them.
MEH: What did you do in New York?
IK: I worked in bookstores—whatever I could get. After a while I'd try to scout
books and sell them. You know, you find it for ten cents and sell it for a couple
of dollars. The biggest thing was I once found one for ten cents and sold it for
ten dollars. It was a pirated copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. I rushed out with it
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 10 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
the next day to the Gotham Book Mart. Frances Steloff gave me ten dollars for
it. She probably sold it for twenty-five. Ten dollars in those days was a quarter
of my rent.
MEH: Were you writing in New York?
IK: For a while I was. I remember sending something to Accent magazine and
getting a long letter back, hand-written, saying they would like to see more that
I would write, but it was not something they could use. I sort of was put off by
rejection, which was stupid. There were a lot of other reasons, personal and
psychological. Contextual, meaning my situation at the time. I realize today that
back then, even starting in high school and college and later at Black Mountain,
my potential for becoming something was wide open. Because there’s a certain
functional capability in some people which allows them to do a number of
things, and I have mastered a number of things. The way I've gone has been
very circuitous to get to a kind of goal. Very circuitous.
MEH: You decided to go to the New School to get a B.A.?
IK: My second wife Barbara prompted me. She was research associate at the
Costume Institute at the Metropolitan Museum.
MEH: M.C. moved back to New York from Black Mountain. She and David Tudor.
They were living in the East Village?
IK: M.C. moved to New York. I became good friends with John right away. John
was living down on the East River.
MEH: Why do you think that you hit it off with Cage? What was the connection?
IK: I don't know how we hit it off. We just hit it off. There’s always been between
John and me a real sense of respect for the other person. I don't know how to

say that, but you know another person and he knows you. That's it. There's a
solid connection, and that's always the way it was with John and me.
MEH: What sort of things did you do together?
IK: Oh, I would make out his income tax. We would go eat. He would play for
Barbara and me parts of the Music of Changes that he was writing. What else?
We'd go to The Club. I would go listen to Suzuki with him and things of that
sort. Throw the I Ching, about our fortunes or whatever. Talk. I didn't play chess
with John. What else did we do with John? Sit in Washington Square.
MEH: Where was he living then?
IK: He had an apartment on the east side, right at the river. It was right, just south,
just downtown of the Williamsburg bridge and on the other side was the
Manhattan Bridge. I forget the name of the street. You can look it up. Same
building where Lippold lived.
MEH: Were you still in New York when they moved to The Land in Rockland County?
IK: When The Land was being first discussed, Barbara and I were the original,
amongst the original planners. I’m good at goose. We had it at a meal with
M.C., David, Merce, John, and Boulez. Boulez was here conducting for the
Jean Louis Barrault Company. I remember Boulez brought a bottle of Johnnie
Walker Red. Or was it Johnnie Walker Black? It was Johnnie Walker
(LAUGHS); I forget which. So we had this bottle of booze. I suggested we cook
a Christmas dinner, and we did a goose. Well, 1953 Paul and Vera [Williams]
came. They were in New York. "Hey, I can cook a goose." So, I cooked a goose
again, now with Vera, I think, but it was up at their place. They were living up on
Washington Heights. There were a lot of people at this goose dinner. I forget

how many. I seem to think that Remy Charlip was there. Remy Charlip,
and—was Nick Cernovich there? Nick Cernovich may have been there. Vera
and Paul and Barbara and me. M.C. and David. Was John there? I don't know. I
would think, but I'm not sure. There may have been other people. I think John
was there. Was Merce there? I don't remember. I think it was at that point that
The Land was broached. The plan. So, we were to be included in the original
settlers.
MEH: Was Black Mountain a common bond among those who were there?
IK: Not a common bond. It was sort of a kind of ground. I never think of John as a
Black Mountain person. John was a friend. I never even think of M.C. as a
Black Mountain person, although she talks Black Mountain. Whenever she can
talk Black Mountain, she has lots to say. But she was a close friend, a dear
friend.
MEH: You left New York and went to Harvard to get your Ph.D.?
IK: Yes, in Clinical Psychology. From there I went to Michigan State to teach for
two years. Then the call came from Duke where I taught Personality Theory,
various clinical courses, and an undergraduate Personality course. Then I
created a number of new courses, including one in Madness and Civilization.
They wanted me to do a special course in “creativity.” I said "What's that?" So I
taught a course "Art and Its Making," which is a little different.
MEH: Was this before you started making art?
IK: No. After. After.
MEH: When did you start making art?
176IRWINKREMEN

--- Page 13 ---

RELEASEDTRANSCRIPT
IK: I used to say that M.C. visited once, and we just sort of were playing around
one evening. We were playing with cloth, sewing, and we sewed a sort of
collage out of patches that Barbara had. I thought that was kinda nice to do, But
I don't know if that was it. Then Barbara and I went to Europe. We stayed for a
month in Ascona, Switzerland. We visited the husband of a good friend of
Barbara's, Italo Valenti. He was an Italian painter and a fine collage maker. I
learned something although I can't tell you exactly what I learned. This is a
learning that just came in through the eyes: what was necessary to make a
coherent work of art. When I went home, I took a painting Valenti had given to
Barbara when she left Ascona in 1950 to a local framer. I saw a bunch of mat
board around. I said, "What do you with that mat board?" He said, "I throw it
out." I said, "Can I have it?" So, I took the mat board to my office at Duke. One
night I started making works. I made works for a couple of weeks, really
imitating Albers and Bolotowsky. I used all kinds of materials. Then I started to
do some collage, and within a very short time I made a collage like a collage I'd
never seen before. It still has my stamp, a certain kind of coherence. It was
mine. I recognized that, and that was it. That's how I started. At the end of that
year, we went back to Europe, and I showed Valenti slides. He said, "You must
continue." Later I had a letter from Whitney Jones. He had heard about my
Black Mountain connection. He wanted to reproduce a couple of my works in
the St. Andrews Review.
MEH: Do you think your becoming an artist had some connection with Black
Mountain?
176IRWINKREMEN

IK: No. Becoming an exhibiter, because it was through the Black Mountain Festival
at St. Andrews College that Whitney Jones first heard about me. He had heard
about me, and he wanted to reproduce some work in the St. Andrew’s Review.
MEH: You were still teaching at Duke at this time?
IK: Oh, sure. I continued to teach, even as my life changed at that point. In effect I
had two careers. It has some connection to Black Mountain but, what is
"connection"?
MEH: What difference has Black Mountain made in your life? What do you think the
real influence of Black Mountain?
IK: First of all, it had the friendships. I'm seventy-two. I'm reviewing my baggage,
and it behooves one to travel light, man. (LAUGHS) Travel light. I have Black
Mountain baggage, too. I don't care if I jettison things now. Let's say, if you've
carried the baggage so long, it served some purpose, right? But now, other
things have to come into being. "How did Black Mountain influence you?" It's
very hard to give a proper response to that without adding to one's own myth, in
a sense. Or mouthing some abstraction that doesn't really deal with the
particularities.
