MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION, TESTING SOUND]. Hannelore, how did you come
to be at Black Mountain?
HH: I came to be at Black Mountain in a very unusual way, I think. I would say I
came to be at Black Mountain because I couldn't get into any other college. Not
that I was an enfant terrible. I was a very docile child, so to speak. Maybe. I'm
not sure. But I did get myself into trouble in my high school. Not meaning to
make trouble, but – I'm a self-starter, I wanted to learn how to drive. I had heard
that one can get driver education classes in high school, and so one day I
called a driver education school, and I said, "I'm a student at Fairlawn High and
could you call our principal and suggest that we have driver education." Quite
innocent, I thought. Nothing happened. We never had driver education. But in
my last year of college – I mean of high school – I received only rejections. I
tried to get into Antioch, Swarthmore, I've forgotten the various ones, small
colleges I wanted to be in – liberal arts. So I asked my English teacher, "What's
going on here? I'm not getting into any of the colleges." He said, "Well, do you
remember when you called up the driver education school? The principal did
not like it, and every time he gets a letter from a college that you want to go to,

MEH: So, you arrived via train and the Black Mountain garbage truck.
HH: Yes! Then the rides through the woods. But it was very adventuresome. I was

MEH: Did you have any sense of the American South then?
HH: Not really, no. That came to me a little bit at Black Mountain when the Congress
of Racial Equality – I just can't think of the name of this wonderful black leader
– he's no longer living, tall and lean. They were going to test this particular law
that would allow blacks to sit anywhere in a bus that went interstate. If a bus
went interstate and wasn't a local bus, they could sit up front or in the middle,
not just in the back. That law had to be tested out. It was passed in Congress,
but, you know, if a black person would sit up front, that person was not
protected by the law. That person was made to sit in the back, and so this
group of people were traveling interstate. They would sort of stop at Black
Mountain College just to sort of have a rest from this crusade they were on.
That's when I heard that. I – As a European I was not really schooled in any of
this racial business, the black-white racial business. Not very well informed, I
must say. No. But it came to me.
MEH: You were born in Europe?

me the United States was opportunity to become somebody, to be whoever I
