MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION] Joan, how did you hear about Black Mountain? Why
did you go?
JH: Well, I had been a student at Windsor Mountain in Lenox, Massachusetts,
which had I think a similar philosophy, except that at that point, it was at the
high school level and younger. So, they had a very close affiliation with Black
Mountain. A lot of the students ended up at Black Mountain. Then I also – I
went to Olivet first, and again there was a connection with people going to
Olivet.
MEH: So, you went – Why did you go to Windsor Mountain?
JH: Well, I was a rather rebellious child who didn't fit in to where they grew up, and I
had a lot of problems. Windsor Mountain offered the liberty, the freedom, and
the attention to individuality that I really needed.
MEH: Where did you grow up?
JH: In Cincinnati, Ohio.
MEH: That was a big trip. That was far from home.

MEH: So you were already sort of attuned to the progressive –

their financial situation. He had fired the liberal professors, and so I no sooner
got there than everybody went out on strike, a lot of the students, me included.
So that was a –
MEH: Now is that when Paul Leser and Flola Shepard left?

MEH: Do you remember any particular parties that were held at the college?
JH: It was just one big party! (LAUGHTER) Do I remember them? Yes, people went
wild. I mean, you know, it was an isolated community and people didn't have
cars in those days, for the most part. They couldn't afford them. It just wasn't
the thing. So everybody was stuck in the country. After a while tensions really
boiled over. So, there'd be a party and everything would just explode. All these

over the place and there was a fair amount of sex, something like that.
MEH: Did you ever leave the campus to go into the village or Asheville?
JH: I didn't go into Asheville too much. We spent a lot of time at Peek's Tavern,

surrounding areas hung out there. There were booths, as I recall, and we would
go into a booth and drink and I guess the fact that we were somewhere other
than the school was liberating. Getting back to the parties, though, there is
another aspect which I didn't mention, which was that there was a lot of effort
put into the artistic side of it, the creative side. Costumes, frequently. Food
sometimes, again, was prepared and presented creatively. I mean people –
really it was very important that everything anybody did at Black Mountain
showed creativity, and so it reached into just about every aspect.

MEH: Were you aware of the conflicts going on in the college at the time?
JH: Oh, yes. It was a very conflict-ridden school. While I was there, the conflict
mainly centered around some of the faculty and some of the students who
really felt they were there just to take classes and not to enter into – They
generally weren't the people who were in the arts. They were frequently people
in science and math who felt that they really wanted to study. They didn't want
to have to take part in some other aspects of the school. They also didn't like
some of the carrying-on. Then there was a power struggle within the faculty.
One of the big splits I recall – one of them – was Paul Goodman, who really
precipitated a big brouhaha.
MEH: How was that crisis handled? Do you remember?
JH: The ultimate thing that was handled is they asked him to leave.
MEH: Were the students really involved in this whole discussion?
JH: They were always involved to some extent, because they were represented on
the faculty. The problem was, I think, the subject of his homosexuality came up
at a faculty meeting, and then it went into the notes. As soon as it went into the
notes, people felt they had to do something about it. Of course, this was a
completely different era than now. But there were a whole lot more issues
involved with him as an example to the rest of the student body: his behavior
was really quite radical. He did, I think, establish relationships with some of the
students. All in all, it did split the school. But I don't even know what all the
other things were that divided people.
