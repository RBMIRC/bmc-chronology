LMM: I’m legally blind, by the way, and what I’m doing now I can hardly see
my eyebrows.
MEH: How did you come to be at Black Mountain?
LMM: I heard about it through art school. I’d been going to art school and
professional theatre. This isn’t a camera, I hope. [MORGENSTERN IS
FIXING HAIR AND PUTTING ON MAKEUP.]
MEH: It is. This is wonderful, this is delightful. What art school?
LMM: Cleveland School of Art. I started to go after school and Saturday to
the Cleveland School of Art, when I was eight years old. Also started in
the Cleveland Playhouse, which was a famous theatre. And also
started to ride horses at eight years old. And my mother used to tell me
that if I ever became an actress or an artist or a horsewoman she
would kill herself, but she didn’t because she’s ninety-eight right now
and still alive. [LAUGHTER]
MEH: And you became a horsewoman. I know that.
LMM: I have a million ribbons there and a bunch of pictures of my exquisite
—and that one up there? I have here a carriage horse, an Andalusian
national champion horse, my old age horse. I’m
seventy-one-and-a-half, and I drive Sabrina, that mare, in Texas in
national shows and I drive her and I ride her around the hills in Malibu,
and I carry a telephone on me in case I get bucked off. So tell
Hannelore she can get me any time.
MEH: [LAUGHING] Okay, I shall.
LMM: We have a fax. We have computers. I mean there’s no way she
couldn’t get me.
MEH: So tell me, you went to Black Mountain in the summer of…
LMM: Honey, I only went for a year. My parents found out that it was — They
thought it was a very naughty place when they heard.
MEH: What did your father do? What did your parents do?
LMM: My father was in the piano business his whole life. It’s called Mattlin
Piano Business. My older brother, who’s seventy-five now, is still in it.
That’s in Cleveland. And I did, at the age— After I left Black Mountain
after one year and they told me ‒ my parents told me they wouldn’t
send me back, they wouldn’t pay for it ‒ I did, runaway and lived with
the Indians.
MEH: What did you do at Black Mountain? What did you study?

LMM: I studied art with Josef Albers, but he was not my cup of tea because
he was so Germanic and I was free and wild. And he used to scream
at me with his accent and he used to scare me, so instead I used to
ride a farmer’s horse around the grounds and peer in the window at the
art classes. Oh, and I majored in psychology and art. And we all loved
the psychologist, including my beloved Hannelore.
MEH: Was that John Wallen?
LMM: Yeah. Is he still alive?
MEH: I think he died.
LMM: Why!
MEH: He was very sick. But I think he died. I might be wrong. I can’t keep up
with who’s living and who’s not. So—
LMM: Pray he’s alive, honey.
MEH: Okay. He’s in Portland if he’s still there.
LMM: He was.
MEH: He was in Portland with that group. So you, you’re still, I can see you
are wild. This is a wonderful interview. So Albers wasn’t your cup of
tea. Did you have any interest in theatre at that point?
LMM: There was no theatre at Black Mountain. However, up in Taos, New
Mexico where I ran away to live near the Indians, I was in the Petrified
Forest for the Little Theatre group there, and I was just wild and hairy
and I used to ride horses with the Indians and lend them mine for their
religious rabbit hunts. And they would drape me in a blanket and take
off my glasses and take me to their religious hunts. And [LAUGHS]—
I’m a terribly old person at seventy-one-and-a-half.
MEH: I don’t think so. I think you’re a terribly young person at seventy-one-
and-a-half. And so did you like Black Mountain?
LMM: I loved Black Mountain, but I loved more to be out in the air than taking
a lot of the classes. I would say I adored psychology. And I did learn a
lot from Josef Albers anyway. But he used to say “Ceramics is
gingerbread.” That’s what he’d say: “Ceramics is gingerbread.” Well,
I’ve made my entire life on making tile murals, big architectural
murals—like that. And they’re all over, our colors and sketches for jobs.
And I’ll take you into my studio. We still have time. [OVERTALK] Why
don’t you live here and we can talk a lot?
MEH: Good. That’s a good idea. I would love to.
LMM: [OVERTALK] I could send you some articles about what I’ve been
doing.
MEH: I would like for you to.
LMM: My husband has been trying to get me to talk him an article for years.
By the way, I’ll just tell you one thing. I breed fancy llamas and I make
wonderful things out of them. I mean I don’t make these things. I share
my llamas and the best wool knitter in the United States makes—Isn’t
that gorgeous?
MEH: Wow, feel that.
LMM: Aren’t they gorgeous?

MEH: Have you always been partially blind, or is this an adult thing?
LMM: No, this is for the last—I’ve been always very nearsighted, but I’ve had
a very unsuccessful cataract operation, and I got a hemorrhage in my
retina. And it’s very scary for me to drive, so she’s driving me.
MEH: Now where are you going now?
LMM: I’m going to get a manicure and a pedicure and then to the dentist.
That’s mundane as hell, and I’ve always done it, but I’ve just decided if
I have an accident, it’s not fair.
MEH: Are you going away?
LMM; I’m leaving this house. Oh yeah, I’m going to Maui.
MEH: For?
LMM: For the Pacific Coast Surgical. I’m going to take some medication. It’s
an elite surgical elected organization that my husband is in.
MEH: Okay now let’s use our time to talk about the other things you’ve done
in Black Mountain. So you left Black Mountain after a year. Do you
have any particular strong memories of it?
LMM: Well, I loved the beautiful red soil in the area.
MEH: [AFFIRMATIVE] I think you would. Like your hair.
LMM: [LAUGHS] I loved the outdoors. And—I think I’ll show you my studio to
my husband.
MEH: Actually, it’s okay. From your—
LMM: From Sabrina. .[SHOWING WALL OF RIBBONS]
MEH: So all from Sabrina
LMM: They’re all from Sabrina. A Spanish and Andalusian pure.

LMM: You might ask where I learned to speak Spanish.
MEH: But you spent time in Mexico, didn’t you?
LMM: Yeah, but on purpose I had to speak Spanish and it was one of the
passions of my life. So I just didn’t go near any of the tourists, the
gringos locos, and I stayed with Mexican people and I’ve been
speaking Spanish all my adult career since I was about twenty-eight.
And I love it.
MEH: So you went and you lived with the Indians and—
LMM: Not with them, actually. Near their pueblo, not on Indian land because
they don’t allow that.
MEH: And your parents had not been willing to fund Black Mountain but they
were willing to fund that?
LMM: Oh no. I had to leave home forever, with three hundred dollars and a
German shepherd dog which my student— I worked in New York for a
year and I was the directory [SIC] of the ceramic department at the
Universal School of Handicrafts on 57th Street, and I was very young. I

was under twenty-one, but I knew my ceramics. My formulas, my
glaze, my colors.
MEH: Where had you learned that?
LMM: Cleveland School of Art. And I’m very imaginative and I’ve been
experimenting on color in glaze all my life. And these are, by the way,
just samples of color that I’ve invented. But anyway, no, my parents did
not only not fund it, they said they’d have me arrested if I went to New
Mexico. So I went secretly and I brought a horse with me [LAUGHS]
and a dog, a German shepherd dog, and I went directly to Santa Fe
where I stayed very shortly and then up to Taos, where I lived for six
years. Where I go back every year. I still have Indian friends who are
alive.
MEH: And this time you were— What were you doing? Riding your horses.
LMM: And making ceramics. Making pots. And I started making tile also and
making friends with the Indians. And it was an artists’ colony and I
knew D.H. Lawrence’s widow and I was very very friendly with a very
famous Russian artist named Leon Gaspard, who went to the
Sorbonne with Kandinsky and Picasso. And he was a horseman too.
He was like my grandfather figure, and he did a portrait of me with two
different heads, in pastel, and later he gave it to me. And it’s in my
house.
MEH: That’s the one I saw in the house.
LMM: It’s in my house. And I loved him and helped him marry a young lady of
fifty-five that he was interested in. It’s just— I’ve had a very exciting
time.
MEH: Obviously. And so when did you meet your husband?
LMM: I met my hus— Well, let’s see, we have to go backwards. I have been
married to Leon for over thirty years, and I was forty-one when I
married him, and I had our son David when I was forty-two. And before
Leon married me, he was a bachelor his entire life until he was
forty-nine years old, and it wasn’t easy for him to decide to marry.
And— But he did. And I went with him for about three years before he
decided to break down and get married.
MEH: Where were you living then?
LMM: I was living in Malibu, not here but in another part.
MEH: Well I think you probably are the one person in the world who could
have convinced him that marriage was wonderful.
LMM: I think he was afraid of marriage. I mean he was married to his career.
He’s a brilliant surgeon and a brilliant medical writer and we go all over
the world, as we are going now to Maui, all expenses paid for him to
lecture and show movies of the operations he’s invented. And he’s a
great speaker, and I like him. I love him. I love him.
MEH: Wonderful. And so your interest in— You continue to ride, and you—
LMM: I ride daily. I have three horses that I ride.
MEH: But you’ve ridden— Really your Andalusian—

LMM: Is my old age horse. [LAUGHS] I’ve been breeding Arab horses all my
life and—since I was able to buy one at about twenty-four years old,
and then I became passionate about Peruvian paso horses, a very—a
breed especially invented in Peru. And I had to go to Peru and have
the best. And I went five times in one year to Peru in order to find the
finest mare I could buy, and I found her and she wasn’t for sale
because her owner was a millionaire. He didn’t need to sell her. He
bought her for breeding purposes. And I sort of flirted her out of Peru,
with his permission, and with his best wishes to send him this small
amount of money he asked for when she arrived in Malibu from Peru,
by airplane. And so I had Faviola here. I’ve had thirty-five baby horses
on this property.
MEH: Oh my goodness.
LMM: And I’m about to have another one. It scares me.
MEH: And then you started breeding llamas?
LMM: I started breeding llamas about nine years ago when I was asked to do
a llama mural of a breeder’s very fine llamas, and that’s another story.
Llamas can look like camels or they can look like a wonderful cuddly
kitten. I mean llamas are very special. And when I went to her place
and I saw her llamas, I had to have llamas. So I bought three. I went
home with three. And now I have fourteen anyway. I’m afraid to count.
By the way, I do have lots of—I have various films and stories written
about the llamas and my work with them.
MEH: So maybe you could send me some copies of the stories and some
things.
LMM: Anything you want my husband and I will arrange, even fax them or
whatever.
MEH: Okay. Okay. So did you ever get a degree, a college degree?
LMM: No. [LAUGHS] One can live without. I think I could get a degree in
Living. [LAUGHTER]
MEH: [AFFIRMATIVE] I think you did. So looking back, did Black Mountain—
I mean obviously Albers’ resistance to ceramics was a conflict with
your own interests. Did Black Mountain really have any impact on what
you’ve done later? Or was it just part of a continuum?
LMM: Not too much impact, except I would say that even that year of my
friendship with John Wallen helped me my entire life. And I mean I
hope it did. And I did some very good figure drawing with Albers, and I
know some people from Black Mountain who still write to me or come
to see me or I see, and I believe Mark Schindler, the son of a very
famous architect, went there. But we’re friends on other levels because
I’ve been very active in modern architecture. So I didn’t go there long
enough to have a tremendous influence, to have it. But it was a
marvelous place. It’s very sad that it doesn’t exist now.
MEH: You’ve really had a marvelous life.
LMM: It’s not over, honey.

MEH: You’re still having it. Tell me, what horse was there to ride at Black
Mountain?
LMM: I wandered around, poked around the countryside on foot and I saw a
beautiful little horse in a field and I went and found the horse’s owner’s
house. And I said “Could I ride your horse?” and he said “Well, no
one’s ridden her because she’s half-Arab half-pony or something.
She’s too little for me.” He was a big fat, thing. And her name was
Redwing. But, he said “If you would like to ride, if you really can ride, I’ll
stand here with you and get you used to that which she does
understand. She hasn’t been trained really.” So he approved of my
riding and I used to go and saddle her and ride her every day. And the
people from college would see me and they’d think I was really crazy
because they didn’t take attendance—you did not have to go to class if
you didn’t want to.
MEH: Would you have stayed another year if your parents would have paid?
LMM: Oh, I would have stayed forever, but they just said “It’s a free love
camp. We just found out. They don’t police the dormitories.” It’s just,
you know, the boring middle class bourgeoisie from Cleveland, Ohio.
MEH: Which you escaped.
LMM: Well, I’m probably just as bad as they are now, because I don’t think
you ever escape anything.
MEH: Maybe you reinvent it.
LMM: [LAUGHS] I’m going to have to stop for one minute. [PAUSE.
UNTRANSCRIBED SCHEDULING DISCUSSION.]
MEH: If you were to describe Black Mountain to this young woman, how
would you describe it?
LMM: As the greatest experiment in education in the United States. This is a
really different college that I went to many, many years ago, where they
didn’t attendance. They didn’t give grades either. But you would not—
You would think people wouldn’t go to classes if they didn’t have to, but
the fact is they were at classes because the classes were so brilliant
and interesting and exciting, except for Laurie who was sometimes
riding her horse around the college. But— And they also you would
think maybe it would be a free love campus—my parents
said—because they didn’t tell you you couldn’t sleep with the guy next
door. But the fact it nobody slept with anybody hardly unless they
wanted to and— They were very mature.
MEH: Did you eat in the dining hall?
LMM: We ate in the dining hall. I can’t remember that a lot.
MEH: What about your study? Did you have a study in the Studies—?
LMM: I had a study and we had long, long, long conversations with people
that really you didn’t, on a certain level that you didn’t have when you
were younger at home. And certainly I had such a relationship with
Hannelore Hahn, and I mean the people you knew, you knew. I knew
Ann Mayer very well. Her father was the Levi Strauss blue jean heir,
