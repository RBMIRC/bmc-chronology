GENERAL BACKGROUND
I grew up in Milwaukee, Wisconsin and married Richard there. We stayed there
for one year. Then he got a job teaching at the University of Michigan and
moved to Ann Arbor. In November of '44, we moved to New York City because I
was anxious to be in New York where I could study with all these so-called
wonderful people. They were wonderful, really.
ON DANCE BACKGROUND
I danced in a dance that Merce composed right there at the school that
summer. I was simply a student of his. I never was in his company.
I also studied composition with John Cage. I used to make my own dances
based on John's idea of the micro/macro, the relationship of the small parts as
reflecting the big parts. I constructed my dances that way. It was interesting. I
liked that idea, and it was a good framework for my compositions..
I had studied with Martha Graham and the members of her company that
taught for her. I studied with May O'Donald (PH) and Gertrude Schuur (PH) and
I studied with Hanya Holm. It seems I was always studying with somebody,

thinking that I could get to be better than I was and be other than I was. Finally,
I realized that if I were ever going to do anything, I'd have to stop that and do
my own thing. The time for studying with other people had come and gone. I
was not very good at learning other people's techniques because I had a very
special way of moving all my own. I was not facile at imitating other people's
mannerisms. I just couldn't do it. It didn't work. Thank goodness (LAUGHS).
I stopped dancing when I was about 35 or 36, after we moved from New York to
Long Island. It was a purposeful decision. I didn't have the energy and the drive
anymore that I had when I was younger. When I was young, nothing was too
much for me. I just drove myself to everything I wanted to accomplish, not only
in dancing but in all the other aspects of my life. I could do all those things and
did do all those things. By the time I got to be about 35, I felt that it was a good
time to bow out.
For awhile I started to write some short stories. Then our son was born and I
spent a lot of time with him ‘til he was about nine years old. Even though I had
never been interested in plants at all and never grown anything, I just suddenly
became interested. It was partly because we lived in a place that had a lot of
light and air in Long Island. A good place. I started with houseplants. I suddenly
became aware of the desert plants and knew that these were my plants. These
were the plants that I really liked a lot. I had a beautiful collection, very
wonderful, and all sorts of interesting things and rare things. Over the years I
gradually gave plants to various people and places. It took me longer to get rid
of my collection (LAUGHS) than it did for me to collect it because the plants

ON MERCE CUNNINGHAM
In the forties, Merce didn't have a lot of success. He had a very small and
faithful audience at his concerts. There were always the same people who
knew him and liked his work and insisted on coming. All that wonderful success
came later. He was first with Martha Graham, and he was marvelous and very
much appreciated for his technique as he performed with her. When he left and
did his own choreography, he was starting from the beginning and had to make
his reputation. He was heavily influenced by John Cage, who wrote all his
music in those early times.
I remember the first concert that we saw of his just a few months after we had
moved to New York. It must have been in January or February of 1945. I think it
was at Hunter College. Richard and I were very impressed with his work. We
had never seen anything quite like it, and we thought he was marvelous.
Richard, in his enthusiasm, I believe wrote a letter of appreciation to Merce and
then we got to know him personally through that.
I had seen Merce dance in the summer of 1940 at Bennington College where I
was a student attending their summer dance program. Martha Graham had
composed "Letter to the World," and he was in that. I remember his leaping
onto the stage – this incredible figure in the air.

ON LEARNING ABOUT BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE
I first heard about it because Richard, my husband, was invited to come down
there and spend the summer of 1948, in view to maybe moving down there and
teaching. I don't know whether it was Josef Albers who invited us. He decided
that we would go down and look it over and see how we liked it and how they
liked us. That's how we happened to hear about it.
ON CHILDREN AT BLACK MOUNTAIN
We had two daughters. One was five and one was one. The one thing I do
remember very clearly about that summer is that we were in the dining room
having a meal, and the one-year-old suddenly took off and started to walk.
Those were her first steps, and I was thrilled with that. She was ready. She was
thirteen months old. I thought that was great, and I can still picture that. It
meant a lot to me. I think it does to a mother – to see a child walk, the first
steps.
Our older daughter was thrilled to be there because of living in the country. She
was great for new experiences, and she just ate it all up. She loved it. She was
very happy there. Very happy. She remembers a lot about it. Five years old. But
she can tell you things about it that – maybe better than I can.

Richard bought the hearse because it was cheap. It had a lot of room in it. The
previous use meant that there was very little mileage on it (LAUGHTER). He

thought it was a practical thing and a novel idea. He was always doing things
like that. We drove to Black Mountain in it. We camped in a cemetery on the
way down and on the way back because it was quiet and nobody seemed to
disturb us. It was comfortable. It was fun. I think we also did it to save money.
We didn't have a lot of money in those days (LAUGHS). The hearse was

One time when we were at Black Mountain, for some reason we were going to
go to Asheville. That was the time we piled in a whole mess of kids from the
school. It was almost like a circus where you would have a car and then
endless numbers of people come out. We were all jammed in there, and I think
everybody got a kick out of riding in the vehicle. It was fun.
ON BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE LIVING QUARTERS
We had a nice cottage there. It was off in the woods somewhere, not far from
the main group of buildings, but it was very comfortable. It had a nice big porch
all around it, and we were the only ones there, just our family. We enjoyed it
very much. I remember one time we had a party and Richard filled the bathtub
with ice and champagne. (LAUGHS) We had plenty to drink that night.

Black Mountain College was a group of all kinds of interesting and a little bit
crazy people. (LAUGHS) I thought it was fun. There were a lot of good teachers
there. There was Bucky Fuller. John Cage and Merce were there and Josef
Albers. M.C. Richards. We met her for the first time there. Richard met Ray
Johnson there, and he became part of our lives. He was a student, not a
teacher. Ray was always a very sweet person, and kind and thoughtful and
very considerate. He was a very nice person. He really was. Very nice.
I remember Buckminster Fuller quite well. I remember how crazy everything
sounded to me, the things that he was saying. I found it hard to understand
then, and I never did learn to understand him in after-years (LAUGHS) I guess
it's just beyond me or not my interest. But I liked him very much as a person. I
liked his intensity and the fact that he dreamed up all this stuff. I think he was
an amazing person, really unusual and unique.
ON TEACHING TECHNIQUES OF MERCE CUNNINGHAM AND MARTHA GRAHAM
Each was very personally his or hers, but I think Graham was very much more
limited in her vocabulary – if you want to call it that – because she had certain
movements and certain mannerisms which occurred throughout her dances
and to her they meant something. But I think they weren't valid except for her.
Then for her to teach those movements as a technique was not the best idea,
because they were so personal to her. Maybe that's the only way one can
teach.

Merce was personal, too. I think everyone has to be in a certain way because
everyone is bound by his own technique, limited by that. I think he was a little
broader, to me more valid as a teaching technique.
ON LEAVING BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE
I wish I could remember more, but I haven't thought much about Black
Mountain. It didn't become a part of my life. It kind of faded into the background
and didn't really mean that much to me. I enjoyed it very much while I was
there. I had a good time, but I wasn't particularly anxious to locate there. When
it didn't work out that we ended up there, I wasn't a bit sorry. I wanted to be in
New York. I'm not a big community person. I don't like to be associated with
communities that do a lot of things together. That's not my way. I'm a kind of
loner, kind of independent. That's just the way I am.
