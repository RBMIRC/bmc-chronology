PASCALE GEORGIEV
Atelier Editions, Publisher & Editorial Director Introduction The bullying began as the five men piled into art and writing student Tom Field’s Buick Roadster. It continued for four miles, down and around the curvy mountain roads from Black Mountain College to Ma Peak’s, the local tavern. Over multiple rounds of Ballantine Ale the needling escalated, coming mostly from writing professor Robert Creeley and art student Dan Rice, and focused exclusively on Tom. Some of the taunting was homophobic, some stemmed from jealousy of Tom’s superior painting skills, some was fueled by literal hunger. By then—late summer 1955—the last Black Mountain College farmer was long gone, and the campus, which had come to rely on the now-collapsed farm for subsistence, was in the grips of a severe food crisis. Fuming, Tom drove his tormentors back to campus. The bullying did not cease. At his wits' end, Tom aimed his car at Meadows Inn, the two-story cottage he shared with other students, and pushed the pedal to the metal. A massive Boom’, then silence, then groans. The Buick had rammed against the fieldstone chimney and rested, student Michael Rumaker remembered, “like an accordion.” The cottage shook, then shuddered, then was still. “It was a battlefield,” recalled college rector Charles Olson of the post-crash scene. “The place was strewn with these bodies— broken backs, broken hips.” An hour later, after the injured had been carted off to the hospital, a confused sheriff surveyed the scene and scratched his head. He'd seen everything, he said to no one in particular, but “I have never Introduction heard of a man driving his own car into his own home.” More than two decades earlier, in 1933, and against all odds, Black Mountain College (BMC) was established in Western North Carolina, just outside of Asheville. From the start the small, rural liberal arts college was a radical departure from other institutions of higher education. Faculty owned the college. Students, faculty, and a small staff operated it together. There were no departmental chairs, deans, provosts, or presidents, and certainly no board of trustees. Fine arts, music, theater, and dance were placed at the center of the curriculum. Learning by doing was encouraged, and students were expected to take ownership of their education. Most striking, the college was communal. Students, faculty, and faculty families lived, studied, worked, and socialized together. Funds were always an issue. The school survived through the Great Depression and World War II, partially on the kindness of donors, but also on the rewards reaped by hardworking members of the BMC community who kept the campus farm producing food. Despite these earlier efforts, throughout the 1950s, with no money and a wayward faculty and student body, the college started to crumble, ultimately collapsing in 1956—just a year after the fateful car crash. The story that’s already been told time and again is that despite its perpetual financial woes, remote location, and brief 23-year existence, Black Mountain College attracted a jaw-dropping band of famous and soon-to-be famous artists, writers, thinkers, and visionaries from the US, Europe, and beyond, a veritable who’s who of mid-20th-century Western art and ideas. An abridged list of faculty and students includes Anni and Josef Albers, Ruth Asawa, John Cage, Robert Creeley, Merce Cunningham, Willem de Kooning, Robert Duncan, Buckminster Fuller, Paul Goodman, Ray Johnson, Franz Kline, Jacob Lawrence, Robert Motherwell, Charles Olson, Robert Rauschenberg, M. C. Richards, and Cy Twombly. Nearly all books and exhibitions about Black Mountain College focus on the famous folk who taught, visited, or studied at the school. This book is different. The Farm at Black Mountain College follows renegade students, faculty, farmers, and community partners as they start and sustain a campus farm in the 1930s, build a better farm in the 1940s, and : watch it all collapse in the 1950s. What emerges is a new cast of characters with riveting stories that shed a brighter light on what exactly happened at BMC through the decades. The farm was of vital importance to the college, not only because it provided necessary food from organic farming, but because it served as a testing ground for self-sufficiency, communal living, and collaboration—the most precious and precarious ingredient at the college. In these pages, the spotlight is on the extraordinary contributions of these folk, many of whose stories have seldom, if ever, been explored. Introduction The Farm at Black Mountain College is the product of extensive archival research. Over the last decade, I have made a dozen trips to the Western Regional Archives (WRA) in Asheville, North Carolina, to immerse myself in their Black Mountain College Collection. I have conducted research using other BMC-related collections at ten other archives and centers, including the Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center, the Getty Research Institute, the Swannanoa Valley Museum & History Center, and University of Arizona’s Center for Creative Photography. I have also had the privilege of speaking with ten former Black Mountain College students—Mary Daniels, Ted Dreier Jr, Marita Pevsner Dresner, Martha King, Trueman MacHenry, Ron Robertson, Dorothea Rockburne, Claude Stoller, Marie Tavroges Stilkind, and Martha Treichler— most of whom lit up at the very mention of the farm.

VICTORY FARM
Though much of the land had been cleared and the new barn was impressive, ambitions to actually develop the farmland at Lake Eden were coming along slower than expected. The only other structures were a hastily built small barn and a derelict, unheated farmhouse, and the only field under cultivation was a single plot near the farmhouse, which grew more weeds than veggies. When it came to delegating the farm work to other people, Dreier proved equally ineffectual He had a reputation for exhorting students to work for some kind of spiritual enlightenment, micromanaging them when they did show up, and publicly shaming them when they didn’t. Too often the plots were planted and tended in fits and bursts and sometimes yielded more consternation than food. Although the college would have preferred a slow and steady buildup of healthy soil and farmland, the cc ry’s involvement in the war dramatically sped up the process. Led by Dreier, mem bers of the college began to make contingency plans for inevita ble shortages. A shortage of meat meant raising their own, which would be difficult since there was also a growing shortage of available livestock. Like ¢ rest of the country, BMC was facing difficult times. To Dreier, the solution was clear. Black Mountain College could and must boost self-sufficiency by growing more food, raisi more animals, and building more farm structures—as quickly and inexpensively as possible. A farm, he would announce to anyone within earshot, could profoundly help the college during wartime by supplying the college with dairy, meat, and vegetables and, the victory gardens sprouting up across the country that bolstered morale while supplying produce, the farm would be a patriotic contribution to the war effort. Publisher: Pascale Georgiev Editor; Ananda Pellerin Copy-Editor: Hannah Lack Proofreader: Larysa Kormikeva Indexer Annette Musker Designer: Emma Singleton David Silver's fascinating study offers a new perspective of the pioneering liberal arts school that was Black Mountain College (BMC). Throughout the Depression and World War IT, the farm provided vital sustenance to the college's students and faculty while serving as a testing ground for self-sufficiency, communal living, and collaboration—the most precious ingredient at BMC. The product of deep research, The Farm at Black Mountain College follows renegade students, faculty, and farmers as they establish a campus farm in the 1930s, build a better farm in the 1940s, and watch it all collapse in the 1950s. We meet a new cast of characters whose stories have seldom if ever been explored, and whose adventures in agriculture illuminate exactly what happened at the renowned college across the decades: from optimistic community building to its plunge into substance-addled scarcity. wm
