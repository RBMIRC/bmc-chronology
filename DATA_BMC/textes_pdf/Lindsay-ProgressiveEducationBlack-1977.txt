PROGRESSIVE EDUCATION
                AND THE BLACK COLLEGE



                             BEVERLY LINDSAY
                        Division of Education Policy Studies
                            Pennsylvania State University

                             J. JOHN HARRIS III
                                 School Administration
                                   Indiana University




When the authors began to study the phenomenon of
progressive education, they were confronted by the con-
spicuous absence of material on this subject in Black
education-particularly Black colleges. A survey of the major
Progressive Education Association journal, Progressive Educa-
tion, provided only a few articles that focused on progressive
education and Black schools and dealt with the issue mainly
in terms of experimental human relations courses in white
institutions. Moreover, Lawrence Cremin's (1964) work, The
Transformation of the School. Progressivism in American
Education, does not discuss progressive education in relation
to Black institutions. After reviewing other works and
encountering similar results, one author asked Dr. William
Kornegay (Professor of Cultural Foundation of Education,
University of Massachusetts) for references on progressive
education in Black colleges. His initial response was, "Oh, to
my knowledge, there wasn't any in Black colleges."

   Professor Kornegay's response and the paucity of literature
on the subject pose a fundamental question. How could a
prevailing progressive education movement so completely
elude Black colleges? Perhaps still other questions could be
raised. Educational historians, Louis Benezet and Dwight
Oliver Wendell Holmes, might lead us to ask the following:
"Was progressive education peripheral to all American higher
education, including Black institutions, so that its effect was
negligible?" Education critics, Nash and Katz, might ask:
"Did minute reforms and innovations occur without causing
basic changes in the educational system, therefore indicating
that a progressive education movement never existed?"
Works by James McPherson, John Sekora, Clarence Karier, or
Carter G. Woodson, all historical revisionists, might lead us to
ask the following question: "Were Black Americans primarily
concerned with eliminating overt and covert white control of
Black institutions, thereby precluding interests in progressive
education?" Answering these questions should begin to
provide an essential comprehension of progressive education's
relation to the Black college.




                     OPERATIONAL DEFINITIONS


  Perhaps the first task is to provide working definitions of
progressive education which began in many American schools
during the 1890s. John Dewey and his national cohorts were
interested primarily in tailoring instruction to the different
kinds of children within schools, applying innovative peda-
gogical methods adapted from new research in the social
sciences, and broadening the function of schools to include
direct concern for the quality of family and community life
(Cremin, 1964: viii-ix). By the 1920s the Progressive Educa-
tion Association contended that, among other concerns,
progressive education meant the freedom to develop natur-
ally, the scientific study of pupil development, and coopera-

tion between school and home (Benezet, 1971: 15). Progres-
sive education in elementary and secondary schools should
apply innovative pedagogical methods to child-centered
learning that could be related to community issues.
   During the same period a few experimental colleges were
founded which seemed to advocate similar features, most
notably Sarah Lawrence, Bennington, Bard, and Black
Mountain. These emphasized individualization of student
work and student responsibility and participation. The
working aims of these colleges were centered around the
development of individual powers and the orientation of the
individual toward contemporary societal needs (Benezet,
1971: 13-17; Duberman, 1972: 40-43). Innovative pedagogi-
cal methods were to be the primary means of achieving these
ends.




                           HISTORICAL FACTORS


   An examination of American history shows that the
foundation for progressive education at the college level was
laid during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries,
which coincided with the infancy of most Black colleges, and
with the paramount Black social condition, which was the
golden age of racism.' Because racism permeated American
life, it determined why and how white organizations would
control and finance Black institutions. "As we move further
into the latter part of this century, it is becoming increasingly
clear that the white power structure and its allies are using a
variety of sophisticated methods or mechanisms for destroy-
ing (controlling) Black institutions" (Gary, 1975: 36).
  That whites would initially control many Black institu-
tions through financial allocations is not surprising, since
Blacks lacked substantial capital. Howard University, often
regarded as the most prominent Black institution, was
founded by the Freedmen's Bureau of the federal govern-

and M and Florida A and M were similarly financed by the
federal government. Northern missionary societies founded
institutions such as Fisk University. Since these institutions
were founded and continually financed by the government or
white philanthropic organizations such as the General Educa-
tion Board founded by John D. Rockefeller in 1902, and the
Phelps-Stokes Fund in 1910, such organizations tended to
make the major governing decisions. This is not to suggest
that the Black faculty and personnel acquiesced in these
decisions. Howard University, for example, experienced
several controversies concerning the hiring of white faculty in
lieu of Blacks. And even more prominent controversies
centered around the board of trustees' consecutive appoint-
ments of white college presidents until 1926, although many
Black faculty and students argued that several Black deans
were highly competent (McPherson, 1970: 1358, 1366,
1367). Likewise, it was not until the 1930s that the first
Black trustee joined the board and the first Black faculty
member was appointed at Lincoln University of Pennsyl-
vania. In 1945, almost 100 years after its founding, Lincoln
elected its first Black alumnus, Horace Mann Bond, to the
post of President (Harris et al., 1975: 64, 65). Blacks
maintained that top administrative and faculty positions
should be filled by Blacks. With these paramount concerns
for control over key positions, it is no wonder that Black
institutions did not address themselves to "secondary"
experimental techniques that were characteristic of progres-
sive education.
  It was not enough that whites be in major administrative
and faculty positions, but white philanthropic organizations
such as the Carnegie Foundation and the General Education
Board maintained that they should determine the major
purposes of Black college education, in accord with board
members' racist and capitalistic aims. Andrew Carnegie, just
one year after granting Tuskegee Institute $600,000, said:

And William Baldwin, a railroad magnate and advisor to
Booker T. Washington, made the following statement:2


   In brief, the aim of Tuskegee is to teach the Negro boy or girl to
   be moral and religious, and how to make a living; to educate them
   in those lines in which the opportunity to make a living is open.
   They are educated for their natural environment and not
   educated out of it. [Karier, 1971: 8]


Both Carnegie's and Baldwin's statements indicate that
industrial or "Negro" education should be a primary focus of
Black institutions. These financiers were actually advocating
an inferior Negro education, which the recipients were to
accept and still provide economic benefits for their bene-
factors.
   Because some Black colleges did not always adhere to
principles that the foundations prescribed, such institutions
were forced to face the issue of survival. The first survey of
Black colleges, conducted by a Phelps-Stokes official, was
viewed by many Blacks as a deliberate attempt to destroy
their few colleges, especially those not entirely dependent
upon white funding. The subsequent report ridiculed several
colleges established by Black religious organizations. Phil-
anthropic board officials such as Wallace Buttrick of the
General Education Board were still arguing that no Blacks
should be on foundation boards, nor should anything be
done to alienate Southerners (Sekora, 1968: 255).
   These Black institutions struggling for survival were con-
fronted with dual dilemmas. The first centered around white

funding, including the limited Black resources. Second, even
if Blacks wanted particular projects funded at their institu-

white board representative, who was aware of Southern
opinion. Barring Blacks from positions on the governing
boards made it necessary for Blacks to consider compromises
in their colleges' quest for survival. In short, all avenues were
being blocked!
  White domination had to be reckoned with at Black
institutions. The most overt forms were in terms of Financ-
ing, staffing key positions, proposing "Negro" education, and
castigating reports. With such constraining factors, can we
even wonder if Black college officials could seriously contem-
plate progressive educational issues?
  Overt control or domination is frequently evident; this is
not always the case with covert measures. Carter G. Wood-
son, an eminent Black educator, historian, and writer,
realized this. In 1933 he published his monumental and
poignant work, Mis-Education of the Negro, which was a
philosophical interpretation of the events that produced
problems in Negro education. This work, which was basically
addressed to Negroes, consists of a series of short essays
describing the mis-education of the Negro, closely related to
three central issues. (1) "Are the 'educated' persons actually
equipped to face ordeals before them or unconsciously
contributing [emphasis added] to their own undoing by
perpetuating the regime of the oppressor?" (2) Education,
which was developed in Europe and America, is an anti-
quated process for both whites and Blacks. (3) "When a
man's thinking is controlled, you do not have to worry about
his actions" (Woodson, 1933: chs. 2, 4, 6, 9).
  Woodson devotes several essays to the first issue. The heart
of the trouble lay in the educational institutions which
influence all spheres of Black life. Negroes were taught to
admire classical education, which is nonfunctional since it

background. Negroes have drifted from the mark by creating
a professional class who do not serve the general Black
populace. Woodson emphatically stated that whites do not
need the services of these Black professionals. These Black
professionals desert traditional organizations such as the
churches, thereby leaving the churches in the hands of
near-illiterate ministers. Woodson states that many Black
professionals should be trained as leaders for service to Black
people (Woodson, 1933: ch. 11). This statement, however,
should not be confused with Woodson's other contentions
that too many Blacks enter service professions such as social
work rather than administrative positions. Black college
curricula should include administration or business, thus
enabling Blacks to have some control, not merely dispensing
services at the command of whites.
   The lack of Black political education is an example of
Woodson's third major contention. Woodson (1933: ch. 9)
asserted that the emphasis on industrial education, at the
same time that classical education was being admired, did not
permit the development of a systematic program of political
education. This assertion sounds incredibly contemporary
when we analyze the theories of Paulo Freire, an eminent
Third-World educator and scholar. Freire's banking concept
of education states that the oppressor, the dominant white
society (in the form of the teacher), "deposits" or imparts
the desired information to the Third-World student (Freire,
1971). Martin Carnoy of Stanford University has also
developed this idea extensively with Black American educa-
tion, which he views as a form of internal colonialism
(Carnoy, 1974: chs. 5, 6, 7). Because the student is unaware
that only "desired" information is being imparted, he
unconsciously accepts the information. Therefore, "when a
man's thinking is controlled, you do not have to worry about
his actions."

   John Hope, a president of Morehouse College who
possessed great administrative ability, also did not concur
with underlying philosophies for a nonfunctional Black
education. The following remarks to a Black audience in
Nashville clearly expressed his discontent:

  If we are not striving for equality, in heaven's name for what are
  we living? I regard it as cowardly and dishonest for any of our
  colored people to tell white people or colored people that we are
  not striving for equality.... If we can not do what other
  freedmen do, then we are not free.... In this republic we shall be
  less than freedmen if we have a wit less than that which thrift,
  education, and honor afford other freedmen. [Fosdick, 1972:
  197; emphasis added]


When such remarks were heard by white audiences, it
becomes evident why the General Education Board rejected
many of his initial proposals for Morehouse College. Hope's
proposals were based upon the desire for an independent
Black educational philosophy and programs. No convert aims
should be permitted to seep in.
  Progressive education placed emphasis on the individual's
learning and self-fulfillment, oriented toward the larger
society. The emphasis of Black education, Woodson and
Hope would have maintained, should not be addressed to the
individual per se. Instead, these Black educators were
concerned with the educational process affecting Blacks as a
group. The educational process that helped to produce a
Black professional class which unconsciously aided the
oppressor by failing to aid the Black masses must be altered.
In addition, political education should be a component of the
Black college curriculum so that one could consciously
contribute to Black amelioration.
  Hence, there was actually an essential distinction between
Black educational philosophies, as expounded by Woodson
and Hope, and that of progressive education. These differ-
ences were present, to a large extent, due to the controlling

                     INSTITUTIONAL PHILOSOPHIES


  Several progressive education colleges were founded be-
tween the 1920s and the 1 903s. Sarah Lawrence College, an
institution for women in New York, derived its philosophy
and program from the general American progressive educa-
tion movement. At Sarah Lawrence the medium of liberal
arts was to be geared to the contemporary experience of
living, the individual should be the end and method of the
educative process, and the individual reference point should
serve as the active adjustment agent to the larger society. No
major area of concentration was required, and the question
of study content was suited to each particular case (Benezet,
1971: 57, 62; Lynd, 1945: 15, 16).
  In September 1932, the first students enrolled at another
institution for women, Bennington College in Vermont. The
following major points are indices of Bennington's philos-
ophy (Benezet, 1971: 80):


  (1) Education for each student should in content and method be
      continuous with life, and especially her individual life.

  (2) The educational pattern for each student should literally be
      defined and motivated by her own individual wants, needs,
      traits, and abilities.

  (3) Such a vital and individual-centered education is not enough
       unless it helps prepare her for intelligent, cooperative living in
      her society.

Finally, the philosophy of Bard College, a residential college
of Columbia University for men, advocated individual study,
learning, and socializing for individual education (Benezet,
1971: 115). The philosophies of other progressive education
colleges of this period, such as Black Mountain, Reed,
Antioch, Rollins, and Stephens in Missouri, also expressed
similar aims.
   An analysis of these philosophies and programs indicates
that these liberal arts colleges actually emphasized the
individual over all other considerations.3 This emphasis is
striking considering the fact that during the infancy of these
progressive education colleges, the country was facing the
monumental social crisis of the depression. Some scholars
have pointed out that there was a tremendous influx of
students attending colleges and engaging in individualized
programs, since they could not secure jobs during the
depression (Benezet, 1971: 28). While this may be true, it
must still be realized that the large majority of American
college students were not matriculating at the eight or ten
progressive education institutions where exorbitant fees were
charged, and were not affected by these progressive educa-
tion schools.4
  The largest percentage of college students attended institu-
tions which focused upon a general education curriculum,
although there were a few colleges, such as the University of
Minnesota, University of Chicago, and Stanford University,
where some aspects of progressive education were introduced
on a limited scale. The general education curriculum, present
in the nearly 2,000 higher education institutions existing in
the 1930s, was designed to provide a very broad background
for unspecialized students. As general education developed, it
broadened its base and began to provide more service-
oriented or practical training for its students, such as schools
of nursing which were now being included in colleges instead
of solely in hospitals. (Numerous community colleges origi-
nated during this period also focused upon a service-oriented

then one with a broadened service-oriented base were present
in the majority of colleges also indicates that progressive
education was peripheral to American higher education.
  The fact that progressive education was peripheral to white
institutions surely affected the curriculum and pedagogy at
Black colleges. Designing a curriculum for unspecialized
students becomes extremely crucial for the Black college
when one is aware of the limited educational background and
exposure of a substantial number of the students. A student
could not design an individualized program if he or she did
not possess adequate reading, writing, or research skills.
Closely akin to this were the issues of evaluation and
accreditation. In 1933, only 47 of the 109 Black colleges
were accredited by regional or national associations with over
15,000 of the total 1 8,000-plus Black college students
attending these accredited institutions (Holmes, 1934: 190,
198). Maintaining accreditation, increasing the number of
accredited institutions, and graduating from an accredited
school were crucial matters for Blacks.
  During the 1930s the task for the Black college student
was also to acquire skills and analytical abilities that would
aid him in coping with a massive economic and social
depression that unquestionably affected Blacks more than
whites. A progressive education curriculum geared toward
individualization and innovation, while not requiring a major
as at Sarah Lawrence, would have been an injustice to a Black
student. A Spellman College graduate, for example, needed
the hard-core abilities and skills that she could only acquire
by majoring in an area; yet this issue was not as pressing for
her Sarah Lawrence counterpart.

tion," Paul Nash (1964: 65,66) questions the belief that

progressive education is a defunct movement, since he is
uncertain that it ever existed. A clear and precise definition
of the movement and its applicability or practicability cannot
be discerned from the writings of education scholars. Nash's
scathing critique of Cremin's work, The Transformation of
the School. Progressivism in American Education, is initially
based upon the lack of a precise definition of progressive
education. One may search throughout the 350-plus pages
and only discover how various writers and educators viewed
progressive education at different periods, but not how
Cremin saw it. Cremin did suggest that the actual emergence
of the movement could not be pinpointed; 1955 was the year
that Progressive Education Association ceased to exist, and
1957 marked the final publication year of the Progressive
Education Journal. According to Nash (1964: 66-68), one
cannot search for a "corpse" from this ill-defined movement.
   It is frequently difficult to define precisely any general
movement. For example, obviously there have been various
definitions of the women's movement, of the Black move-
ment, and the peace movement. Given the several concep-
tions of any movement-particularly progressive education-a
scholar may criticize another for failing to point out whether
a movement has been successful, that is, realistically achiev-
ing many of its ideals as defined by the various segments of
the movement, the critics, or the public.
  Nash briefly points out the distinctions between progres-
sive education as a theory and progressive education as a
practice. Classroom teachers, of necessity, were forced to
deal with the pragmatic aspects of these education ideals,
while scholars frequently wrote from a theoretical framework
(Nash, 1964: 71, 72). That is, scholars analyzed progressive
education as a process, while overlooking its actual applica-
bility within a school system. Application of progressive
education ideals could be very different from the stated
ideals.
  Here education critics Katz and Karier prove helpful. Both
writers maintain that what the progressive education theorists

did not even try."

primarily to progressive education in public schools, it is
rather easy to extend the analogy to the several progressive
education colleges. Where is the substantial evidence to
support the contentions that the administration, faculty, or
students addressed fundamental social issues? Surely the fact
that progressive education, during the depths of the depres-

issues demonstrates its failure to respond adequately. Only
affluent students could afford to matriculate at these
institutions, thereby showing that their education was differ-
ent from the average college student. The limited field work
of these students did not accomplish many reforms. And,
finally, there is no evidence to suggest that the "progressive"
institutions made any more attempts, financial or otherwise,
than other American colleges to enroll minority students.

(or is dead), because its ideals were not achieved, is to argue
from a faulty premise.' Certainly the fact that two eminent
progressive education advocates, Dewey and Kilpatrick, were
at the University of Chicago's and Columbia University's
influential schools of education undoubtedly effected much
progressivism in general American education. Both also

few colleges. Perhaps their real contribution will be to serve
as social critics.




                                    SUMMARY


   We now return to our fundamental question, "How could
a prevailing progressive education movement so completely
elude Black colleges?" The movement bypassed Black col-
leges for several reasons, with the most dominant being
Blacks' efforts to gain control over their own higher
education institutions from white organizations. The ideals of
progressive education simply were not viable aids in this
struggle for control. Progressive education was peripheral to
most American colleges, for example, it did not provide the
opportunity for students with limited educational back-
grounds to develop hard-core skills. And such skills were
desperately needed by students, especially minority students,
during the 1930s. Progressive education at the college level
was not as prevalent as we might have supposed initially.
Last, the progressive education movement has experienced
several lulls during its existence and has never achieved many
of its expressed aims. At this writing, however, the movement
is evidenced in some program philosophies of university
