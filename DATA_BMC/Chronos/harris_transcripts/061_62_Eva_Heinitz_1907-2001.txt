EH: I lived in New York at that time. I still played in Pittsburgh with the symphony, a
member of the Pittsburgh Symphony one more year, and then I came entirely
back to New York. So this was—Black Mountain was summer, so in summer I
was free to do. It sounded very interesting to me, and I had, as I told you
yesterday, several friends who told me I absolutely had to go. I went with a whole
bunch of musicians, most of them very good, and in unknown country. Unknown
state, unknown people, everything entirely different, and I liked it very much.
MEH: Do you remember how you got there? Did you take the train?
EH: Train. Yes, I went by train, with two instruments—two large instruments, the cello
and the viola da gamba. Don't ask me how I did it because they were with me. I
had a great technique to travel with them, and then I was much younger and
quite healthy. I still remember when we came in. I suppose somebody picked us
up, or me. Details I can't remember. But I remember driving to the college and
near the road, not far from the main building there was a lovely lady sitting on a
stone, and that was Johanna Jalowetz. I took a look. We didn't stop. We were not
quite there yet. Later I found out who that was, and what a lovely person and how
beautiful she was. That’s beautiful. We became great friends with both of them.
That was the main building. That I still can remember, but I lived away from it. I
can't describe that. Somewhere. Somewhere there was a house, and I had a big
upstairs room. I can't remember who else lived there. Anyway, it was very lovely.
It was only too hot and too humid. The humidity I can't stand. And very bad for
the instruments. And otherwise, well—
MEH: Who do you remember most vividly from Black Mountain?

MEH: I think so.
EH: I think it was about two months. And we were busy. We worked, and also the
specialty there was that so to say you lived with open doors. In other words,
anybody at any time could come in to talk to you. This was something I was not
used to at all. Actually, when I work, I can't stand anybody, you see. But I was
new, so it was not too bad. I met, probably at the dinner at the—cafeteria-style. I
think we were sitting probably outside and inside. There was a little lake, and the
dining room, which was used for anything we did there, all concerts. Every
Sunday there was a concert, which we had to provide. I met—In the beginning, I
can't recall but—Motherwell? A photographer.
MEH: Motherwell was a painter. And I'm not sure who the photographer was.
EH: Well, some Motherwell was there.
MEH: [AFFIRMATIVE] He was.
EH: Yes, I remember. That was him. I didn't know his work at the time. I just
remember the name. There was—well, of course, all the musicians. Then there
was Jalowetz and his wife, who had a lovely place where they lived. I loved their
room and the big library and the piano—was wonderful. That was like Europe for
me, very European. There I felt absolutely at home. And I met Albers. Is that
correct what I say? Albers? And the man who got me there, Lo-—
MEH: Lowinsky.
EH: Lowinsky. And I never could figure out how, what he had to do with Black
Mountain College. But he was the one who came, visited me in New York and

We had every morning breakfast together, and became great friends, and I was
terribly impressed by his wonderful pictures. Then came, as a visitor, Roland
Hayes, whose name was familiar to me, but I had never heard him before. A
Negro tenor, one of the greatest artists I ever heard in my life. And that was
fantastic. So we became friends, and I still saw him later in New York. A great
artist. You met the most extraordinary people there, no matter who you were
yourself. It was really wonderful.

MEH: And you were telling me yesterday about Feininger's giving you a painting?
EH: Yes.
MEH: Could you repeat that?
EH: That was a great surprise in my life. One of the most beautiful days in my life. I
have to say that. The day before—the evening before he left the next day, he
asked me to visit him at the house where he lived. I came to the house, and his
wife packed the luggage for next morning. He was all alone with a big table, and
all his pictures were lying on the table. He said to me that he enjoyed our music,
and he specialized—he said "Your music which you played every Sunday. It was
beautiful. And I am under the impression that you don't really dislike my
paintings, and I would like you to pick out one and take it with you." I was—got so
jittery—I said "Mr. Feininger, may I sit down first?" Then I told him that I don't
understand anything about painting. He said "Nonsense. Of course you do. You
are an artist. You will be surprised how much you understand." So I gave up. But
I was not afraid any more to look at the paintings and maybe make a choice.
Now I wanted not to stay too long, so I looked at several. The first one I picked
up I put aside, and then I picked up several others. I didn't want to keep him that
long, so—And I always came back to the first one I picked up, and looked at him,
and he nodded to me—I've never forgotten that—with the most reassuring face,
and said "You've made a very good choice." Then we got into—which to me was
fascinating—a very interesting conversation why I picked that particular
watercolor, and since I had informed him before that I don't understand anything
about painting, which he refused to accept, but then I felt free to talk. I went into
