MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Eleanor, how did you come to be at Black
Mountain College?
EHS: I needed summer school credits and my college didn't have summer school,
so that's why I went there.
MEH: What college?
EHS: Wesleyan Conservatory in Macon, Georgia.
MEH: They would accept Black Mountain credits?
EHS: That's what she did, the dean.
MEH: What type of school was Wesleyan Conservatory?
EHS: Well, it was the fine arts part of Wesleyan College, which – The college still
exists, it’s a liberal arts – and the conservatory no longer exists. That's why I
don't go back. But it was music, drama, art, and they did have a two-year
secretarial course, which was really interesting. Those gals, they could get a
job anywhere when they got out of there. But it was a good school.
MEH: Where are you from?
EHS: North Carolina.
MEH: From where?

EHS: Well, around. My father was a minister, and we moved every few years.
MEH: Methodist?
EHS: Yes. Of course, what else is there? (LAUGHS) So, we moved a lot.
MEH: Eastern or western part of the state?
EHS: Western. In fact, we lived in Asheville.
MEH: Okay. So how did you hear about Black Mountain? That was my next
question.
EHS: Well, my family had a house at Lake Junaluska, which was a little bit west of
Asheville, and they wanted me to go to summer school in the mountains so
that was the closest place that had art.
MEh: Your family had no objection to your going to Black Mountain.
EHS: They didn't really know what it was like. (LAUGHS) Too bad.
MEH: You needed credits in art? Is that why you went?
EHS: Yes. I took a weaving course from Anni Albers, and THEN I took a painting
course from – I can't remember his name. He was a character from California.
MEH: Varda.
EHS: Yeah, right. And you know, I ran across that name in a biography I read of
Anaïs Nin. He was in and out of her house. It was an interesting experience,
but I didn't learn much. It was culture shock for me, coming from a girls'
school.
MEH: And a Methodist family.
EHS: It was a Methodist school, too, the whole thing, so I didn't learn too much.
MEH: What about it really shocked you or – ?

EHS: Well, everything was so different as far as the art was concerned. People
were doing more modern things and, oh, stuff with broken glass (LAUGHS),
you know, shit like that. So, it was interesting. I wasn't bored, I must admit.
Also I was rather – When people kid you about your Southern accent every
time you open your mouth, after a while you don't open it much.
MEH: People kidded you at Black Mountain?
EHS: Oh, yeah. I don't really have a strong Southern accent, not really. Because
we'd been – we'd lived all over, you know. I just wasn't ready for it. If I could
go now it would be a wonderful experience, but I was too young and had
never – well, you know. It was a nice place to be for the summer, and I made
two or three good friends there. Believe it or not, a woman from Beth (?)
Wesleyan showed up too.
MEH: Who was that?
EHS: Janice Walker. Her last name is Miller now. But she did not stay. Her
grandparents were paying for her time there, so she decided to study painting
with Jacob Lawrence. As soon as they found out she was studying from a
black person, they yanked her out of there. I said, "You fool, why did you tell
them?" But she left very quickly. There's a woman who lives in Winston now,
whom I have seen recently. She went there – Sue Moore.
MEH: Right
EHS: I don't know her married name.
MEH: She never married.

EHS: I ran into her this last winter. My grandson was giving a piano recital there at
Reynolda House and she came.
MEH: Who were the people with whom you remained good friends?
EHS: None.
MEH: Oh, I thought you said you made a couple of close friends. But you didn't stay
in touch.
EHS: Right. Just Sue. Another girl I met was Tony Allenbach (?), or something like
that? She was from Syracuse. I didn't see her in that booklet, the list or
whatever it was. Anyway, she was a nice person. I spent a lot of time running
from these two guys (LAUGHS). I wasn't used to doing that, so – But I made
it. I didn't get trapped. It was a nice place to be. I enjoyed it, but I didn't learn
much.
MEH: What about from Anni Albers, did you learn anything?
EHS: Well, what I really wanted to learn there was how to thread a loom, only none
of us threaded the looms. They were all done. So, we didn't do that. It was, it
was okay. I just was not used to all this stuff, you know. I managed to live
through it. It was not that bad. I sound like it was terrible, but it wasn't. It was
just all quite different from where I had been and where I came from. As I
said, I'd love to go now and I would probably get more out of it.
MEH: What do you remember about Varda’s class?
EHS: He was funny as hell (LAUGHS).
MEH: Where did he hold it?

EHS: You know, I don't remember. It was in a big room, somewhere in the Studio
Building. I can't remember though. That was a long time ago. But we were
doing mostly collages for him with colored paper. There was a wide variety of
stuff done.
MEH: Had you been exposed to abstract art before then?
EHS: Oh, yeah, in art history, but we did not experiment with that in painting
classes. In design classes and stuff like that, we would, but –
MEH: Did you have a design class at your college?
EHS: Oh, yeah, we had to take everything, and we did. Our curriculum was laid out
for us. Now, they choose it all, but we had to have a certain number of credits
in this and a certain number of credits in that. That was it. I had to take history
of costume because it was the only other one I could get. We had to have
three art history and that was the only other one I could get and I hated it.
(LAUGHS) But – I made it. And I'm still painting.
MEH: Let's go back to Black Mountain, and then we'll go back to your later work. Do
you remember any particular experiences there with respect to people who
were there or events or concerts or exhibitions or anything like that?
EHS: I don't remember any exhibitions. We used to have open air concerts on a
Sunday afternoon, I think it was. We'd all lie out on the grass, under the trees,
and that was very pleasant.
MEH: Who would play?
EHS: It was records. No live music. Jacob Lawrence was there.
MEH: Do you remember him at all? Did you have any association with him?

EHS: No. Just in passing. I like his work. His wife was with him, too. She was a very
beautiful woman, a dancer. Real pretty. Let me see, what else? Oh we all
made sandals. Everybody had to make sandals.
MEH: Was Rudofsky there that summer or was it the summer before?
EHS: I was there in '46. Must have been the summer before.
MEH: Did the college supply the leather? Was there like leather there for you, or – ?
EHS: Yeah, and we used the tire treads for the soles. (LAUGHS)
MEH: Were you accustomed to wearing sandals?
EHS: Oh, yes, sure, sure. I didn't live in the boonies. But it was fun to make them.
For a while I kept up with Tony, but that didn't last long. She lived in Syracuse.
That's a long way off. And, you know, you get busy, and I married right – the
year after I graduated. So, I didn't paint for a long time.
MEH: Do you remember anything about meals at the college?
EHS: Oh, yeah. I liked the food, very much. But I remember all these Yankees, and
they said, "I hope I'll never see another grit!" (LAUGHS) They hated the okra.
I love it. But we had to do chores, and I think I set the tables and did things
like that. I think they had a farm with cows. Milk cows, I think, yes. I never
went to it but – It was fun, swimming in that little lake, too, or a pond really.
The place is still there, isn't it? What is it doing now?
MEH: It's a boys' camp now.
EHS: A good setting for a camp. Very pretty. And way back in.
MEH: Where did you sleep?

EHS: We were in one – The girls were in one of the lodges. It was almost directly
across from the dining hall. We had two to a room. I don't remember – ours
was downstairs, and I don't know whether any of the rooms were – you know,
at that age you never notice things like that.
MEH: Did you have any association with Josef Albers?
EHS: No.
MEH: You didn't take his course?
EHS: Not at all. Just saw him in passing. When did he die? She –
MEH: She died recently, but he died quite a few years ago.
EHS: She lived much longer.
MEH: (OVERTALK) He died like fifteen or twenty years ago.
EHS: Well, our museum in Raleigh has several of his pieces, and I still see mention
of her textiles in various art magazines. She did beautiful things. She was
greatly troubled with arthritis though. Poor thing. She was just really in pain.
MEH: Did you ever leave the campus, like going to Asheville or going into Black
Mountain?
EHS: Oh yeah, I went to Junaluska. I'd go for the weekend or whatever. But going
into Asheville held no interest for me. Of course, the people in Black Mountain
town and around, they thought everybody was crazy, you know, that route.
They still think artists are crazy. Normal people, I mean. But they always say,
“I wish I could paint, too."
MEH: So, you left the college and went back to finish your –
EHS: Yes. I graduated the following year, '47.

MEH: And what did you do then?
EHS: Then I got married in '48 and then by 1951 I had three boys. Wham. So, I
didn't paint for a long time, needless to say.
MEH: Were you living in North Carolina?
EHS: No, we started out living in Florida, and all three of my kids were born there.
And then we came back to North Carolina.
MEH: What did your husband do?
EHS: At one time he was a salesman for a surgical house and then he became a
purchasing agent for a hospital. So, he bought the things he used to sell.
Then we ended up here. He worked at Memorial. We lived in western
Carolina. It was fun because our children liked to camp and fish, so that's
what we did.
MEH: When did you start painting again?
EHS: Well, actually – Well, no, we moved to Burlington first from western Carolina.
And then I started paint – I was teaching art there, in their Art Center. And I
guess that was in the early '60s. And I've been painting ever since. The kids
got bigger. Now I have one exhibit a year, and that's it. I'm not into marketing
very much.
MEH: Where – Is this a gallery?
EHS: Yes, a gallery in Carrboro.
MEH: That's pretty good.
EHS: Yes.
MEH: How many years have you been living in Chapel Hill?

MEH: I'm going to turn and put some of your paintings on the videotape before I

EHS: No. None, whatsoever. That's what I meant by culture shock.
MEH: What sort of clothes did you wear at the college? Besides your homemade
sandals!
EHS: Just what I've got on now. I don't wear shorts now. But shorts. You know, just
like camp clothes. Casual. That's what everybody wore. Who wants to do
laundry and iron? Ugh. Not me. It was a neat place to spend the summer, but
I sure didn't learn anything. (LAUGHS)
But they – I can't understand why this regimented college I went to accepted
those credits. Weird, huh? Probably because my father was a minister and
they assumed that the church supported that school. (LAUGHS) Doesn't
sound too good, does it?
MEH: But it's interesting that your father, being in that part of the state – Do you
think he knew anything about the school before?
EHS: No. At that time, my parents lived in Florida, and they would come to
Junaluska for the summer. That's the Methodist Assembly ground, as you well
know. We always spent our summers there, every year. It was fun. Everybody
else was a P.K. too, so we didn't rat on each other. It's much easier now for
preachers' kids.

MEH: Are you sure?
EHS: Yes, it is. I don't know, we always had to worry about people, what people
would say. So going off to college – Everybody complained about the rules. It
was like getting out of jail for me (LAUGHS).
MEH: Were you accustomed to having even – after college – the amount of freedom
that you had at Black Mountain?
EHS: Well, there's always a way (LAUGHS). Just getting away from the parental
influence and the church because people were always watching you, you
know – You were supposed to be better than everybody else. Of course, we
weren't. Black Mountain College was an interesting experience, but like I said,
I didn't get so much out of it except pleasure, meeting people, and – But it
was an experience and I did get some credits. So that was good.
MEH: Do you remember anything about Varda’s car?
EHS: Yes. God, I don't know how he got here from California in that car! It was that
Model T Ford. Every piece of it was painted a different color. It was great. I
loved it. I don't know how he got that car across the country. Maybe he
shipped it. No, he drove it.
MEH: He drove it.
EHS: It looked like it was falling apart. But he bombed around in that thing all the
time.
MEH: Did you ever go with him in it?
EHS: No. No. I wasn't very outgoing. It would have been nice to be mature and be
there.

MEH: Did you have any – The college was integrated at that point, wasn't it? Was
that something that you were accustomed to?
EHS: Oh, no, I wasn't accustomed to it, but it never bothered me. In fact, I have lots
of friends that are black. Being a preacher's kid we couldn't do as many things
so we read a lot. That makes a big difference in how one feels about things
like that. Of course, living in Chapel Hill, it's cosmopolitan. I think all different
kinds of people are much more interesting than just one kind. I've never been
into the country club set. They’re so boring. My college did not have
sororities, and that was one reason why I went there. Because I went from a
high school that had sororities, you know.
MEH: A high school that had sororities!
EHS: Yeah, isn't that awful?
MEH: Where was that?
EHS: In Miami. In Florida. It was awful. But at Wesleyan there were two sororities
that were founded there, and it got so bad, the snobbery, that the powers that
be said, "That's it. No more sororities!" So, that's one reason why I went there.
One of them.
MEh: Do you remember a Greek party they had that summer?
EHS: Yes.
MEH: What do you remember about it?
EHS: I remember – Let's see. Ati Gropius was there at the time, and her favorite
guy. I can't remember his name.
MEH: Charles Forberg.
