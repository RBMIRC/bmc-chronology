MEH: You were saying that your arrival at Black Mountain was unplanned.
SG: I was only sixteen years old. I had just graduated from high school. My parents
would not allow me to go away to college, but they conceded to send me away
for the summer somewhere. It transpired that a friend of mine who lived in the
same building on West End Avenue at the time was going to this place called
Black Mountain College. I mean, neither myself nor my parents had ever heard
of it. They agreed to let me go, largely because this girl was going, and I could
be shipped off with her. They felt safer sending me with somebody else.
Ben Shahn was going to teach there, and Robert Motherwell. My father was
also an artist so he certainly knew who those people were. It was a bit of an
unknown quantity to everybody. When we got there, it was a little unsettling
because I'd not really been away from home except for summer camp. It was a
strange environment to me. For one thing, I had never heard of homosexuals. I
didn't know what that was. I was pretty naive. My first vision was of three guys
coming up the road in thong sandals – leather sandals – and cerise pink sort of
Schiaparelli shirts open to the waist, with these hairy chests hanging out. They
had them knotted at the waist, and they'd obviously hand-dyed these shirts. I
just had never seen anything quite like that.

I suffered some discomfort initially there because I didn't have the hang of this
kind of person. I'd never been in a Bohemian setting, but it was very congenial
to me. It wasn't too long before I found my little niche there, and I really liked it.
But it was unlike anything I had ever known. I secretly knew that if my parents
really knew what it was, that they would never let me be there. Whatever it was,
I wanted so badly to be away from them. I was determined to make my way in it
so I wouldn't have to go home and spend the summer in New York with my
parents.
Meanwhile my friend was not faring too well. She was doing badly with the
same discomfort that we both felt. Whereas I was kind of getting through mine
and getting more and more comfortable, she developed – whether
psychosomatic or physical or who knows what – serious allergies. She could
hardly breathe and was sneezing all the time and would stand around in a
trance, semi-immobile, not knowing where to go or what to do. She really didn't
like it very much. I guess she told this to her mother on the telephone, and her
mother came down to check out the place and see what was wrong. She was in
the opposite situation of mine. It turned out really she wanted to go home. Her
mother, who was divorced, didn't want her kid at home and wanted the kid to be
down here so she could have the summer free to do whatever it was she was
doing. My parents really hated letting me out of their sight because they liked to
monitor and control my every breath and movement. So, they would have loved
if I had said, "I don't like it here. I want to come home," which is the last thing I
would ever say or admit to. This mother came down, and I gather she was
given instructions from my parents. My mother: if you get down there and you

known what it was, would never have sent me to. I didn't know what it was so I

MEH: Can you be a little more specific in terms of the nature of the community, what

SG: Well, it was a world of artists and poets and people who were not living
according to consensual reality. They were not conventional, they were
creative, and they just seemed to me at the time interesting and glamorous and
exciting and romantic, all the things that my life with my parents did not. My
father was an artist, but he had a very hard time living any life like that with my
mother, who was [a] very strict and kind of unimaginative person. My mother
was the sort of person who, if she would send me down to the store to buy
sandwiches in the Tiptoe Inn, would insist that I get dressed so that I looked
nice to go down there. I was not supposed to wear jeans and things to go do an
errand. Why? Because you never know who you're going to run into on the
street.

MEH: Did you have any idea at that point of what you wanted to do?
SG: Well, I went there to do art, to do painting, and I never made a painting there. I
was completely intimidated. I didn't know what to paint. In the meantime I kind
of fell in love with Charles Olson and fell under his spell. He was wonderful to
me, and I started to write my first poems there. I used to stick them secretly into
his box. He would say that he thought they were wonderful. Eventually I joined
the class. He used to read the poems out loud in front of the class. I became
encouraged by all that. I was very impressed with him as a teacher and a
reader and a poet. I had never had an experience like what he was like. His
classes would go on sometimes until one in the morning. There would be so
many people that they would be crowded out the doorway and into the halls.
People would sit on the floor, which amazed me. They drank beer and smoked
cigarettes and all that sort of thing. All of these very heady ideas, and the
excitement of staying up halfway into the night. I'd never seen teaching go on
like that. I was just out of Hunter High School, and I was then slated to go to
Hunter College. The first day at Hunter College I was sitting in this auditorium
[at a] welcome invocation that we had to do on the first day of term. In came all
these pot-bellied ladies who walked up the aisles to go on the stage. They were
all these fat, older, ugly – I thought at the time, or my recollection is – very
undistinguished-looking middle-aged ladies. We were all expected to stand up
and applaud as they walked in. It completely freaked me out after my Charles
Olson experience, where I thought I was with a god. I knew they were not

years that I wrote poetry. Between the ages of sixteen and twenty-one or so, I
wrote like that. I recently came upon some of those old poems when I moved
here. When I had to go through all my things to sort of dismantle my life in

MEH: In a similar vein, Black Mountain itself was equally unprogrammed, in terms of
what happened there. The founders created this experimental college in the
1930s, following certain progressive ideas. But they had no idea that it would
evolve into this sort of bohemian art community and something that no one
would create intentionally any more than you intentionally planned to arrive
there. Some people about five years ago wanted to recreate Black Mountain
College. I just said, "You can't do it." It was something that evolved out of a lot
of people's experiences and ideas at a particular time.
SG: Right. No you couldn't recreate it now. I mean the times are so different.
MEH: I don't think you could recreate it in any situation, because it wasn't a planned
thing. Once you plan these things, something like that, then it becomes a
programed thing.
SG: Say something like the Institute for Social Ecology that Murray Bookchin
founded up in Vermont. That's the sort of place. There are some places like that
now that would be an equivalent kind of cutting edge for our time, people who
are really working on that sort of putting the ecological underpinnings into the
education system and putting all that into action and really living it. You couldn't
