MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. I was going through some material – you were
already teaching when you went to Black Mountain.
MKJ: Yes. I was teaching at Drake University in their College of Fine Arts, and I had
already had six years of training, including college and art school and graduate
school. But when I went to Black Mountain College, in those six weeks – I was
only there for six weeks – I learned it seemed to me more than I had learned
ever before. Maybe I was just ripe to learn this, but I actually went to study with
José de Creeft because I was doing stone sculpture.
MEH: How had you heard about the College?
MKJ: My mother, who was an art teacher at the College of Wooster in Wooster, Ohio,
sent me a tiny little clipping from an art magazine which said, just José de
Creeft was going to be teaching direct carving at Black Mountain College, North
Carolina. She thought I might be interested. I surely was so that's all I knew
about Black Mountain.
MEH: That's interesting.
MKJ: But when I got there and discovered Josef Albers was there, and what he was
teaching– the combination of being able to work with both Josef Albers and

José de Creeft was a total delight. After all, de Creeft was such a colorful
character and understood form in stone, and I just lapped it up. But also
studying basic design, two and three dimensional, with Albers was absolutely
for me a fabulous experience. We worked with wire as drawing in space. We
did color relativity problems. The thing I got most from it was the fact that not
only is color relative to what else is within a particular work, but that the same is
true with shapes relating – a curve appears less curved if it's against a more
curved line or shape. In fact, nothing is absolute. It's only what it is within the
environment within which it's working. That is so basic, and it really got through
to me. The way it changed my life was that forever after that, including now, I'm
learning from what Albers taught us in that brief moment that summer, six
weeks. It helped me plan my own teaching in a much better way. I developed
my own particular problems for students to solve, but the basis was this
fundamental approach to the visual elements and their relationships that I got
from Albers. When I think of all the people he must have influenced in that way
– what a legacy he did leave.
MEH: Were you familiar with Albers at the time?
MKJ: No! In fact, that was a whole revelation. I did know about the Bauhaus. Maybe I
did know he was a teacher there, but he wasn't somebody prominently in my
mind at all. I probably did learn about him, because at Pratt – when I was a
student at Pratt, we went to an exhibition of the Bauhaus at the old Museum of
Modern Art, and I remember being very much impressed by that.

MEH: What sort of things did he say?
MKJ: There was no character. I can't remember exactly what it was, but what I got
from it was that there was no character to the work, no spine to the work. The
designs were good, the compositions – and his criticisms had to do with the
paintings and sculpture. He said, "Look at that arm. It doesn't have any
substance to it" or "The movements don't work well." I imagine it was
something like that, because that's the impression I got from it. I just was so
shocked, because I had always thought that what I was doing was, you know,
not so bad. I was working very hard and all that. But that really crushed me for
a few hours, and then I realized, "I'd better see what this man has to offer." It
just made my sponge so wet that I wanted to listen to every word he said. Now,
if he hadn't criticized me so harshly, I just wonder if I would have thought, "Well,
I know those things." Maybe, I would have only taken them in lightly. But he had
a real purpose. He had a reason for pulling my works apart. I wouldn't have
wanted to show them to anybody after his chastisement.
MEH: He certainly made you dissatisfied.
MKJ: Yes! So, I got so much out of what he had to offer. One of the things that struck
me was – We had one problem in doing wire, three-dimensional works, and
