MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Harvey, you came to Black Mountain in 1949?
HH: Right.
MEH: How did you come to be there?
HH: I heard of Black Mountain through Cliff, my brother, and Barbara. They
were in Taos, New Mexico, newly married, in ’48 or ‘9, and they had come
across Oli and Joan Sihvonen who had settled down there for the
summer, I guess—painters. And Cliff got interested in the Albers kind of
painting and so they got together and were talking about Black Mountain
and what a great place it was. You know, they were really urging Cliff and
Barbara to go there. Well, I came out of the Army at that time, stopped by
and met Oli and Joan, they introduced me to them. But I had no intention
to go way out to North Carolina at that time, so—the other end of the
earth. So I spent a term and a half at Pepperdine College, which my father
had told me—he’d kind of given me the introduction to Pepperdine there
as a small college and I wanted to go to a small place. Well, I lasted a
quarter and a half there and decided that maybe I didn’t want to go
through a campus-style school. So I wrote to Black Mountain and ended
up there the next—after a summer. I took a summer trip up to Montana
with my mother and left for North Carolina in September.
MEH: And where were you from?
HH: Santa Monica, California.
MEH: Do you recall how you physically got to Black Mountain the first time?
HH: Oh. yeah. [LAUGHS] Yeah. Two friends, three friends of mine—I went
through tech school to study radio operating there during the war. They
picked out a car for me. I wanted a Model A Ford. They talked me out of it
and got a ’36 Dodge, and they took the engine apart and put it all together
and I had a car that lasted me all the time I was at Black Mountain. So I
drove out that way, cross-country. You wanted to know how I got there.
MEH: It’s interesting, as we look back in time, you know, everybody flies today,
but [INAUDIBLE- OFFMIKE] routes to get there.
HH: Yeah. Well, I really enjoyed the trip. I mean—As I say, I’d taken a trip up to
Montana where my mother was from the summer before and that was
such a wonderful trip and I really looked forward to driving across the
country. Of course, I knew how long it was. It was—I wasn’t going, I wasn’t
averaging sixty miles an hour. I was averaging probably forty, so it took a
while, but I was driving a lot, you know. I wasn’t stopping a lot. And I really

heard of the Bible Belt—and I never knew anything about that. But when I
got to—I had a very hard time finding the actual place because, of course,
Black Mountain College is off the highway, main highway, so I was going
back and forth, back and forth, and finally hit on it. And when I went up
there, there was nobody around and so I had to go back to Asheville. I
guess I was so tense and so nervous from driving so long, I didn’t get out

to go back to Asheville and go to a hotel and sleep overnight [LAUGHS].
The next morning I came out, went up and I saw the sign that said
“Office”—I guess it was a black door, if I remember right. And there was
Natasha GoldowskI and a tall blonde, I don’t remember her name, who
was the secretary there. And she says “Oh you’re Harvey Sample
Harmon, aren’t you?” [LAUGHS] I had signed everything Harvey Sample
Harmon. So they set me up. Eric, don’t remember his last name, Eric . .
MEH: Renner?
HH: Yeah, yeah, Eric Renner, yeah. He took me down to North Lodge and
gave me a nice corner room because I was there early. I came there
before everybody else, I guess. He gave me the corner room kind of

HH: Well, of course, I’d seen some pictures. Oli Sihvonen had described this

MEH: Did you have any idea when you went what you wanted to study?
HH: My whole idea was I wanted to write, so I didn’t, I didn’t have any idea
what I wanted to study. And, of course, when I saw that they had language
and they had anthropology and writing, a writing class, that seemed fine to
me. I mean that was what I wanted, that kind of thing.
MEH: Did you take Paul Leser’s anthropology?
HH: Oh yeah.
MEH: What do you remember about his class and the teacher, the person?
HH: Well, Paul Leser, before he arrived, he put up an assignment on the
board, and he said “Read these three or four people on human origins.”
And so when he arrived with his little dog, Beagle—he was a very
charming, very funny—amusing, I should say—he always had a smile on
his face and always—in his European way. I mean there’s that
standoffishness, but still very warm. Always looking for some way to kind
of prick you into saying something or thinking something [LAUGHS]. And I
remember the first time, when he came, arrived, he called each of us
together. Oh, that’s right, we had to write a little paper on what we thought
of human origins, how we summarized it after reading his assignment.
And I had made a very wishy-washy thing about how everyone must have
arrived on the planet all over the place. I concurred with somebody from
the University of Washington, I forget his name, that we could have arrived
on the earth everywhere and raised up similar civilizations. And he, I don’t
remember exactly what he said, but he made me feel very stupid. And I
was [LAUGHS] when it came to that, when it came to human origins. But
in a word, he did it in a—not in an abrasive way, but he did jolt you in his
way. Making you say things funny. I mean, that’s the way I would say: he
made you say things that well, I mean, we couldn’t back up our knowledge
with very much so he would make us look foolish. But he made us study
and we loved it. I mean we loved the fact that he could get things done,

MEH: You were saying that, that you didn’t study painting, that the smell of paint was
everywhere.
HH: Yeah, I mean, I mean, it is literally so. I mean everyone was—And they had all
their doors open. We didn’t have, well, you know, closed doors. Mostly writers
would have their doors closed—but not necessarily. But, I mean, usually the
artists had their doors open and you had everyone painting and really immersed
in it. And you could almost say that music was also, to a much lesser degree, but
there were some times when there was music playing all the time all over the
place. People took music seriously then because—at that time was also the
emergence of the long-play record. It just came out. And everyone was bringing
in their new records and a lot of nice, nice music was coming out (?).
MEH: Was there much jazz around then?
HH: Well, I don’t remember any jazz personally. One day I came into North Lodge,
and that must have been the second year. And I heard this wonderful jazz sound.
I had never heard anything like it before. And I knocked on his door and he was
one of the new fellows. I didn’t know him. And what was his name? I grew very
friendly with him. Oh yeah, I also joked about Superman’s name, what was it?
Clark Kent, his name was—Carl Kent, [SIC] yeah. Okay. So he had these records
which we call “cool jazz” now. That was a welcome introduction. And also Neil
Noland, I guess his brother Ken came by a few times. I think he brought a few
records himself, beautiful cool jazz—Gerry Mulligan and, I forget their names.
West Coast jazz. Wonderful stuff. Yeah. And, of course, the first year again I
remember so clearly we had all these old 78s playing from the library, and Tim
LaFarge would always put on Huddie Ledbetter’s “Irene.” [LAUGHS] “Goodnight
Irene.” He would play that forever. And we had a lot of that, Spanish Civil War
stuff, records. They were all 78 then. This was all before we got the influx of the
long-playing stuff. Which reminds me, just to put this in perspective, I guess I put
it in my previous interview, but before I went to Black Mountain my father had
informed me that they were on the Attorney General’s list. What was that? I didn’t
know anything [LAUGHS] and he had me go to my old friend, the Secretary of
the YMCA, to tell me that the Attorney Generals was a very serious thing, that
they were suspected Communists, involved probably, and therefore in this day
you don’t want to get mixed up in this kind of thing, the FBI would be
investigating and all. So I mean that was the context we went into that year, and
those years.
MEH: What did your father do? How would he have been aware of this ?
HH: Well, he was an American Legion member for many many years so he kept up
with what their—You know, they sent out Flashes, I’m sure
[LAUGHS]—warnings. I even read—oh, my God, I think it was ’52 or so or ’51
when I was home. I opened up an American Legion magazine and here was this
venomous thing against William Carlos Williams in the American Legion. What is
this! And the contention was that he didn’t like Milton. [LAUGHS] I couldn’t
believe it. Anyway—And my father—
