MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Marjorie, how did you come to be at Black
Mountain College?
MM: From Cambridge School of Weston. My mother was quite an investigator of
schools, and she looked into Bennington and into Reed College, of all things,
and Black Mountain. So, we took a trip down to Black Mountain. I guess it was
in '39. I really liked it and my mother really liked it. She's quite a powerful
woman (INAUDIBLE) (LAUGHS) – not unusual. So, I just decided to go there.
She said if my father had seen it, I never would have gone. My dad was a
Republican, conservative businessman. A good one.
MEH: But they sent you to Cambridge School, which was a progressive school?
MM: Yes, right. It was my mother that found that. Cambridge just saved my life.
Public school – I was very, very, very shy, and public school was just painful. I
went to Cambridge, and while mother was looking around the school, I was in
this little room in the cellar with Frank Wigglesworth – Mr. Wigs – playing with
clay and making little animals, and I was in my element. So, that was beautiful,
because I majored in sculpture, and I could follow through with what I really

MEH: You were there the year they built the Studies Building.
MM: That was quite a gift to be there. Like the first year I was there, we were
building the Study Building. We'd load up in a truck from Lee Hall, go over to
Lake Eden and, you know, dig ditches. I did some farming. I remember learning
how to use a mattock. Nowadays when I'm working with a friend or a child or a
gardener, I say, "Well, I learned one thing at Black Mountain College and that
was to wash the tools after you finish!" So, – But that was quite wonderful to be
– The community. I think I – I loved that part, the community and – actually,
going across the valley in the truck and coming back all grungy, and cleaning
up. It was so beautiful around there. I used to get up very early and go up into
the hills and walk – in early early spring, the flowers, and come down for Jack
Lipsey's good breakfast. I was there when Ted Dreier's son, and Bobbie's son,
was killed. That was the first year at Lake Eden.
MEH: What do you remember about that?
MM: It was so – It was the agony of Jack Lipsey, and that the black people – You
know. What was so beautiful was in our community the black people were
loved. But even when I landed at Black Mountain, it was incredible to me. It just
blew my mind, you know, the separation and segregation. I had a fight with a
redhead on the train as I was coming into Black Mountain. "You know, what's
this about?" and he said, "Well, do you want to go sit in back, you know with the
black people?" "Well, why not?" It just angered me so.
