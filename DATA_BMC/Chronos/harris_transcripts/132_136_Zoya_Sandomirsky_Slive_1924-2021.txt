ZSS: Well, I heard about Black Mountain—I think it was in 1941 or '42—through a
friend of my mother's. See, I was fifteen years old when I came to this country.
This was in 1940. I came from Europe, from—actually a long story, but the last
place we were was in France, and we came just at the time when France fell. We
were very lucky to escape Europe, you know, and we got to the United States.
We first landed in New York and then we moved—It was my mother, my sister,
and myself. My father had already died. So there were three of us, and we
eventually moved to Chicago. I went to a high school, a local high school in
Chicago on the South Side, and I was frightfully unhappy. I mean it was a very
difficult time for me. I had seen war and so on, and I was probably a bit
precocious and shocked by the war. Of course, my peers seemed like babies to
me, and I had a very hard time sort of—Also, I didn't speak any English at the
time—anyway, but adjusting or assimilating. This friend of my mother's, who was
a psychologist, said to her, "You know, the University of Chicago—Zoya just
doesn't fit very well. She's so much more mature than the other students, but you
can enter the University of Chicago on the basis of examinations. You don't need
a high school diploma. So why don't—maybe she could take the exams." Well, I
took the exams and I was accepted to the University of Chicago. But the idea of
going to college at the age of—I probably was about approaching sixteen at the
time, and the idea of I think the university, it was still terribly, terribly
overwhelming to me. So, then this friend—I wish I could remember his name but
I don't—said to my mother, "Well, I know of another place, a very interesting, very
experimental, very progressive college. It's far away. It's in the South. But I think
