MEH: [PRE-INTERVIEW REMARKS NOT TRANSCRIBED] Etta, how did you come
to be at Black Mountain College?
EMD: I lived in the outskirts of New York City in the suburbs. I went to the Brooklyn
Museum Art School. I studied with John Ferren, and he outlined a number of
places I should go. Black Mountain was one of them. So of course I went there.
MEH: Had you finished high school at that point?
EMD: Yes. I had just finished high school.
MEH: And you wanted to be an artist? Was—?
EMD: I guess so. Yes. I did I want to be an artist, yeah.
MEH: Do you recall how you traveled to Black Mountain?
EMD: Yes, I traveled by train. It was a very interesting experience of the racial
situation, because I got off the train and I saw black drinking fountains and
white drinking fountains. It appalled me, being a New York City liberal. I couldn't
believe what I was seeing. I was still a teenager, but I had graduated from high
school.
MEH: So you arrived at Black Mountain. Of the faculty who were there that summer,
were you familiar with any of them?

MEH: What did you study that summer? With whom did you study?
EMD: I studied with de Kooning and Grippe, who was a sculptor, and Cunningham,
and I took a class with—the name is going to escape me. The dome.
MEH: Buckminster Fuller.
EMD: Buckminster Fuller. And there was somebody teaching some kind of Russian
literature, who was very famous, whose class I sort of liked and sat in on.
MEH: I haven't thought of him for a long time, but I know who you're talking about.
EMD: And that, I think that was pretty much it. That's enough, actually! I was busy.
MEH: Did you have any idea when you went to the college what to expect in terms of
the setting or—?
EMD: No. I just—It just seemed like another school to me. I mean there was a lot of
interesting experiences, but I didn't have any presupposition about what it was

to. His whole sense of the scope of the world was totally uninteresting to me, to
tell you the truth. It was just not part of my life. It was definitely more advanced
than I was.

MEH: How was that the case?
EMD: Because it gave you a place to be private during the day, seeing that you all

MEH: Do you remember the Satie play they did that summer?
EMD: Not at all. How interesting. Now thinking about it, they had a vegetable garden

MEH: Are there any other memories that you have? I'm asking you to remember
things that happened fifty years ago. At first I would say, oh, the interview is
over, but then I find memories start to float, you know, if we sort of muddle
around for a while and then maybe something else.
EMD: I guess I should have thought of something. I thought of it before but I haven't,
because I've been busy.
MEH: Okay. Were you aware de Kooning's own work that summer? What he was
doing?
EMD: You're going to have a problem with me, because I knew him afterwards, and I
knew what his work was afterwards also. I had a studio near him—in the early
fifties. De Kooning said something that was very important to me that summer.
Something to the effect that the passion is everything and if one has the
passion, one would make it. He went on to say that I would be fine—a very
sustaining remark. So it's hard for me to say that. He did exude something.
There was no question about it. Or maybe it's just anybody who I thought was
an artist. He was an artist to me. So I must have had some awareness of
something about his work. But I was a total novice. I wouldn't have known what
to think about anything. I'm amazed that I even decided that Albers wasn't for
me. Just goes to show the predisposition was really there, of your direction
anyhow. I'm not really very helpful.
MEH: That's okay. When you left the college, you returned to New York?

MEH: And what did you do then?
EMD: I don't know if that was the year my mother enrolled me in college. She was
always trying to drag me away and make a conventional young lady of me, so I
don't know. It might have been that year. My experiences after that were I went
to college for a year, somewhere along the way. I studied with Hofmann for a
year. That was ended because my father had no more money and I had to go
to work. I went back to college for half a year. I got a studio and started
painting. That's pretty much it.
MEH: And you've been a painter since?
EMD: [AFFIRMATIVE] I think I just sort of found it and that was it. That's what I
wanted to do.
MEH: When did you move to the West Coast?
EMD: Oh, 1971.
MEH: This was after you had married and had your family?
EMD: Yeah. My husband's a psychiatrist, and we got married when he was in medical
school. We moved many times and for many different reasons. And this was
the last move. Sounds rather bland, doesn't it?

whole of New York City—and please tell this to the person who's cataloguing,
because this is something I'm convinced of—there wasn't an artist that I knew,
famous or not famous, that didn't know by the middle of the next week what
Hofmann said the Friday before. And they’d pretend to be indifferent—and they
were all students of Hofmann. All of them. It was amazing. I was a kid, and
these people, a lot of them were ten years older than I was. There was one
person in that class that was at Black Mountain, a young man, who Hofmann
couldn't teach. He was incredibly rigid. He drew like an angel.
