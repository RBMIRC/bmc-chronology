MEH: Okay. We were talking about your coming to Black Mountain, and you had said
that initially the Schmitt parents weren't terribly thrilled with having Betty come
down to Black Mountain.
RS: Right, yeah. She had to scream and yell and holler, and my dad said, "Absolutely,
no," and she just did a lot of screaming and the whole – very traumatic. And he
gave in.
MEH: Apparently they must have realized later that this was a good thing. Did they
scream and yell when you wanted to go?
RS: I don't remember anything about my going. I would imagine that perhaps they felt
it would be good for me to go.
MEH: At that point.
RS: Yeah. Or maybe it would help me to find myself or give me some direction.
MEH: Had they heard anything about Black Mountain? Or was it just the idea of going
to this experimental college in the South?

RS: Gee, I don't know. I do know that Betty and Elaine had gone to college in
Milwaukee, and had been with Ruth Asawa, and, of course, Ruth Asawa was
very special for me, growing up.
MEH: Did she visit your house?
RS: She met Elaine on the first day she went to college in Milwaukee (1943), she met
Elaine, and they became very good friends. Elaine brought Ruth to our house,
and Ruth had been living in a camp for the Japanese Americans, in Arkansas.
So, she'd been in that camp, and then she took a train or bus to Milwaukee, and,
lo and behold, she got to know the Schmitts.
MEH: I'm sure it was important for her to have a family there.
RS: Yeah. Definitely. It's like all of a sudden she really liked me, and I liked her, and
that was nice. She gave me a basket of hers, and that, like so many things,
finally disappeared. I'd love to have that basket. It was the beginning of the
baskets that are all over her house.
MEH: You went to Black Mountain in, say, '49 or '50 and you were there for a year – or
longer?
RS: For less. Second semester for college year 1950-51.
MEH: For less. So why did you leave?
RS: I don't know. One of the biggest sources of information in our family was the
letters that Elaine wrote and Elaine's diaries. I’d like to know. Later I went in the
army in 1952 and began writing very long letters. So, then my letters would be a
source of information on lots of things. I have written one hell of a lot of letters.
But back at Black Mountain, I don't know – Perhaps my letters are somewhere,

MEH: Have to learn to type (LAUGHS) to earn a living, typing for lawyers.
RS: Yeah. I feel good, lately. I've learned to use the computer and I'm learning to use
self-publishing software. When I look at things I've done in my life, what I feel
good about are the creative things. The people I like to talk to, they're the writers
the people who have that passion to be creative. Lots of this other stuff seems
sort of ridiculous. But as far as I go, I'm a very poor guy. I mean, I make very little

MEH: What do you do to earn a living?
RS: I'm a landscaper.
MEH: (LAUGHS!).
RS: For a while I had a fantasy. I mean, I had the illusion that I could be a boss and
I'd have guys working for me. We'd build fountains and have gardens and do
things like Ruth Asawa and Albert Lanier have at their place. Have statues in the
gardens and just all these beautiful things. The reality was people want you to
pick up leaves from the woods and they want you to spray things and kill things.
MEH: And cut back the ivy.
RS: Yeah, cut back the ivy, pick up dog droppings, and do all this. So, to me the work
wasn’t according to my vision, and so I've wanted to get out of it, you know, and
teach or something. With our reunion, with the program, the ideas were so
important to me, the people who know something and have expertise and beliefs
and passion should teach instead of having to take education courses for
certificates.
