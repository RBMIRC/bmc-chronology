LD: —the initial periods of Black Mountain. I first heard about Black Mountain when
I was in Virginia. I was an editor of the Virginia Quarterly Review. And we were

doing a lot of talking at the University of Virginia about other academic
approaches. And it was actually in the Quarterly office that the original St.

Hamilton Holt [NOTE: Holt was at Rollins College. John Andrew Rice was at
Black Mountain.] experiment at Black Mountain. I left Charlottesville in ‘38 and
was in New York. And there again I heard about Black Mountain, primarily, I
suppose, from Alfred KazIn, who became a friend of mine and who had been, I
think, down there in the 30s. I suppose as a teacher. I’m not sure.
MEH: Yes. He was there. It was in the 40s.

LD: Well, that can be easily settled because here’s the one that I received.
MEH: How did Martha come to be at Black Mountain? Not through your influence.
LD: No, no, I had no feeling one way or another, really. As I say, when I first came
down here, because of what I’d heard about Black Mountain from back in the
‘30s and then again from Alfred Kazin and others in New York in the ‘40s, I had
assumed that it would be in association with other educational enterprises
down here. At that time there seemed absolutely no contact that I can make
out. A few people talked about Black Mountain and said, well, it was obvious
that it was—and I got this from Alfred Kazin too—he said that they had no
contact with the local people. They happened to settle in the intellectual capital
of the Baptist and Methodist church down there. It was not a sympathetic
background for them in that sense. And I think there was possibly on the other
side a certain amount of intellectual arrogance on the part of the Black
Mountain people. They looked down on these “hicks.” They saw themselves
surrounded by Billy Carters. The reaction was a mutual disdain, I would say.
That more or less settled Black Mountain, is far as I could make out when I
came here. I was also told it seemed to be going downhill. Then my one and
only contact was when Martha wanted to go there. Where she got it from, I
don’t know.
ID: She read an advertisement in Glamour magazine.
LD: Oh, come on. [LAUGHTER]

ID: That was the summer we were going out West, and Art got her a job in a

LD: So, on our way back from the West Coast—we went to a meeting out there—
we stopped by Black Mountain. This was in ‘55, July ‘55, and frankly I was
appalled at the sort of physical rundownness. There probably were not more
than 12 or 15 people there. I didn’t object so much—I did object to that. But not
only that. But it seemed there was a complete lack of morale. I mean, you can
be very run down you can have a very gung-ho spirit for what we’re doing and
be for it. But it just seemed to me—and it was a very brief spell. We were just
there overnight. But the general feeling I got was simply, we don’t give a damn.
We’re not terribly interested even in what we’re doing. So I brought home to
Chapel Hill just a completely negative feeling about the place. Then Martha—
ID: Remember. Describe the library. That was a shock.
LD: Well, in the library the books were lying on the floor, a rather dirty floor too.
Alright, okay. I’m a publisher. I believe that books should be taken care of. And
this just struck me as being almost criminal negligence with regard to what after
all—the tools of creative work of any kind that involves writing as well as
scholarship. So, as I say, I brought back a very negative feeling. Martha had
gone a year in school then. Did she write? I think she wrote from there and said
she wanted to stay, and I said, “No, come on home.” She did come home, and
we talked a bit about it. I think that was the occasion for my first letter to Olson

LD: Well, that came up, because another thing that Jonathan wanted about that
time. It was separate from publishing his poetry. There were two things. He
wanted us to take over his press—the Jargon Press, and we talked around the
subject for a long time, and finally we decided not to because the virtue of the
Jargon Press is that it’s one man’s judgment. All right, I felt that it was
practically impossible because we’re ruled by a Board of Governors. It would be
a terrible marriage on both sides, that either his independence would be
destroyed inevitably or that we’d just fall into a terrible fight over getting
approval and that defines the difference. The essential spirit of Jargon is its
independence, of being one man’s idea. It can be two men or something like
that, but that spirit is really quite the opposite from that of academic publishing
which in practically every case that I know of involves the committee approval,
scholarly outside referees and this sort of thing, and it just doesn’t work the way
the whatever you want to call—the independent publisher does. They don’t
work together and you need both. It interests me a great deal that the National
Endowment for the Humanities seems to be subsidizing independent presses
like Mulch far more than it is the scholarly presses. And that’s not our affair.
Basil King gets more money than the university presses—the university
presses generally do from the federal government through the National
Endowment. But they both need to exist. But they just are not the same kind of
operations at all. To change the subject a little bit, what will be the relationship
of your work to do Duberman’s work?
