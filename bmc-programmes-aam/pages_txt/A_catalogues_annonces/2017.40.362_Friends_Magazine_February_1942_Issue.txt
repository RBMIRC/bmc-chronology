Black Mountain College
A Striving Example of Democratic Education
A ringing answer to those who say that modern education is not in harmony with modern living can be found in the aims and development of Black Mountain College in western North Carolina. It was founded in 1933 by a determined band of teachers and students who felt that education must adapt the student to the community and the community to the student, so that citizens would live, not by absorbed rules, but by a practical understanding of what the citizen and the community owe each other.
The college is small, but it was designed and built almost entirely by the labor of the faculty and students. No grades and no rigid courses of study are required, yet Black Mountain students are admitted to such august bodies as the Harvard Graduate School. In a multitude of ways, Black Mountain demonstrates the basic and vital truth that real democracy is a living, dynamic force.

No athletics at Black Mountain, but plenty of healthy and fruitful work as students help built their own college. Every student must join in some practical work. Sometimes professional artisans are called in from nearby towns, when work is too skilled for students to do. But they dig ditches, mix cement, hammer and saw. Secretaries work in building that was a summer lodge, pending better quarters. Betty Mosley (left) was secretary to mayor of Dallas, now works for college treasurer.
Robert Babcock conducts class in political science. Classes are so small that every student gets near-tutor attention. There are no grades, no required courses.
Cardinal principle at Black Mountain is no fraternities or other groups where money or social position matters. College holds dances twice each week; all join.
Ingenious Paco Leon makes more room for daytime by designing a bed that raises out of the way. He spent most of his life in Spain, is interested in radio engineering.
Living quarters are cramped, but all fare alike. Each student shares sleeping quarters but has private study. Tuition is $400-$1200, depending on ability to pay. Students do not know what other have paid, so all rank as equals. All keep own rooms neat.
This is completed wing of model shown on opposite page. By helping to build it, all have learned to cooperate, have greater appreciation of value of community effort. College has twenty instructors, only 75 students; hopes to raise that to about 125.
So ingrained in the spirit of democracy that work does not have to be assigned; the first on in the dining room, student or teacher, begins serving food. Connie Spencer balances seven cups. Each tried to out-balance others, but broke too many dishes.

Chase Osborn acts as his own weather man, has two barometers at Possum Polk, his Georgia winter home. His daily readings of weather conditions are kept in diary.

Friends visit CHASE OSBORN the “Iron Hunter” of Possum Polk
The lusty whoop of delight is in praise of some especially good dish served by Lee, his cook. Guests are served liberally, but if they do not eat everything, portions are smaller next time. Chase Osborn even saves soap slivers. “It may mean a few pennies more for charity,” he explains.
Each afternoon, the Governor hauls down the American flag and renders a prayer asking God’s blessing on the people of the United States. He also raises flag each day.
Democratic and truly interested in everyone, the Governor chats with two field hands. He is a sound scholar; in 1939 added 40,000 statistical square miles to Michigan showing that former tabulations had failed to include American portion of Great Lakes.
Several dozen letter a day come to Chase Osborn, and he personally answers all. His daughter, Stellanova, serves as secretary. He puts in a ten-hour working day. This is in line with his maxim: “Apply your principles thoroughly and do no slight small things.”
A fabulous character indeed is Charles Salmon Osborn, one time-Governor of Michigan, intimate friend of Presidents, who rose from a log cabin to the heights, and has returned to a log cabin for the rich final years of his life. Known as the “Iron Hunter,” for the ore-prospecting that earned him many millions of dollars, he gave away nearly all his possessions, now lives quietly at Possum Polk in Possum Lane, Poulan, Georgia, in the winter; spends the summers at Duck Island in Michigan’s St. Mary’s River. He is 82, but still rises at five a.m., does a full day’s work before retiring early in the evening.
“My greatest inheritance was poverty,” says this wise man of his early life in an Indiana log cabin. “It was bitter, but poverty is ambition’s stepladder. With money came reflection. I saw the earth as a vast banquet table, with plenty for all if each would take a fair share, but some were taking more than they needed and others were going hungry. I decided to give back all I had that I did not need to live on. People who are blessed with a surplus should think of themselves as trustees for those who have nothing, and help them up to the table. That day is coming; it is not charity, it is Christian justice.”
Chase Osborn practiced what he preaches. His millions went to churches, charities and schools. And at 82 he is happy, healthful, beloved by his friends, brimming with zest for life. Of thrift, he says, “The best way to save is not to spend.”

Basketball is America’s Biggest Sport and its home is Indiana
Asked to pick America’s favorite sport, most fans might single out basketball, or possibly football, for the crown. Paid attendance, however, tells a different story. Judged entirely by the number of people who lay down hard cash to see what happens, basketball, with more than 80,000,000 paid admission annually, leads the pack. Basketball is a half-century old this winter. In January 1891, a Y.M.C.A. official at Springfield, Mass., Dr. James A. Naismith, called some boys together and explained a new game he had a new game he had thought up, involving tossing a ball through peach baskets nailed several feet above the floor. From that time on, basketball climbed steadily upward in the number and interest of its fans. Few observers today would deny that basketball’s true home is in Indiana high schools. Indianians are not fans- they are basketball-crazy. In town after town in Hoosierdom the gym or coliseum where basketball is played is the town’s largest and finest building. Each year there is a state tournament at Indianapolis, and 15,000 rabid fans jam Butler University field house to cheep the home-town boys. Potential players are watched anxiously from grade school days, and any six-footer not out for his school team had better tell why. Last year’s champions were the Hatchets, of Washington, Ind. Mickey Rooney and Judy Garland RETURN IN “BABES ON BROADWAY”
Friend’s Movie of the Month by Dick Francis
A movie success-formula that has proved its potency twice already is being revived in the picture Babes on Broadway. That formula is to team together Mickey Rooney and Judy Garland, add plenty of music, lots of songs, a plot that doesn’t have to do much more than keep up with the cast, and there you have it. Just such concoctions were Babes in Arms and Strike Up the Band, and both were the box office’s best friend. Busby Berkeley, who took care of directing the first two, is back on the job again, so how can you go wrong?
As you might expect, Babes on Broadway is a merry little bauble of entertainment that moves without limping, from comedy to pathos to trickery to self-sacrifice, and smoothly up to a happy ending. Rooney’s incredibly expressive face and rather explosive brand of likeableness have full opportunity to hold sway, and Judy is once again the talented nice girl who brings him to his senses at just the right moment. There are several front-row tunes, one of the best being How About You? Which Judy dshes out with gusto and assurance.
Plot never means a great deal in these Rooney-Garland get-togethers, and all you need to know is that the story deals with the plight of talented young hopefuls who have a great yearning to see their names in Broadway lights but have a good many problems in getting them there. In this case, a benefit show seems the best way to attract the eye of producer Thornton Reed, capably played by James Gleason. Mickey is all set to run out on the scheme, but Judy’s scorn reforms him and it all works out beautifully for everybody.
Judy Garland does her share of impersonations to keep pace with Mickey. This one is of the great Sarah Bernhardt, Judy also does an encore of her famous Franklin D. Roosevelt Jones.

May be America’s Next Sport Craze
A new sport that seems to have all the elements that make any game popular is bicycle-soccer. Action, thrills, spills, teamwork- this game has them all. Pictures on these pages were made at Ponce de Leon High School, at Coral Gables, Florida, where climate, athletically-minded students and habitual use of bicycles provide a natural setting for one of the nation’s newest games. Basic rules are the same as for soccer, with variations to fit the needs of playing on bicycles rather than on foot. Also, instead of the usual eleven-man team, bicycle-soccer teams seem to work better with an abbreviated six-man team, to cut down confusion and chances of collision.
As in soccer, players are not permitted to touch ball with their hands (except the goalees), but have the added advantage of being able to block the ball with their bicycles. Rules provide that players may not dismount to kick the ball toward goal, but must have one leg on each side of the bicycle. Expert cyclists pedal madly toward ball, coast up to it and kick “on the run,” with legs thus free. At the start, it’s easier and safer to stop before kicking the ball. Anyway, it’s a fast, lively sport, and as such, may well appeal to the younger generation.

TRANSPLANTING A TOWN New Englanders Move Entire Village to New Location
U.S. Army engineers decided to build a flood control dam in the valley of the Pemigewasset River, near Franklin, N.H. this project would have doomed the village of Hill, located in the area to be flooded, had not the villagers decided to move en masse.
Led by Selectmen Theodore Dickerson, Edward Amsden and John Huse, the townspeople worked out all details of planning and financing the moving of Hill to its new location. To assure related architectural design, an Association, in complete control, was formed.
When the Federal Government decided to build a flood control dam at Franklin, New Hampshire, it put the village of Hill literally in the middle. Hill was in the middle of the valley to be flooded, and might have been expected to follow the example of other towns, which simply went out of existence when faced with similar problems. The townspeople decided, however, that they liked one another well enough to tackle the problem of moving the entire village a quarter-mile west, to higher ground, so that their town would still be an entity. For good measure, they decided to make the new village “New England’s Model Town,” with planning and architecture worked out to make it the best-looking, most livable town in the region. Led by the village selectmen, they worked out ways and means of financing the civic cost involved, and did most of the moving and rebuilding work themselves. They became so excited over the idea that they erected this sign,

First Selectman Theodore S. Dickerson, one of the leaders in planning details of new village, is a farmer. Here he gets on with storing his supply of winter firewood.
Houses that could be re-designed to conform to the architecture of the new village were moved in the old-fashioned New England way- two horses operating windlasses providing power. About 75 families live in Hill. Only a dozen houses were moved.
Most houses and other buildings in the old village were left to the wreckers, and brand-new ones built on the new site. The villagers are proud of their new town hall (shown in contrast with the old), which contains auditorium, library, fire station.
All the housewives are enthusiastic- and no wonder- over the spick-and-span new homes and kitchens. Mrs. Charles Willard likes her kitchen especially well. All homes, stores and factories, present and future, are to be similarly modern colonial in design.
Main street in the old village was typical of small New England towns, with all buildings lined along one main street. There was no harmony of house or building design. The residents determined to correct faults in their new town site.

Pop Shaffer’s Animals Grow On Trees
PHOTOGRAPHS BY CHARLES HERBERT FOR FRIENDS MAGAZINE
Clem Shaffer, known as “Pop” to all his neighbors in Mountainair, New Mexico, is a prime example of a man who does just what he wants to do. Sixty-odd, Pop is a blacksmith by trade, owns a hotel that provides his living and leaves him free to follow his engrossing hobby-the creation of Rancho Bonita, stocked with wild animals of weird and wonderful forms, all made from nature’s own materials, just polished a bit.
Gnarled tree trunks and branches, roots, stumps and rocks are Pop’s raw material. What he does is use a boundless and lively imagination to see in these twisted forms a resemblance to some wild animal. Carving away the surplus twigs, etc., and putting them out to weather for a year, he has created more than 150 of these creatures, hopes to complete a thousand before he finishes. People who have seen the “ranch” are amazed at the results. Pop has already spent $10,000 on his zoo, but none of his animals are for sale. It is a hobby pure and simple, and a tourist attraction that always amazes visitors.
In his workshop at Mountaineer, New Mexico, Pop Shaffer puts the finishing touches on one of the queer, animal-like forms his quick eye saw in a part of some tree’s formation.
Last year, more than 12,000 tourists visited his Rancho Bonita, where more than 150 of his completed animals are on display. He says they’re the ones Noah wouldn’t take in the ark.
Children as well as adults are fascinated and delighted by the strange denizens of Pop Shaffer’s wooden zoo. Little Betty Jo Tabet thinks this one to eat from her hand.
This remarkable caricature of an elk, by nature, with Pop serving only as “spotter” of her handiwork, is typical of the strange sights visitors see. He hopes to create many more.
Only Pop Shaffer would have seen a lion’s mane in a tree trunk surrounded by a fungus growth. Properly located branches serve as legs, a scraggly branch for a tail. Pop sawed out a mouth, added realistic teeth- and a fearsome yet instantly recognizable king of beasts is the result. Pop retired from business in 1937, devotes full time to hobby.

ANSWERS TO “How Friendly Are You?” (See page 2)
For each question answered “Yes,” give yourself ten points. Your standing will then be based on your total point score. If your score is 30 or less, you need to give your personality and your approach to life a little overhauling. A bit more unselfishness and willingness to be interested in, and help, others is needed. If you scored between 40 and 60, you’re about average. You have as many friends as most people and are pretty well liked generally. If your score is between 60 and 80, you are blessed with a remarkable gift of winning and holding the friendship of most everyone you meet. You’re lucky, and you deserve it. And if you scored above 80, you top the top. You run Santa Claus a good race for title of world’s best-liked man.
