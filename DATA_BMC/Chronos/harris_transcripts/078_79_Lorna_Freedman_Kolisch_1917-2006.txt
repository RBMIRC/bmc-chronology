LFK: Well, what was remarkable about it, until we realized there was anything that
wasn't—I mean, it was remarkable in the other direction—was that not only the
spirit on the music faculty was so wonderful, but there was this very close
connection between all of the faculty. I mean, everybody participated, and there
was a close—there was a close association between—The artists were just as
interested in the music performances. I don't know if you knew what my
husband had inaugurated that summer with the open rehearsals. I assume that
Marcel might have—
MEH: Yes, I've heard about them, I mean, from everybody. The rehearsals? It's very,
really delightful.
LFK: Well, he inaugurated them—open rehearsals, which, of course, had been done
since then very often, and we did the first Schoenberg Quartet, which at that
time was considered very revolutionary. We had—I'm not sure whether it's eight
or twelve open rehearsals, where it's just a certain section was done each time,
and the score was projected on the screen. I would say almost the whole—the
whole student body was present at almost all of them and really participating in
it. It seemed very impressive at the time that people were going around
whistling melodies from the First Quartet. I mean, now it doesn't seem
remarkable at all, because after all this isn't that advanced anymore, in a
sense.
MEH: Then it was remarkable in this country that people were going around whistling
anything classical, not to mention contemporary music.
LFK: Right.

LFK: Well, I think it was very fine. I do believe that we had never played quartets [the

incredibly good. It was excellent. I can't remember any, you know, major
mistakes. Nothing happened at all, which, you know, certainly could in such a
work.
MEH: Did you perform in the dining hall area? Is that where you gave your
performances?
LFK: I believe so.

MEH: Do you—How would you compare that summer at Black Mountain, not just
musically but in terms of community and relationships and whatever to the next
summer at Kenyon College?
LFK: Oh, I think that had an unusual element to it. I mean. as far as performance is
concerned, it's hard to say. But I think it was something which couldn't be
duplicated, even though we sort of tried. I don't feel the spirit was quite the
same. Well, you see, the conflict—I can't even remember the details, but that
had something to do with the college itself and not that summer. These were
things which were going on, internal problems which had to do with the whole
college during the year, but not specifically for that summer.
There was Black Mountain College, which, you know, was a college which
existed for I don't know how many years. Then this Fritz Cohen—I had had a
feeling it was Fritz Cohen, but it probably was Jalowetz who was, who wanted
to have—He was there permanently, wasn't he, Jalowetz?
MEH: Yes.
LFK: And his wife.
MEH: They were regular faculty.
LFK: They were regular faculty, and they then wanted to have just a special summer
institute, and that worked out only the one summer and then the next summer
they went to another place.
MEH: The Jalowetzes visited Kenyon.
LFK: They visited only, but then Fritz Cohen was the one who—

MEH: Yeah, there had been a split that had nothing to do with the music in the
college, that had to do with just internal factions, more political than anything
else, in the regular faculty. But Fritz Cohen was with the group who left because
of the split, and Heinrich Jalowetz stayed, and they were—Really, Cohen and
Jalowetz had co-organized that '44 summer institute, and so the next summer
there were—In fact, right up through the winter there was a debate whether it
would be at Black Mountain the next summer.
LFK: Right. (OVERTALK) correspondence.
MEH: (OVERTALK) correspondence in this. But there were difficulties because of
those people who split from the college that summer wanted to start a new
college, and essentially they wanted to use that next summer session as a way
to recruit students for their new college. So, therefore Black Mountain, naturally,
was not eager to have it at Black Mountain because they used it to recruit for
their own college. So I mean it was a very difficult split. So, the next summer
they had it at—they had essentially the same music faculty at Kenyon College,
and Black Mountain had its own separate Music Institute and Art Institute at
Black Mountain. Jalowetz did come—This was organized—the second
summer—by Cohen, and Jalowetz did come and lecture but he was really at
both institutes. That's when he—
LFK: Yes, that's what I think I remember. Up to the last minute they were still writing
to Rudy and asking him to somehow get the people together, the two sides.
MEH: It was a difficult split because musically everybody was—there was no musical
split, apparently. But it was a loyalty to different people outside of the music
