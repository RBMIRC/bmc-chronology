MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Pat, how did you come to be at Black Mountain?
PP: Well, that’s an interesting story as a matter of fact because I—When I went to
Queens College, I imagined that this was, you know, a great center of learning
and everything that I wanted to know would be there, and as it turned out they
did have an art department, which is what I was especially interested in,
and—but it was only art history. Shortly thereafter I got a job working for them,
filing their slides and helping them mark things and keeping a little order there. I
immediately started complaining to them that there were no studio courses.
What I didn’t know is that art history had just made into academia as a legitimate
course of study, even a major, and so studio hadn’t been accepted into
hierarchies, yeah? But anyway, they had no head of the department then. I think
that he had left or retired or whatever, and Robert Goldwater was kind of the
person that I reported to and who was the most respected person in the
department—certainly by me. I just was crazy about him, and he was an
extremely quiet, shy man, could hardly put a sentence together except when he
was teaching. Eventually they did get somebody to teach a drawing course and
his name was Barce [PH] Miller, [Bruce, Brice ?] and he was really an illustrator.
A very nice man, but not too interested in art, really. Then they got somebody to
teach design, and really to some extent I feel responsible for pestering them
until they did something. This guy—his name is escaping me—but he was at
Black Mountain in 1948 when I went there.
MEH: As a teacher or a student?
PP: His wife—He sort of came along as a friend of Merce.
MEH: Lippold?
PP: Yes. Anyway, he was teaching this design course, and it was sort of modeled on
the Chicago stuff. It was like “Burn the museums and let’s be modern.” He did a
lot of attacking of the Renaissance. He did the Renaissance. But I didn’t quite
understand what he was saying, because it was unimaginable to me. One day
one of the students kind of piped up and said, after he made a very radical
statement, “But what about the Renaissance?” and he said, “Anybody can go
out on the street and pick out an interesting nose.” Like that took care of the
Renaissance and I said, “Now I don’t know very much but I know better than
that.” I was so indignant. Outraged. I just picked up my books, walked out of his
class, and walked out of Queens. I quit college on that day, because of him.
Coincidentally, at the very same time—this was the spring of 1948, and I had
seen Bill de Kooning’s first show and got so excited that I didn’t know what to do

come. We’ll just have to find a last-minute substitute in New York somehow.”
The instant he said that I knew who it was going to be. Absolute, such surety I
don’t know where I got it from. Do you remember Bodky? His daughter was a
flautist, and we were sort of friendly at that time and she was hearing over the
dinner table the saga of the search—who turned it down and who might. So I
was getting a little bit of this through her, a cliffhanger for me because how could
I be so sure. Of course, finally that’s who it was, and I went down actually on the
day they met him at the train. Somehow, even though I’d never even seen a
picture of him, I knew which one he was. Over there, that guy.

MEH: How did you get there? How did you get from New York to Black Mountain?

MEH: Had you been in that part of the country before?
PP: Well, I’m born in Georgia. So—I know all that red soil, although it wasn’t so red

snakes.
MEH: What is your general recollection of the summer, just in terms of your impression
of the college or its influence on you?
PP: Oh, its influence on me was profound because I went looking for something and
found it there, so it was more de Kooning than it was the college as a whole, but
I think I learned from all aspects of it, frankly. It’s true that, you know, it was a lot
more sophisticated than the kind of blue-collar education I was used to.
MEH: What do you mean by “blue collar education”?
PP: Well, the City University at that time was free—good old days. Very, very good
sch-, very good education, but it’s, you know, they’re commuter schools. People
have jobs while they’re going to school, there’s not the middle-class thing where
you’re “finding yourself,” I don’t think. You really don’t have time for that. So—I’m
not finding the right words for it, but I know it’s natural that the kind of
sophistication and bohemianism that went on at Black Mountain, to which I
hadn’t been exposed except in literature. I’m not that type, actually [LAUGHS].
But it was still interesting to me. I don’t care one way or another. I don’t have
any moral feelings about it, but I thought it was a wonderful school, and I
admired, later admired Albers tremendously for his being able to really hold it
together as well and as long as he did. That struck me a lot, even when I was
there, because I could see how easily it could just disintegrate. Especially since
everybody was sort of on their honor to be doing something and not too many
people did—which I thought was a wonderful idea, but it made me a little
cynical.
MEH: In what way?
PP: Well, even my own friends didn’t like to do their part. That disturbed me. I
thought they should have. I certainly thought I should have, since I was on a
scholarship.
MEH: Did you work on the farm at all?
PP: No, I would have liked to though—being a vergarizen [PH], and we have to talk
about it.

when he first—on the day he arrived at Black Mountain, he walked up to the

college before and he said, “So this is what a college looks like!” [LAUGHS] He
was so impressed. Anyway, I asked him and—a stupid question, of course, but I
assumed he must know something, and he didn’t really, and he said, “Why don’t
you study with me?” and I wouldn’t have dared ask that question. But I said, “Oh
boy,” and, of course. So I did. I actually studied privately with him for awhile, as

some kind of prosperity, and then there was no use for what they were making
and they disappeared or they had expanded and then they would pull in again.
So, there were lots of very cheap, empty lofts after the war. I really think that that
was a really big factor that helped make New York a center of the arts was that
you could come, get a loft for fifteen dollars a month, and struggle somehow
to—and not have to take a job that would suck all the juices out of you in order
to pay your rent. So, of course, this block between 3rd Avenue and 4th Avenue on
Tenth Street was a particularly good one for that. It had two metal stamping
factories, face to face, in the center of the block, on each side of the block, and
you can imagine what that sounded like. Boink. I don’t know about the other
one, but the one on my side had been making things like fountain pen and
lipstick containers before the war and during the war they made bullets. Now

MEH: Where was the Waldorf located?
PP: On Sixth Avenue, just south of Eighth Street. There’s a subway entrance. There
had been a whole series of these cafeterias and automats and so forth that
artists hung out in different neighborhoods, as they moved around the city. Like
around the 20s and then around 14th Street and—This was the last of them. So,
usually, almost every night I’d say that there were a couple of tables put
together, maybe more than that, with a bunch of people sitting around. They
were the regulars and then the ones who stopped in from time to time. So
usually you could find, for instance, Kaldis and Lewiten, who manned the two
corners of Eighth Street so nobody could get by on any kind of business without
their noticing. Nobody could be with anybody without their noticing and reporting
to everybody. So people talked a lot then, and I think one of the reasons is that
you had this situation and also I think one of the reasons is that we were going
through a very big change, and not only was the center moving from Paris to
New York but the kind of painting was changing, and so you were going from
Cubism, say—I know people say Surrealism, but that really wasn’t—It was
because there were a lot of Surrealists interested in Gorky, but most of the
so-called Abstract Expressionists did not like Surrealism because it was bad
painting, frankly, or not painting at all, maybe—and they liked paint. Anyway, I
think when you make a big change like that and you’re feeling out new territory,
sometimes it helps to talk about it a lot. In a way people used to say that you
kind of talk things out of existence in The Club, you know, and if you could talk
about it of course you couldn’t paint it. So, it got rid of a lot of overused thoughts.
You always knew when somebody was new because they would ask a question
about something like, perhaps, commitment. [LAUGHS] You’d know he was
from the Midwest or something. Anyway, what went on in places like the Waldorf
was, I thought—I learned a great deal.
