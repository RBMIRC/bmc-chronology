MEH: Would you want to send a child there for four years?
CSG: No. No. I don't think that they prepare for today's living, at all. You see, back
there, Black Mountain was groundbreaking. It was groundbreaking in that it
was the first college to take blacks in the South—in North Carolina. It didn't
have any set curriculum, as a regular university or college would have—at
least not that I was aware of. I don't even know if they conferred a degree. But
it was groundbreaking. As such, it sort of made up its rules as it went along,
which I don't think would fly in today's world. Things are too different. I
remember there were a group of—I don't remember who, but there were a
group there who, knowing that Black Mountain was the first college to accept
black students, I think had a focus on—I remember there was some course
about the status of black entertainers and the roles that they played in
movies, and how they were stereotyped. This is what? Fifty-some-odd years
ago, which was a little bit, I think, advanced thinking for that time. But there
were a group who were very, very interested in this. Of course, having Carol
Brice down there for the summer—she had a child with her, as I remember.
She had a baby at that point. I'm sure it was a problem for her going into
Asheville or going into the neighboring communities. As far as I know, the
Black Mountain—town of Black Mountain—treated us nicely enough. I don't
