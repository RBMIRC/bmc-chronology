FH: Well, I had a friend who was there, and it sounded very interesting. I don’t
know. I know that Albers suggested to my dealer that I go there. I just can’t
remember the transition from, you know, talking to a friend and his suggesting
I go.
MEH: Who was the friend?
FH: Hilda Terry. She’d been going there about a year.
MEH: Did she live in Philadelphia then?
FH: She did. Right.
MEH: How did you know Hilda?
FH: Well, through the Boston Museum School. We both went there in the twenties.
MEH: Are you from Boston?
FH: Yes, I was born and brought up and went to the school there and then to the
Museum School of Boston.

MEH: Do you remember the general reception at the time to abstract art?
FH: Oh, I think you are always battling something. At that time it was people like
Curry, John Steuart Curry. The sort of stylized American scene was very
prevalent then. It always seems there’s something that’s way out one way –
either over-realistic or under. There’s always something that seems to be
catching the eye. But I don’t mind that. I think sometimes it’s not too good to
be too popular.
MEH: That’s interesting.
FH: Well, I think some dealers are demanding you paint faster, and – I don’t know
– I don’t still think it agrees with people.
MEH: I think that’s true.
FH: I mean, I can’t think of anybody who was popular but they didn’t go downhill.
Even Picasso. Of course, there’s always isolated cases. I mean, Miro, even he
almost got too simple. You know, he was very complex as a young man. Then
it was fine. But then toward the end it really didn’t seem to be as satisfying as
his middle period or early.

MEH: What about – what was your general impression of the college.
FH: Well, I think, I’ve been saying that I thought it was hard for them to – They
really didn’t have enough money. It was all things they couldn’t help. But I think
it was certainly pleasant. It was very uncommercial. You know, it was just not –
you didn’t feel it was organized, and you had to do something at certain times.
It was casual. It was nice. I think they did have evening get-togethers which
were pleasant. As I say, it was a long time ago.
MEH: Did you feel that the students took their work seriously?
FH: Well, I think just like teaching anywhere, some do and some don’t. I think the
teachers that I was teaching were more satisfactory. You know, they were
there because they wanted to learn something. You have to realize – I don’t
know, but I know it was true at Cornell – People would take art because they
only needed three credits.
MEH: Credits?
FH: Well, so they take art. They didn’t need any more than three. So, naturally,
there were a lot of people who weren’t too crazy about art. But there was no
feeling of that – which I call commercial. More credits than needed was too
much hard work.
MEH: That’s one complaint I’ve heard from teachers – as I’m talking now – one
complaint that everybody makes who is teaching now –
FH: Yes.
MEH: – is that students are only interested in credits. They want to know how many
points.
