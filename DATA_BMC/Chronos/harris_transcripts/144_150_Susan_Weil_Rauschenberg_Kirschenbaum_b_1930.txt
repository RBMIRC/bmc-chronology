very best solution, because it was a place that I just loved. I went to visit it
when I was looking at colleges, and I loved it. And to me, it was my chance to
do exactly what I wanted to do, and I was literally in college

specific way of teaching you to be an artist. It was sort of contemporary
academy. And the classes were quite formal. We had work to do, to bring to
class each day. And we had specific areas we would study. The drawing class
was one thing, and the color class was another. But in that color class, we
would work on these studies of making colors vibrate, and making one color
look like another, and all this. The thing that I found Albers was most firm
about that one had to have the attitude of a student and not think of oneself
grandly, and not be interested in your work as you would be if you were a
working artist, but that being as student was a specific frame of mind. And that
was very different that anything I’d ever had before.
MEH: What do you think about this particular attitude?
SW: It was sort of a waiting time. In other words, you were supposed to hone up
certain skills, and then wait around to figure out who you were later. Actually, I
did learn to draw a lot better at Black Mountain. I don't think too much of what I
learned in my classes there really carried over into my work. But I think what I
got out of it a great deal was just the environment and being around other
people who were thinking they would one day be artists. And, I mean, I did get
a great deal out of it, but I don't think too much of the specific Albers teaching
really carried over into my work as it developed later.
MEH: Were you interested in other classes in art? I think there was weaving being
taught and....
SW: Yes. But not other art classes really.

MEH: Like what other classes?

Mountain. You could explore all these areas that you normally wouldn't have
had an opportunity. I always regretted that I didn't study photo graphy with
Hazel. She's a terrific photographer, a very good teacher. And later I had to
teach myself, you know, and that was very annoying, to have had it there all
the time and to be too young and dumb to take advantage of it.

MEH: Yeah. What...? You said that you don't feel that Albers' work has had any direct
influence on what you're doing. What do you think was the importance of Black
Mountain then, if any, to what you've done since?
SW: Oh, I don't think it directly effects the look of my work right now, but it was a
real learning experience. And it was the first time I had put together in my head
the idea of any kind of disciplines being connected with learning about being
an artist, because even in Paris at the Academy Julian, the criticism was just
about the quality of your work and not there is a way to learn to be an artist. it
never occurred to me there was a way to learn to be an artist. And it was… I
spent most of the year trying to sort out in my head the way I had started, the
teachers that I had had before and the attitudes, as opposed to the Black
Mountain, which was more academic in a very contemporary sense. And I
think it was very valid for a lot of people. I was busy reacting against it when I
was there. And I suppose a lot of it I sorted away. I particularly loved drawing,
and I guess I got a lot out of that. But I think it was fine for a lot of people, and I
think Albers was a very fascinating person. And I just never knew anyone who
felt so firmly that they knew the way other people should learn, and that they
were right and it was the only way.
MEH: Yes. There was something on my mind. How would you compare the type of
teaching there to what was being done at... you said you went to the Art
