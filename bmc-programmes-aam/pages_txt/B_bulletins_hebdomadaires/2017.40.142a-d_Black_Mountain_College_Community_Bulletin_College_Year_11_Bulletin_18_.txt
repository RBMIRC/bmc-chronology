BLACK MOUNTAIN COLLEGE COMMUNITY BULLETIN
College Year 11 Bulletin 18
Monday, February 7, 1944.
CALRNDER FOR THE WEEK:
The Discipline Committee will meet this afternoon at 12:45 in the Round House for thirty minutes to discuss the visiting in the Studies Building of people who are not members of the College Community.
The Board of Fellows will meet this afternoon at 4:00 o’clock in Study 10.
The Faculty will meet at 5:00 o’clock in Study 10 to continue discussion on the Winter Quarter class registration of students.
The International Relations Club will meet this evening at 6:45 o’clock in the Lobby of North Lodge to continue the discussion on “The Post-War Treatment of Nazis.”
The Faculty and the Student Officers will meet in Study 10 on Tuesday afternoon at 4:30 o’clock. The entire meeting will be devoted to a discussion on Black Mountain College, with Dr. Charles Lindsley acting as Chairman of the discussion. (See excerpts from letter from Dr. Lindsley in this Bulletin)
The students will hold their regular weekly meeting in the Lobby of North Lodge on Thursday evening at 7:00 o’clock.
Dr. Edwin G. Boring, Professor of Psychology and Head of the Psychology Department at Harvard University, will address the College Community on Thursday evening at 8:00 o’clock in the Dining Hall on “The Psychology of the Soldier.”
The Entertainment Committee announces that there will be neither concert nor dramatic production on Saturday evening, February 12; that, instead, there will be an informal talk on “Music in Nazi Germany and Soviet Russia” by Dr. Walter Rubsamen of the University of California at Los Angeles. Dr. Rubsamen is at present engaged on a book about politics and music.
ANNOUNCEMENTS:
The Black Mountain College production of “The Importance of Being Earnest”, scheduled for production at Western Carolina Teachers’ College on Saturday evening, February 12, has been “postponed indefinitely.”
At the meeting of the A Capella students on Sunday evening in the Lowinskys’ Living Room the music of Bach was compared with the music of Handel. Gretel and Edward Lowinsky played a violin-piano sonata of each composer, then Edward Lowinsky played a piano suite of each composer.
$145.00 in cash has been contributed to the Music Fund. There is also a pledge of $100.00 for next year.
Eric Bentley is under contract to finish in the next few weeks his book, A Century of Hero Worship. Excerpts appear in the Winter Number of Accent under the title: “Notes on Carlyle, Nietzche, and Stefan George.”
The paper on “Economical Costumes for Amateur Theatricals”, written by Marilyn Bauer and Jane Slater and read by them at the Western Carolina Drama Institute on Saturday, will be used in a Spring issue of The Carolina Stage, published at Chapel Hill.
Ted Dreier’s letter on “An Opinion As To The Necessity of a National Service Act” appeared in the Saturday, February 4 issue of The Asheville Citizen. In this same issue appeared also an editorial commenting on Ted’s letter.
Edward Lowinsky has been asked to contribute an article for a forthcoming book to be entitled Music in the Renaissance.
At the Tuesday meeting of the Faculty last week Irene Sagan was admitted into the Senior Division. Irene has returned to New York where she will continue her work in chemistry and biology at Queens College.

In the Mail:
Larry Fox writes from Camp Endicott in Rhode Island: “I am taking part in the broadcasts. Last week I played the Captain of an American oil tanker. In this coming show I retain my rank, Captain, but change the brand of my Navy. That is, in next week’s broadcast the part I play is that of a German submarine Captain. It requires that I speak a few German words and roll my r’s when using English ones. The director is pleased with my accent.”
Lieutenant Jack Kasik writes: “Greetings to..all the College from me in Morrie England..The countryside is very much like our New England states, only it is in miniature and lacks trees; the homes are small, neat, and everything around them were tended. The weather is cold, damp and foggy. The people are reserved but friendly…”
Mendez Marks writes from New York City: “Erik Haugaard was in town in furlough recently. He had had a narrow escape..had been in a plane crash and the man with him had been killed. Erik, fortunately, had suffered only from shock, and is once more hale and hearty. Harriett Engelhardt, who has a job in Canada with one of the world’ outstanding endocrinologists, whose name escapes me at the moment, was also here on her way home for a jaunt.. Morris Simon was in recently, looking fine and getting a great kick out of the Army’s psychopathic cases. Renate Benfey was here recently visiting Irene Sagan on a few days sojourn from Boston; we all had lunch together when they went to see ‘Carmen Jones’.”

In the Mail:
Charles Lindsley writes from Charlottesville, Virginia: “One of those strange coincidences has just occurrer. The day after deciding to go down to Black Mountain, as I wrote last week, I was asked to talk to a joint meeting of the faculty club, Phi Beta Kappa and Sigma Xi on Black Mountain College!
At the University of Virginia, as at so many other universities and colleges throughout the country, the faculty and administration are critically examining their curricula, their requirements for degrees, their whole educational philosophy in order that they may be prepared to meet the inevitable changes that will follow the conclusion of the war. It was for the prupose of learning what if anything the philosophy and experience at Black Mountain might contribute to a better understanding of the problems here that I was asked to speak informally.
I have accepted the invitation, though I shall make it clear, of

Course, that I no longer have any connection with the college. The occasion next week to refresh my knowledge as to what the college is doing seems most opportune. But it occurs to me that I might be able to bring back to the meeting here something of more value from my visit if anyone feels it worth while giving me a little time. Let me explain what I have in mind.
“I have always felt that there could be two rather distinct contributions that BM might make to education in America. One is to develop a new way in education, a pattern that could be followed more or less closely by other institutions; this is probably the most obvious contributions. But in the nature of things, institutions that could make direct application of BM principles and practice would have to be small. Certainly the pattern could not be followed even approximately by institutions with several thousand students, even if faculties, board of trustees, or controlling legislatures came to accept that pattern. Does BM have anything constructive to offer to those large, established institutions? This questions leads to the second contribution I believe it can make. It can discover and rediscover and develop principles in education which are universal in their validity and application; it can be a kind of pilot plant in which general problems can be studied and worked out in order that the larger units may be better designed and operated.
“Now it seems to me that while the faculty at Virginia may have a mild curiosity as to what goes on at Black Mountain, the topic that would be of greatest interest and value to them is: have you found out anything there that we can use here, with our two thousand and more students and with the restrictions placed upon us by our organization and control?
“While I feel that it is this aspect of the BM experiment and experience which would most interest them, I also feel that answer to such a question, or the attempt to find answers, would be of value to the college, teachers and students, as well. I know attempts have been made to answer this question in times past; it is one which might be reconsidered frequently. It is with this in mind that I make the following suggestion: Some time next week, when I am at the college, I would like to meet with any teachers and students who would be interested in discussing the matter. In this way I could gain new knowledge and insight into the objectives and methods of the college, and so be of more value to my listeners here. And I hope that at the same time the discussion would not be entirely profitless for you.
“Of course the feasibility of the suggestion depends entirely upon the interest and available time of those at the college. I simply mention it now so that if there are any who would like to participate, arrangements could be made and thinking about the question could be going on before I arrived. The time could be anytime Monday or Tuesday, so far as I am concerned..”

COMMUNITY WORK SUMMARY:
(Week of January 31 through February 5)
Wood chopping began being really effective this week. Crews worked hard and were well organized, Because of the danger in the mica mine the miners are now working full time on the wood lot.
Six pounds of mica were trimmed and split.
The stalks of corn from the Grove Stone Field were taken to the Farm to be used as fodder.
At the Magnesium Plant grading, cement mixing and pouring, and painting and staining were carried on by student workers.
Coal hauling went on as the need of various cottages arose.
Dirt from the excavation for the Magnesium Plant was hauled to the Studies Building where it was used for filling in underneath the building.
We hope to be able to complete the terrace at the Studies Building sometime this Spring. ---Reported by Nell Goldsmith
From the Farm came: nine bushels of potatoes and 462 quarts of milk. ---Reported by Janet Heling
