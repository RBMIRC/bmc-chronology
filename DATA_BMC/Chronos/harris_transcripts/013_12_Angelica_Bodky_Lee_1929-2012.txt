MEH So it’s recording.
ABL I remember THE Strauses, of course. They were friends of my family and were
over a lot.
MEH: Going back, because I mean listing names is okay but I really want to know
more with the name, that you can remember.
ABL: Well, with Fannie Hillsmith—
MEH: Okay, going back to Straus. The Strauses. What age were they?
ABL: Fifties, I’d say.
MEH: Fifties, okay. What were they like? Do you have any particular memories? Or
descriptions?
ABL: Cheerful. Yeah, and lively and—I think this is what did the whole community a
lot of good, being able to talk. Everybody could kind of put the awful
experiences behind them and look forward and help the college go. I was
aware of a lot of strife that was going on in the meetings, I think, but it didn’t
concern me. And reading in the book I realize that my father was very much
torn between loyalties between both factions. He loved everybody, and hated to

see all this push and pull, which happens in academe a lot—which is too bad.
But Fannie Hillsmith and I had a long argument about mountains. She said they
are triangles, and I said they are not [LAUGHS]. You know, I was a kid. I didn’t
see things as triangles, and Fannie Hillsmith, of course when you see her
paintings you realize what she’s doing and I admire her work very much. But
then I didn’t. Now I do. Yeah.
MEH: Do you have any particular memory of what she was like as a person in the
community?
ABL: Quiet. Yeah. Said things quietly. But strong, yeah. But she didn’t say much. I
think she—She was there to teach, and she did her work there. I was never in
her studio. I don’t know. Albers was also quietly hidden in his house, did his
work. My parents saw more of it. Ma was appalled that he used a ruler to paint,
but of course [LAUGH] he had to.
MEH: If you want a straight line, you have to. [LAUGHTER]
ABL: Yeah. He was one who did not want to rule the roost. He wanted to do his art
work and teach, and not run the college. I think much against his will he
became head of it for a while, you know, towards the end. And then he was
glad. When he was not, he came to my father and said, “What is to become of
me?” I think there was suddenly nothing ahead for him in the future.
MEH: Certainly no prospects of income.
ABL: No. Dad said, “Just wait, you’ll be kicked upstairs.” And he landed at Yale. I
remember the expression “kicked upstairs.”

MEH: Is that a translation of a German phrase that’s used, or is that just your
father’s—
ABL: I think my father was just thinking, you know—Then there was a whole group
here, Paul Matthen , the singer, with his wife, Joan. And Klaus Liepmann, a
violinist with his wife—
MEH: Were those people that you had known here?
ABL: Yeah. They were invited down one summer to play music, and Matthen did a lot
of cantatas then. That was his specialty, sort of.
MEH: Are any of these people still living that you know of?
ABL: Klaus Liepmann is, and I think he’s in Westport.
MEH: Westport, Mass?
ABL: Mass. Yeah. And Harold Sproul. He’s not living any more but his former wife is.
MEH: If I were to send you like just a list of the people from the summers, the
musicians, you know, with some space, would you annotate if you know the
person is dead or if you know they’re living or where they might be?
ABL: Sure. Of course, Frances Drinker was doing the flute parts for the cantatas and
so forth. I think that’s about most of the people that—Did you have any more
questions?
MEH: If you had any more thoughts about particular things that happened at the
college, any events or—?
ABL: Not really, I don’t think.

MEH: It seems to me that the hikes—this comes up continuously from people, you
know, hiking in the mountains, and it seems that that was—in that sort of
activity—so much education took place. Conversation.
ABL: And right behind the college was a very steep trail up the mountain and back,
which we often went with Max Dehn.
MEH: In groups, or alone, or both?
ABL: No, in small groups, three or four or five. Whoever came along. He said, “I’m
going to the Garden of Eden,” and that was just about underneath the top of the
mountain, in a pasture, and we often saw Black Angus cows that were raised
for meat, I think. They just roamed around. And we were told don’t pay any
attention to them. They’re not always that friendly, but they’ll leave you alone.
The Garden of Eden.
MEH: The Garden of Eden. That’s wonderful. And the Garden of Eden was just
where?
ABL: Right behind the college, way up high.
MEH Was it like a wooded area, or a flat area?
ABL: There was a pasture there, and you could sit down and admire the view. There
were not that many trees there. That was a wonderful thing to do sometimes
when things got too hot or when people got too involved—and go and look at
the flowers. And Max Dehn knew them all, and then he’d talk about various
things. There was also the creek, which was a very important part and which
had lovely flowers growing along it—behind the college. Because I walked the
dog a lot, I was much more aware of all these little places, the little picnic areas.

And these were places that people could go and philosophize and enjoy, away
from academe. Yeah. Then the view also towards Grandfather Mountain on the
right, and they were building the highway across—you could see the Blue
Ridge Parkway being, going right across the mountains that you could see way
back, and that was sort of exciting. Later on we went on that highway home,
which was quite new. So the mountains played a very strong part. And this was
probably true especially when it was originally at Blue Ridge, Robert E. Lee
Hall. We went there to visit—it was very impressive. Have you seen it?
MEH: Yes.
ABL: Those columns. Unbelievable. Is it still there?
MEH: Yes.
ABL Thank God. [LAUGHS] I think that’s about all I have.
MEH: I meant to ask you before. How did you get to Black Mountain? Did you say you
took the train down?
ABL: Yeah. The first year. Then the next year we took the car, and it took us three
days. And these days you could do it probably in a day and a half, with the
highways.
MEH: But from Massachusetts, that was a long drive.
ABL: It was a long drive, and Route 1 wasn’t—But we actually went byways. We
were interested in the Amish, you know, and going through Lancaster in
Pennsylvania, [UNINTEL] and so forth, and stopped sometimes—We had a
cousin in Washington, D.C., and stopped to see him. And so the trip was an
actually leisurely visiting thing.

MEH: Do you have any other memories of Albers’ class? You said you sat in on it
without taking it formally?
ABL: Yeah, I—Well, he analyzed things very thoroughly, very critically, and nobody
was ever really hurt because a lot of Germanic teachers teach and kids go out
sobbing. I’ve seen it at the Museum School, when a German painter could
come down like an ax. Albers explained everything so thoroughly, and
everybody in the class learned from what he criticized in a piece of work. And it
was always a very positive thing. And that I enjoyed, especially, you know, and
learned a lot.
MEH: Do you have any particular memories of mealtimes at the college?
ABL: Very lively. [LAUGHS]
MEH: In what respect?
ABL: Because—Lively discussions. People sort of congregated. There were eight to
a table, and people somehow were groups (?). I think I squirted a watermelon
seed at Josef Marx once. Oh, boy, did I get a lecture in front of the whole
college. [LAUGHS] But he did some wonderful chamber music with us, but
could lord it over sometimes, and he also lived above me and stomped around
a lot, I [UNINTEL] sleep, and so there was a legitimate excuse for squirting the
watermelon seed, when he was making a pompous statement [LAUGHS].
Yeah, he was that way. Oboe players are. They are a special breed. [LAUGHS]
But a good teacher too. Yeah, I learned a lot with the chamber music.
MEH: So were you really taking the music classes then, or sitting in on them, or—?

ABL: I sat in on the classes and some of my father’s. He organized a group of
winds—wind players—to do some quintets and things. That was just side—
Fun, yeah. But a learning experience just the same as any session is.
[CUCKOO CLOCK] Is that recorded?
MEH: Yes.
ABL: Good! Thank you! [LAUGHTER]
MEH: It gives a particular flavor to the—It runs throughout, the cuckoo clock.
ABL: Gosh. Those are all the memories I really have.
MEH: Okay. [IRRELEVANT REMARKS NOT TRANSCRIBED]
ABL: Yeah, Max Dehn was a wonderful personality. The perfect personality for the
college. He was very levelheaded, and at the same time if you sprained your
ankle or cut something, he was the one to call and give his learned opinion
about whether you should be taken to Asheville or not. Yeah. It was such a
comfort. And then later his wife came, and she was a great cook. A baker. As
Mrs. Jalowetz was too.
MEH: What about—was it you who raised the name of Peter Grippe as a person you
remember studying with, or was that someone else?
ABL: Yeah.
MEH: What do you remember about him?
ABL: Well, he kind of made you do the clay work like he does it. I mean it was sort of
building, but it didn’t make sense to me at the time. Now it would. But then
again the college was more really for graduate students, especially the summer
sessions in the arts are not for painters like me. But I enjoyed seeing him work.

He had a wonderful facility with the clay, and Mrs. Grippe was a fascinating
person too. I think he was a great asset there. Then later he appeared at
Brandeis close to us and we saw more of him, teaching clay at Brandeis. He
didn’t carve. He just worked the clay.
MEH: So, then he taught at Brandeis when he moved here.
ABL: Yeah, when Brandeis started—I think the Music Department started before the
Art Department. Yeah. That was one of the earliest ones there. Bernstein
inaugurated the Music Department and then left for his conducting tours and
things. Dad carried on the grunt work.
MEH: Would it be accurate to say, or not—and this is something that really could be
historically looked at—that the crux of these departments that were being
started, the Music Department, was it primarily refugees, or was it Americans?
ABL: At Brandeis, or—?
MEH: Brandeis. Or generally the Collegium Musicum here in Cambridge.
ABL: It was both. Yeah, very much both.
MEH: Can you describe—Describe Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge.
ABL: Elizabeth Sprague? Well, she was a big lady, and very influential in
supporting—and yet at the same time quiet. She supported all these things,
and when she arrived with her entourage, it was a big moment. After (?)
silence. Everybody respected her enormously, and the family of course was a
big family around here. Strong at supporting Shady Hill School. Then, as I said,
John Coolidge was head of the Fogg for a while. The kids went to Shady Hill
School. Then they had a summer place in Manchester, a whole peninsula of big

MEH: Any more, any other things? Do you think there are any topics we haven’t

ABL: I can’t think offhand right now. Each year was—each summer was just a little
bit different because of the different personalities that came. Those
personalities then went and carried on in New York or somewhere and became
great, and so each summer had its particular flavor, which was fascinating. The
lunches were exciting because friends from let’s say around here would gather
and have a beer.
MEH: Did the German community at Black Mountain—did they basically speak
German among themselves, or English?
ABL: Sometimes. Both. It depended who was there. If there were English speaking
people there, of course, everybody spoke English. If two Germans were
meeting each other, well that was different. I don’t know whether Dad and
Jalowetz spoke German. There were Germans, Austrian refugees. De Kooning

MEH: I don’t think.
ABL: Yeah. It suited her perfectly. [LAUGHS] I think that’s about all I have. Yeah, the
people who printed are Williams and his wife, Vera. She was a very hard
worker, very quiet. Paul Williams.
MEH: Were you at all involved with the Print Shop activities?
ABL: No, but I walked by there all the time and admired his work a lot. He had to do
all the printing.
