In the Mail:
Private Neil Goldsmith writes from Florida: "I'm drafting in the Army. Yes, when I got down hero in Homestead, the Classifi-cation Examiner discovered my architectural training sonawhore in my paper and pounced upon me with glee. He almost made it sound as though they had been waiting for someone with that type of experience. I work in the drafting section, doing all kinds of jobs, some vaguely connected with drawing and architecture, and some not so far off. I've drawn several plans for alterations in present buildings. The plans have gone through, and the buildings are in the process of being changed now. Makes me feel just a little important... The recent hurricane that you must have read about missed us the last minute -- after we were evacuated and, everything. It was very exciting, but almost a disa-ppointment to come out of our 'shelter' (the Power and Light Company in Homestead) after a night of semi-sleep amid pounding machines and find no houses strewn about, no water to wade through, not oven a fallen tree -- in fact, no excitement at all. I guess we were lucky to get just the edge of it.... I'm finding Homestead a swell base to be on. The follows hero don't like it because they've been stuck here too long; but what with a nice bunch of girls to live with, a super C. O., and Miami thirty miles away (with an ocean to swim in), I find it plenty O.K. Nothing like spending a winter in Florida."
Sergeant Pete Hill writes from the Dutch East Indes on October 13: "I have done some wandering since last writing, namely from New Guinea to an Isle in the Dutch Indos. I'm an infinitesimally small unit of the force that landed on this undisclosable island some weeks ago. I disembarked from my L.C.T. in which I made the journey from New Guinea at H-Hour plus twenty-five minutes. It was all very interesting and exciting with such notables as Douglas MacArthur swelling our ranks and presumably praising. our behavior. I made the landing in great luxury, with a cot (which a truck very obligingly carried in for me), a copy of Shakespeare, and a half-dozen bars of wonderful melt.-proof tropical chocolate. Since then, my condition has steadily im-proved. I have a new sturdier cot, several books, and moro chocolate. Could man ask for more?.... We have our air raids almost every night, but they are quite light and more often than not are more a good fireworks display than anything else."

Private Isaac Nakata writes from somewhere in France on October 13: "I have travelled a lot in France, yet I have really seen very little of it. The army travels rapidly in rear areas, and it takes a little time to get the 'atmosphere' of a country and its people. The cities and the sizable towns are well-kept and compare favorably with those in our Midwest. The country, for the most part, is agricultural. Ripe, bright, orange pump-kins dot the countryside.. We saw, in our journeying across the country, the wreckage and debris of Jerry equipment near roads -- helmets, gas masks. Flotsom in the wake of war!...  The woods here are quite thick. The flora is something like that around Lake Eden, but with more evergreens. It is wonder-ful outdoor country for the summertime, with its wooded hills and mountains and its numerous streams. I haven't seen any wild animals yet.... Food, except vegetables and fruits, is not ample. The vino is sour, and the beer is somewhat flat in rear areas; this indicates a fresher vintage.... It's a small world & Recently I met Don Page coming out of the San Carlo Theatre in Naples.. It's rainy and cloudy here. I'm quite well. I cut down a fir tree this morning for a fire. Now we can't fire. Orders"
STUDENT DECISIONS: At a meeting on Wednesday, October 25, the Students chose Bill McLaughlin Student Coordinator to work with Molly Gregory, the Faculty Coordinator, of the Community Work Program and defined his work as follows:
a). to shift labor to the areas where it is most urgently needed, b). to delegate particular responsibilities to the sub-coordinators, c). to serve as a clearing house for information about all new or extra jobs that need to be done.
The Students decided that the sub-coordinators will generally assume responsibilities for routine jobs.
NEWS ITEMS OUT OF LAST WEEK: "The Dumbarton Oaks Agreement is a realistic document falling short of the high goal that many people had hoped for it," said Dr. Herbert Miller in addressing an audience of students and teachers on Wednesday evening in the Lobby of North Lodge. "In this respect it is wise. Social and political change has to wait for social evolution. We shall yield sovereignty, not by talking about it, but by finding that we are living under new conditions. We move towards 'One Worlds rather than have it imposed upon us. We must share in the approach to it by support-ing moves in that direction. It is not a question of perfection or nothing; it is a question of adopting the basic principles of Dumbarton Oaks or the destruction of civilization."
Dr, Miller pointed out that the Dumbarton Oaks Agreement is an outgrowth of the Hague Conference for Arbitration held in 1899 at the instigation of the Czar of Russia who hoped by it to reduce armaments and thereby prevent future wars. "This Conference failed. It was followed by the Permanent Court for International Justice and the League of Nations, now the United Nations. Both the organizations failed. Now we have a logical successor, with the same objectives: but it undertakes to stop the gap which made all the other attempts fail. It provides a means of com-pelling compliance with decisions."

Dr. Miller described the Dumbarton Oaks Agreement. "It is a skeleton which must be filled out by the hard way of experience and compromise. It has been written during the war and must be accepted before the nations have a chance to concentrate on their local problems and lose their present consciousness of interdependence."
The talk by Dr. Miller was part of a program of the College to begin to get the students there to develop "world vision". ***
The Junior League of Parkersburg, West Virginia, has just con-plc-bed arrangements with Betty Kelley for producing her first play, "Moro Straw for the Scarecrow", for the children of Parkers-burg. No definite date has been set for this production, but it is understood that several presentations will be made by a troupe of actors that will tour the schools of Parkersburg, a city of thirty thousand pooplo. Betty wrote the play especially for the Asheville Children's Theatre. It was first produced at the Plaza Theatre in Asheville on April 29 of this year. Later performances of it wore given in the auditorium of the Stephons-Loe High School for the Negro elementary school children and in the auditorium of the Grammar School in Black Mountain for the school children of this nearby town. Rehearsals are now under way of Act I of "An Almost Lonely Christmas", Betty's second play written for the Asheville Chil-dronts Theatre. This Christmas fantasy will be produced at the Plaza Theatre on December 2.

The students and teachers of Black Mountain College went to the opera on Saturday evening, to an especially arranged performance of Mozart's "Don Giovanni". This performance was planned and executed by Heinrich Jalowetz, Fritz Hansgirg, Anni Albers, and William Rood.
The vocal and instrumental music for the opera was furnished by Dr. Hansgirg from his collection of recordings of outstanding European productions of Mozart's operas. While the music was being played, miniature cardboard scones, created by Mrs. Albers and Bill Reed, and mounted in front of the audience, followed the dramatic action of the play,
Dr. Jalowetz, an authority on the music of Mozart, briefly ad-. dressed the opera goers before the playing of the overture of the Mozart opera.
