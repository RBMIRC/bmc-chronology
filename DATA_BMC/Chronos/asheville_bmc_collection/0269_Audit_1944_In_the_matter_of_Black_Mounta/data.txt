William Morse Cole

Harvard University
Cambridge, Massachusetts
November 14, 1944
In the matter of Black Mountain College
To whom it may concern:
I have today completed the eleventh annual detailed audit of the accounts and records of the Corporation of Black Mountain College, I have examined its books of account, its records, and its property, have lived in one of its residential halls, eaten in its commons, and participated as far as time would allow in its activities during the conduct of the audit. For the tenth time I find its books and records carefully kept, and its expenditures not only supported by adequate vouchers but also, in my judgement after a lifetime of university teaching, justified by its educational policy. Proper accounting procedures have been employed.
I hereby certify that the accompanying statements (I, Summary Statement of Income and Expenses for the tenth year of its existence, September 1, 1943- August 31, 1944), and II, Summary Statement of Financial Condition, August 31, 1943), are in accord with its books of account as of the dates named, and, in my judgement, give a correct picture of its finances.
(Signed) William Morse Cole

1This is in addition to housing, heat, light, and meals, for members of the faculty and their immediate families. The cash salaries averaged $640.48; but the distribution of this is adjusted to the number of member sin a family. In some cases both husband and wife are on the faculty, and in other cases the additional salary is based on the additional number of members in the family; so the same allowance is made for non-teaching as for teaching members of the faculty family. The average per person of a faculty family was $420.31. the provision of meals and housing for all members of faculty families at cost to the College raises the effective compensation to about $1353 per faculty member, and about $888 per member of a faculty family. The conditions of living at the College (the immediate mountain surroundings, the climate, the community spirit, and the direct personal contact with students), appeal to many as better worthwhile than greater financial return elsewhere. Indeed, the economy of community living, as compared with family living, makes even the financial return greater than appears by the figures. Nevertheless, since the College is still in the throes of finding itself, it must find means soon to raise salaries in order to free its faculty from unwholesome financial concern. In the first year of the College, the cash drawing of member of the faculty averaged by $87.27. in the first ten years covered by these audits, the average cash drawing was $830 per year, and has never exceeded $1250 per year. The faculty itself, through a committee on which the study body has faculty itself, through a committee on which the student body has a representative, controls the budget.

If, then, the College had collected full fees ($1200 for tuition and board) from all students, it would have had not only no deficit but a net operating income independently of gifts of $7,000. In years of large enrollment, with the resulting gain from operating on a larger scale, the ratios have been ever higher. These reductions of fee have enabled the College to adjust each student’s fee to his ability to pay, so that it has attracted students representing a cross-section of the country, both economic and geographic, and can elect those whom it deems most promising.

The amount here shown is the cost to the College, but does not include any figure for the labor of students or faculty, though both students and faculty have worked part time for many months, over several years, under the supervision of an experienced architect member of the faculty and of an experienced paid builder. This has been part of the regular college program, because such work is deemed an improvement over the usual college athletic activities, an element in cultivation of community spirit, and a factor in establishing an appreciation of some of the problems of labor. The economic value of such unpaid labor is suggested by the fact that professional appraisers recently set a figure, for insurance purposes, of over $150,000 for the buildings now owned by the College.
