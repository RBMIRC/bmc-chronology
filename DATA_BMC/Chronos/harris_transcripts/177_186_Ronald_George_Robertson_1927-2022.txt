MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION] Do you go by Ron, or Ronald?
RR: Oh, Ron is more comfortable. It's what I usually use.
MEH: Ron, how did you come to be at Black Mountain?
RR: I had several friends in Detroit [MEH: Robertson was one of several students
from Cass Tech High School to attend BMC.] at the time who went there who
went there. A girlfriend of mine, who'd gone there one summer, was one. I'd
been in Mexico going to school. So I decided I'd pick her up on the way back
and take her home to Detroit after the semester finished. So, that was my first
introduction to Black Mountain – or visit, I should say. Anyway, it certainly
intrigued me, and shortly after I enrolled and spent the summer and then a fall
there also. The fall, though, was spent cutting wood more than any academic
pursuit. That's a story in itself.
MEH: What had you been doing before you came to the college?
RR: Oh, I'd been going to Wayne State University in Detroit.
MEH: Are you from Detroit?

never forget a bull session they had one night with Shahn and Motherwell and
Aaron Siskind and Callahan, the photographer, and Siegel, Arthur Siegel, the
photographer. They were arguing about humanism. Shahn was just berating
them because they were such anti-humanists, dealing with all this abstraction
and not treating the human figure in a more compassionate way. I remember
Motherwell telling him he was expressing his humanism in his own way
(LAUGHS), words to that effect. I wish I could quote him. It was a knockout
drag out fight. Really, I thought they were going to come to blows.

MEH: How, as a young student, did you react to this, to see your heroes –?
RR: I think that was what was most impressive. I had put professors on a pedestal –
they were always so distant, and they seemed to be some kind of inhuman
character that did what they did. At BMC I saw a lot of the professors in their
worst light as well as their best light. At first I said, "Well, they're just like me,
you know. They're not that hot." So, my appreciation and my adoration, you
might say, declined considerably for a week or two. Then after studying with
them and working with them and getting to know them, it climbed even higher. It
was amazing, the transition that occurred there because I realized they were
human beings and had problems. I'll never forget Callahan crying on my
shoulder one night. He was upset with his wife. He was really letting his hair
down. That was an example, you know. Normally you don't find a professor
doing that with a student.
MEH: Did Shahn teach a class?
RR: Well, he was just kind of there as a consultant, you might say. He didn't really
teach. He visited people and talked to them about what they were doing. He
was doing his own work too. He was doing a lot of his drawings with kind of the
jagged line that he used at the time. He was rather solitary, in a way. He didn't
get along well with the people there. I remember his wife – he had his wife and
two, two or three kids. I think one of his sons and a daughter. I remember he
asked me if I'd teach his son how to swim, and if I did, he'd give me one of his
drawings. I taught him how to swim and I never had the courage to ask him for
the drawing. One of my major regrets in life (LAUGHS). I remember him coming

RR: Oh, he seemed ra- – I didn't take any classes from him. Being so large, he was
very impressive. He seemed, I don't know, he seemed rather distant. He was
so preoccupied with his own work. I remember sitting in on one lecture he had,

with his feet up on the desk, flicking cigarettes out the window. I remember the
particular lecture lasted till two or three in the morning. That was another thing
that really impressed me about the school – the fact that class could go on
forever, if necessary. In fact, it's something that's impressed me since then, the
fact that it was a school where the activity determined the time, whereas in
most institutions the time determines the activity. I've felt ever since then that
the most humane way of educating people is to allow the activity to determine
the time. It's more natural, whereas we're forced into this industrial mode of
timing our activities. It's not wholesome at all. Sometimes it's necessary. But I
think essentially it's not very life-enhancing. Put it that way.
MEH: It's not learning enhancing.
RR: (LAUGHS) No. No, it makes things too generalized and produces mediocrity, I
think, because there's so few completions. Which is, I think, one of the major
problems in education. So many students get through without completing what
they've pursued. Halfway, always halfway measures. Get them out. Get them
graduated.
