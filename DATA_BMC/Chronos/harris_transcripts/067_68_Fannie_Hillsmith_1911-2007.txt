FH: She told me how wonderful it was.

students were too—I mean, they weren’t very cooperative. If a student didn’t
appear for class, you were supposed to round him up. You were supposed to
look for him in his little studio, whatever they called it. I don’t think that was very

MEH: What type of art was being taught at the Museum School in Boston?
FH: Well, we had two Englishmen from the Slade. So, we learned to draw with a
great feeling for the pencil—line. They were interested in beautiful line and the
way you shaded. I mean, it wasn’t just scratching around. That was very helpful.
They would draw on your paper to show you what they meant. They would shade
the nude and the way they would get the relationship of the torso to the legs. It
was very inspiring. You know, they certainly weren’t into modern art at all. But at
that time it was good. It was good to see just very nice drawing. You see, the
year before they came in it was Philip Hale, the old-fashioned, conservative type
teacher. Drawing from a plaster cast, with charcoal. So these Englishmen were
quite an innovation. Everything is related to what’s been before. I mean, that’s—
MEH: What influenced your work towards abstraction?

FH: Well, I suppose it was the Bauhaus because I liked Klee and all the people that
had been there. I mean, I never liked the typical American. I mean, I think I
[UNINTEL] Birchfield. I mean, I can see what he was trying to do, and then he did

MEH: Had you taught before you went to Black Mountain?
FH: No, I don’t think so. I might have had a few private pupils, but I just can’t
remember. It was how many years later that I taught at Cornell. See when I was
at Black Mountain there were really very few young students. It was mostly—I
mean, it was half-and-half students and the other half were teachers coming for
sort of a refresher course. I mean, there were a lot of older people.
MEH: Especially during the war there were a lot of teachers who came.
FH: Oh, I see. But I enjoyed them. They were much more sympathetic to what you
had to tell them.
MEH: Where did you teach? Did you teach in that area down under the Studies
Building? Do you remember?
FH: Yes, it was this big building, on the ground floor. I know it was the same building
that had a lot of these cubicles. I don’t know what they called these little tiny work
areas the students had.
MEH: Studies.
FH: Studies. And they were in the same building. Upstairs. And that’s what you were
supposed to do. Go upstairs and round them up. Well, another thing which they
did which I really didn’t like at all was they could visit a class to see if they wanted

FH: Oh, we were neighbors in New Hampshire. And she stayed on after I left.
MEH: Without you.
FH: Yes. And survived and, of course, was married to someone she met. I’ve

MEH: Tasker Howard.

instance the very first day we were there they would have a meeting and all the
students would be there. You’d stand up and they would say, “This is Fannie
Hillsmith. She’s going to teach painting. How many people would like to take the
class. ” Well, I can’t think of Bernard’s wife’s name.
MEH: Berta.
FH: Berta. Nobody felt like making sandals at that point. She felt—I mean, it was
upsetting. Well, then it turned out her class ended up being the most crowded
class that whole summer because it was fascinating making sandals. You see,
you were put on the spot and that—I think that was unfortunate. What else? Oh,
another thing. On the first day we were there, we had a meeting, and they said,
“If you go to Asheville with a black student—well, in those days we said
Negro—you must walk at least ten feet behind her or in front of her.” I was really
quite shocked at that. I mean, I thought it was incredible.
MEH: It’s incredible they said that. Students went into Asheville all the time together.
FH: Well, you have to remember. This was thirty years ago, and it might have caused
a stir.
