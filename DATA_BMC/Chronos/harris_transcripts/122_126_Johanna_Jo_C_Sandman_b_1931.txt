JS: Yes, when I was in grammar school, my school district was in the Ward Street
School area, and it was a quite radical school. So, I spent my whole grammar
school time in a school where there were enormous rewards for creative activity
whether it was in science or in art. A lot of collaborative activity going on and so
it really fostered a lot of mural activity and working in crafts as well as in the
arts. It was wonderful. It was really wonderful.
MEH: How did you get to Black Mountain?
JS: How do you mean?
MEH: Did you go by train? Did you drive?
JS: I think I went by train, although it's not really vivid. Although I do have a good
remembrance for landscape. The landscape was so beautiful, the mountains. I
remember once we there the red clay was something that really had a lot of
impact. It was wonderful.
MEH: Had you ever been in the South before?
JS: I had been in Atlanta for brief periods, visiting a very close friend. Aside from
that, no.
MEH: Do you remember your first impression of the college?

JS: Well, I was overjoyed. I mean I thought first of all it was so informal looking, you
know, with all the lodges. Then when I saw the Studio Building, I thought it was
just amazing, both architecturally – I thought it was a wonderful structure. But
then the whole idea of everyone, everyone in the community could have their

MEH: Do you remember where yours was?
JS: Which lodge it was?
MEH: Were you in a lodge or were you in the Studies Building? For your studio.
JS: My studio was in the Studies Building, yes. I think I was on the third floor. The
whole notion of having the freedom to stay there twenty-four hours if one
wanted was very liberating. I think even more, being able to interact with
colleagues on the spur, even. It was just wonderful. For feedback to one's work.
MEH: Who were other students who were there that you remember?
JS: Well, someone I remember fairly vividly is Trueman MacHenry. He was a
regular at the college, and he fell madly in love with a young woman from New
York who happened to be my roommate, so we all – we just all spent a lot of
time together, and later when they honeymooned in New York, I was able to
loan them my little railroad apartment for their honeymoon. That was really
great fun. I remember that Fielding Dawson was there, and Jay – Jay Someone
who was also an aspiring writer.

JS: Yes, I knew about Cage before I went, and admired him.
MEH: Did you study painting with Shahn and Motherwell?
JS: Yes. Motherwell was there the first month and I did. I really very much admired
his approach. It was clear he had this kind of solid philosophical base in
addition to his artistic expertise. I remember one thing he told us when he was
critiquing painting was he said that one way to establish a kind of spatial unity
in one's work is to pretend you're a tiny insect crawling across the surface of
the canvas, and if suddenly you fall into a deep chasm, something's wrong
there because it shouldn't be that deep ever. This is some – that whole notion
of maintaining shallow space in the modernist painting, and how important that
was as a kind of working within the matrix of limited depth. Because after all,
what was abstract. It was not realistic. So that was – I found that very
interesting and memorable, and I've actually used that notion with my own
students in painting.
MEH: Where did Motherwell hold his class? Did he actually teach or did he do
critiques?

JS: I think it was more critiques. Critiques I think. Then I remember that Cy
Twombly and Bob Rauschenberg and his wife were there. I remember Twombly
– maybe it was Rauschenberg, too. I remember them working under the
Studies Building, painting very, very large canvases which had been thrown on
the ground. Maybe that was just Twombly. The other thing I remember about
Twombly was that he used to – in the evenings, he used to make the most
astonishing little collage studies out of like a shirt cardboard with maybe torn up
pieces of his shirt and other fragments and bits along with painting and
drawing, all combined – they were just quite wonderful. I'm not sure whether I
remember this or not. I've read so much about it I'm not sure it's really so, but I
remember that Rauschenberg did his famous studies on blueprint paper, using
a solar, making solar prints. I just don't know whether he really did that there, or
whether I've just heard and read so much about it. But it may have taken place.
They had Sue lie on the paper and he would expose it. I'm not sure whether he
actually used the light, whether he did it at night with light, use artificial light, or
whether he put it in the sun.

JS: Well, actually, what happened at Brandeis was I had had quite a bit of work in
painting before, and drawing, and my professor, actually – I don't know whether
he thought I was disruptive or he thought I really didn't need to be in a separate
studio, but he decided to put me in an adjacent, separate studio so that I could
just work undisturbed. He would come in and give me crits, and then let me be
enrolled in the class when he thought it was appropriate, but he didn't seem to
really feel that that was appropriate for me. So, it wasn't so much that I was
taught there as I was like given encouragement and guidance. So it was a
curious – unusual situation. Another thing I really remember about Black
Mountain is the wonderful performances that would take place with Katy Litz's
work. Katy Litz performing and David Tudor playing the piano and maybe
Motherwell having created some of the sets with students, or later with Shahn
doing it. What I remember about it all is that people would become so
stimulated by it that they would then say "Oh, I want to put that piece of music
to a different sort of setting," and do just totally different visual aspects, and so
someone else would like take it and run with it. Just things were always being
transmuted.
MEH: Did you become involved at all with the work program? Working on the farm or
doing dishes.
JS: Oh, yes. Oh, yes. Yes, I remember helping repair a footbridge with a small crew
of people, and I had some kitchen duty. Oh, yes, I was involved with haying at
one point. I think we were – Yes, yes. I mean, I thought that was another quite
amazing aspect of Black Mountain that it had been figured out that the

JS: Yes. Yes. I went into Asheville, went in with a group, and I remember we were
sort of primed beforehand to be a little circumspect and not to behave too
wildly, which would put people off in the town. So we were warned about that. I

MEH: What was it about the red clay that caught your attention?
JS: Well, I think it seemed like a kind of almost creative parallel to college for me,
because it seemed like it was kind of unexpected, unpredictable, and yet solid
and moored – rooted. But it did kind of spring out in rough and ready ways, you
know. I was always surprised and refreshed by it. I'd never seen anything like it.
In New England there's this. You know, the soil is brown, the grass is green,
and we have some wonderful boulders up north, but this phenomenon didn't
exist here. It was very welcome.
MEH: How do you remember Katy Litz? Did you take any dance? Were you
interested in dance?
JS: No, I didn't, but I was very impressed with her. I mean she seemed to tap into a
very deep well of emotion and yet at the same time she was – and her body
was very strong and she was very energetic, but there was also a really kind of
a lyrical aspect to her work. I hope I'm not misremembering. (LAUGHS)
