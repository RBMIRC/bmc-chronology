MEH: You went to Black Mountain in the summer of '52 or you went in the fall of
'51?
VF: I went in the fall of '51, I think. Then I stayed through the end of spring. I
went back that summer because Merce was coming to teach. I didn't know
who Merce was because very few people knew Merce at that time. But I had
seen his picture—a tiny, tiny little picture in some dance book—and I wanted
to study with him. So, that must have been the summer of '51. I stayed in
order to take classes with him. Then after that I went to New York.
MEH: That would have been the summer of '52.
VF: Yes. Right.
MEH: Where had you been before Black Mountain?
VF: I had gone to several colleges and being, oh, mainly bored. I read an ad in
the Saturday Review about Black Mountain, and I decided to go there.
That's just about what I did—packed up and went. Once I got there, I was
just terrified of the place, because I'd never seen people like that before. It
was mainly men students that had beards. Yes, they had long hair in ‘51. I

MEH: What type of background did you come from? I mean, was the sort of thing
that Katherine Litz was doing and Lou Harrison was doing the sort of thing
you expected in music and dance?
VF: Yes. I was pretty open-minded about what the dance would be like. I had
studied in Washington with a woman who had come to this country from the
Wigman School in Germany. What I really wanted to do was study piano,
which was what I had done. Lou Harrison was not a pianist, but he gave me
piano lessons. It was very interesting, because he gave lessons from a
composer's viewpoint—how things sounded rather than technique. Actually,
we had difficulty getting along, so we would do anything to avoid these piano
lessons. Like I would have terribly sore throats, and he would be indisposed.
But when I took them, they were really very, very interesting. Katy Litz—I'm
not sure what I think about her teaching but simply to see how she worked
was very well worth it.
MEH: In what respect?
VF: Well, she was like something out of a fairy tale. She was so beautiful. Her
dancing had something extraordinarily light and colorful, very much her own.
I think we all tried, all of us. Four at the most took class with her. We all tried
to look like Katy and couldn't. But that was all right.

MEH: I was going to ask you. He had had people dancing with him and then he
officially formed a company?
VF: Well, no. He had worked with three or four girls, and he had done some
things with them. Then for whatever reason that didn't happen anymore.
He didn't say, "I want to form a company. Will you dance with me?" He
said he was making some dances, and he was going to go to Black
Mountain in the summer. He asked those of us if we were interested in
doing that. That's the way I started in a company. I did the same. I think
it's the only reasonable way. You don't say, "I'm gonna have a company."
Make the company, and then say, "My God, what am I gonna do?" You do
it because you want to do something. Then, if you keep on wanting to do
something, that group of people becomes the company. So we started
working in New York in the spring, and then he took three of us to Black
Mountain with him for six weeks. One of them was Paul Taylor and, of
course, Anita Dencks and I. [correct] We went for three weeks, and then
some more people came—the ones who were further along came for the
last three weeks. Some of us were there for six weeks, and others for
three. We gave a program in the dining hall which is where everything
happened.
