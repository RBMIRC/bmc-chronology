MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Ruth, when were you at Black Mountain?
RLS: Summer of 1944, in the depth of World War Two.
MEH: How did you come to be there?
RLS: My sister was a student there for her freshman year – music, voice, and it
seemed like a good idea for us to both go at the same time – me being an art
type and she thought the area was so beautiful. My parents really believed in
the philosophy at Black Mountain. They’d done a fair amount of reading. Of
course, Sam Brown – everybody remembers Sam– did a good job of
convincing them that it was a good place to be.
MEH: Where did you live?
RLS: We lived in those days in Wilton and Westport Connecticut, two different places
but sequentially. [TECHNICAL INTERRUPTION].
MEH: You lived in –?
RLS: Well, Wilton, my parents were good friends of the Browns.
MEH: Okay. Was Sam from Wilton?
RLS: Yes.
MEH: What did your parents do?
RLS: My father was a commercial artist and magazine illustrator, and my mother was
a housewife, very much interested in the arts and education.
MEH: So they had a background in the arts. It wasn’t—
RLS: Yes. Music also, as well.
MEH: Okay. What was your sister’s name?
RLS: Harriette. Harriette Lyford Rauh is her name now. R-A-U-H.
MEH: I don’t think she’s on my list.
RLS: No, she left Black Mountain to study with Lotte Leonard, who was the voice
teacher that she studied with at Black Mountain that summer. They went to
Cincinnati.
MEH: I’ll have to get her address when I leave today. So your parents were happy to
have you, both sisters, go to Black Mountain.
RLS: Yes. Yes. Well, it was between my – I was still in high school, my sophomore
and junior year, and I thought I was ever so worldly having gone to Black
Mountain for a summer.
MEH: How – Did you know about the program that summer? Was it the particular
summer program that interested you?

RLS: Yes. And the chance to, oh – Jean Charlot was also somebody that I knew

MEH: Did you know about de Creeft or Ozenfant?
RLS: Yes. A little bit. Also the dance was very interesting – Elsa Kahl was there with

MEH: How did you get down there?
RLS: We went by train. We took a – We shared a Pullman, and it was very weird
waking up and hearing, and looking out the windows and seeing “Color.”
“White,” you know, at the stations. I think that what they put. “Colored Only.”
And the train ride was very interesting.
MEH: Had you been to the South before?
RLS: No. It was a real eye-opener.
MEH: Was it your first real experience of formal segregation?
RLS: Yes. I had written – I’m not sure if it was in the Reminiscence in the Black
Mountain book that Melvin Lane put together, but I did write about the impact of
Jim Crow as I saw it that summer.
MEH: Could you repeat that, or review it? What you wrote?
RLS: Well, one – This is kind of wordy, but one of the things that really struck me was
taking the bus into Asheville from Black Mountain one day. We had been taught
to go to the back of the bus, at home, because it was during gas rationing and
everybody rode the buses. And polite bus behavior was to go to the back of the
bus, the last empty seat, which I did. I just didn’t even think. I felt this palpable
“What are you doing!” There were no white people on the bus except myself
and maybe the bus driver. The rest of the people were black, and it was such a
weird experience. I don’t know if I moved myself to seat thinking, you know,
what’s going on? Or whether it was something that hit me afterwards was this
experience, riding the buses. I made several trips into Asheville and made the
same mistake because I didn’t realize what was going on.
MEH: Do you think it made the blacks on the bus uncomfortable?
RLS: Yes. Everybody.
MEH: Everybody.
RLS: There may have been whites in the front part of the bus. It’s funny how you
scramble for some kind of explanation or reality-check for what’s going on. It’s
just what I’m feeling. I mean – then another part of my experience along those
lines was seeing the chain gangs. It was very surreal to be having our lovely
Arcadian romps through the mountain paths and through those wonderful
grassy growths and so on, rhododendron and so on, and then suddenly to
appear and here’s this chain gang going by. I looked at the metal shackles and
so on, and it was really like a sort of fast-forward thing where you were in one
world and then suddenly in this other world. So, and remembering always a
sixteen-year-old, beginning the loss of innocence, along those lines. Then
afterward, over the years, I thought “My goodness, here were so many

MEH: Do you remember the integration of the college being discussed that summer?
RLS: No. No. It was very intense along the arts lines, and the Work Program. I mean,
to my experience. But then – I’m not sure whether this went into the book of

MEH: Did you explain to him what it meant?
RLS: Yes. I think I knew enough to tell him that it was a sign of good luck, and that
the four prongs were the directions of the compass. But I think it still – it upset
him so that maybe he didn’t really take in what I had said to him.
MEH: Did you feel really aware of the plight of the refugees who were there that
summer?
RLS: Yes and no, on a level of a sixteen-year-old. I was aware that Fritz Cohen had
to leave Germany and had done this ballet, the music to the ballet, The
Three-Cornered Hat. Am I right? The Green Table [OVERTALK]. That it had to
do with repression. I mean in a metaphorical sense more. Then the Mann
daughters were there? You know you get things mixed up. Monika—
MEH: Monika Mann was there.
RLS: Yes, and there was another one. Her name was something along—Erika? Yes.
It was kind of filtering there. Then I loved the Work Program. I loved the farming
aspect and the way everybody sort of came together out of their differences,
and city life background. Then the ones who were kind of suburban and so on.
The Penley – there was – Max Penley was the son of Ross, who was the man
on the farm. He was so proud of the fact that he didn’t chew tobacco. He said
he quit chewing tobacco when he was twelve, and he only smoked. [LAUGHS]
What was he, sixteen or something? You know, it was funny.
MEH: What courses did you take at the college?
RLS: I took Albers’s course in Drawing. Matière. Well, the drawing was life drawing.
Then I took drawing with Charlot, with Jean Charlot, and he taught fresco
technique, and we did a fresco project on one of the pillars or the pilings under
the Study Building.
MEH: What do you remember about doing those?
RLS: Well, I remember doing a grid on the brown paper, and he explained how we
had to do just one section at a time. We had to schedule everything so that we
could finish before the mortar dried. I remember mixing the cement with a
shovel. I mean, we did that ourselves – scrunch, scrunch, scrunch, scrunch.
The wonderful timing and command he had of each step of the way in the
process.
MEH: Did students help at all with the painting, or –

RLS: Yes. He really—I think it was his design. Then the students did the pigment
mixing. It wasn’t a big – It was maybe four by eight feet. It was a section, and I
think the idea was that they would do – they’d continue on this, as people were

MEH: What do you remember about Charlot? What was he like?
RLS: Oh, Charlot was so dear. His English, his use of English. He used to say things
like “deef-i-culty,” “with great dee-fee-culty,” but Albers did the same thing, it
seemed to me. They had those – I notice people who came from English as a
second language or something that they made many of the same cute – not
cute, but, mannerisms or whatever. He was there with Zohmar. I think they had
three children by then, or four. They were very inclusive for me. It seemed more
my milieu – their family, wherever they were living. I don’t, I can’t identify that at
this point. But I did have lunch with them several times, and they were sort of
more quiet in their lifestyle, whereas there was the partying and the politicking
and the usual thing, which now I know what that was, but in those days I just
thought, “Well, some people like more partying than others.” I wasn’t a big
partier. I was always in bed by 9:30 or 10. A real farm girl. And up at 6 and that
kind of thing. I remember Charlot’s talk. One evening – There were those
wonderful evenings when people put together an after-dinner get-together, and
there was a slide presentation of Albers. Charlot did a commentary, and he
liked to put some meaning to these abstractions of Albers, which there were
groups of the rectangles overlapping. Charlot said something about, “This is
Mary and Elizabeth, meeting, and one is with child with Christ and the other is
with child of John the Baptist.” He was fond of giving a Biblical or religious or
some kind of family or historic meaning to something visual. Albers – I’m not
sure if Albers was giving a frantic disclaimer. I wasn’t aware of their relationship
artistically. But he made a disclaimer. He said “There’s no content in my work at
all other than just the rectangles,” and so on.
MEH: Where did Charlot teach his course?
RLS: You mean which area? I don’t have a clear idea. In one of those lower level
rooms. We were kind of around. We were in different areas, I think because of
this working up the image for the fresco. I’m not too terribly clear on that. But
the Albers life class was in one of those areas facing – It’s funny how it’s very
clear in some ways.
MEH: Did he teach a life class, Albers?
RLS: Yes. [AFFIRMATIVE] It was kind of hard to find life models, and he – or nude
models – and he was able to get people who would come with a bathing suit of
something. It was very kind of an interesting subject – was how you get good
models. He was very good at that. Very excellent at the life drawing. Both of
them. This is my recollection.
MEH: What about – Did you take his matière course, did you say?
RLS: Yes, yes. That was, that was like a whole—I hate to use the word “epiphany,”
but it was wonderful. I was very—not intimidated, but overwhelmed by the
worldliness, sophistication, of so many of the other people there. There were a
few sort of bumpkin types like myself there. I thought, “Oh, this is going to be a
real fizzle, but what the heck.” I love the color orange, and I had this big piece

MEH: What do you remember about them?
RLS: Well, it seemed to me he had a project to make a type of fastener. Not buttons,
not zippers. I don’t remember if I did anything along those lines or not. But it
was quite an eye-opener, his idea of – I think he was getting ready to tour his
exhibition of “Are Clothes Modern?” Does that line up – the time? You could
hear a pin drop in his talks – evening or afternoon talks that he gave. Clothing
being not to conceal but to reveal. Things like that.
MEH: Did he use any illustrations? Did he have slides, do you remember?
RLS: Yes. Yes. He had slides and diagrams that he had taken slides of. For instance,
how many layers of clothing there are with your underwear, then your
undershirt, and then your pants overlapping, and then your shirt overlapping,
and the vest overlapping and the coat overlapping. He made some – It was
quite humorous, as well, which is a good hook for something that can be a little
bit earthshaking when people think they’re very cool in the way we dress. Then
lo and behold – Well, and I think he had slides of the way the tribes in Africa, of
the women that stretch their necks this way, and then the Ubangi thing in the
lips, and then he contrasted it with the way we do things, or in Western culture
the ridiculous things we do. I don’t mean that’s ridiculous, but – Oh, then I was
– there was the dance and the music and singing, and was it Lowinsky? Oh
dear. There was a community chorus.
MEH: Jalowetz.
RLS: Jalowetz! That was wonderful. He was such a gentle, wonderful soul and could
get people to come together and rehearse.

?

MEH: You say you did the music and the art.
RLS: Loved it, loved it. I remember an evening when Simon Sadoff was there and
Fritz Cohen, and they played two pianos, those two wonderful grand pianos in
the Dining Hall. It seemed to me magical the way they could just take a given
space and transform it from where we all ate, fed our faces, and then suddenly
it was a ballroom with pianos and so on. They played Carmen all the way

something else, I don’t know if I just sketched this to Mervin, but there was one

music to dance. I hope I’m right in this. She played cello? One day I was in
there just bopping in and there was a black widow spider hanging on the roof
behind her. These little vignettes. I thought “Well, this is the way it is here.
Nature and danger exist, they coexist very well in the environment here.”

MEH: What did you do? Did you say “Oh, by the way, there’s a – ”
RLS: No, I guess we just sort of remarked and went right on what we were doing,
and [LAUGHS].
MEH: Do you remember any snakes that summer? Was that a snake summer?
RLS: No, I don’t – I don’t remember that, but I do remember the wonderful way the
cows went sort of making themselves at home in the grass spots between the
little practice cubicles? Those little cabins. And I can’t remember if that’s in The
Sprouted Seeds or not, but I did write about that in one draft. They just sort of
checked out the practice cubicles, as they were being occupied and used with
practicing and vocalizing, and stick their heads through the window or through
the door and then mosey on, and – There again was this blend of the natural
world and the man – humans creating art. It was wonderful. But I really – I was
not aware of all these undercurrents. I was so blissfully into my own
sixteen-year-old summer.
MEH: You weren’t aware of all the crises that summer, and the infighting, and the
resignations –
RLS: A little bit, a little bit. But I think I had a good mechanism for denial or shutting
out things like that. There was so much that was so right about the whole thing.
Yes, I do remember it, actually. Some other people who were there was Ati
Gropius, and I think – She married Clark Foreman, or one of, somebody named
–
MEH: Charles Forberg.
RLS: Forberg. Oh, Forman. Forberg. Yes. It seemed to me they were a couple at that
point, and I didn’t know about Lori and José, which was great, I mean, and –
But there was a feeling of this difference in European and sort of a crusty

MEH: Sure. Why don’t we talk about – You left Black Mountain, and then we’ll come

RLS: Yeah, and I finished high school in Westport at Staples High School and then I

winters – At Bennington, you had a nonresident term, we called it, and I did
things like make jewelry with someone in New York, and another one was

Graham’s studio when it was down on Lower Fifth Avenue, at that time. Then
after Bennington I returned to New York and worked in window display in
several different places. And papier mâché, finishing things, matière, a great
deal of what I had learned there. Then, because window display work was
seasonal—you work very hard for each – the Christmas and then the Easter,
the summer things. In between everybody went off and got – They free-lanced,
and I started doing children’s portraits at that point, and got going on that. It
wasn’t anything that I sat down and said “Well this is what I’m going to do with
my life.” Not at all. I wish! [LAUGHS] But then after four years I went to Europe

Mediterranean and all sorts of stuff. Then came to New London to live, here.
And continued with children’s portraits. And painting. It’s branched out into
regional paintings. I do landscapes of local scenes. My work is more a blend of
– not really primitive, but with very much that kind of strong design, which was
the backbone that I got from, say, Albers, the emphasis on design. I didn’t know
