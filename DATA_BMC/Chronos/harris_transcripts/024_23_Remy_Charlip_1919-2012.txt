MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION]. Remy, when did you go to Black Mountain, and
how did you get there?
RC: [LAUGHTER]
MEH: This is totally unclear. I don't think you have a file at the State Archive.
RC: Oh really? Well I got there the first time when M.C. Richards and David Tudor
drove down together and I went—I was sitting in the back of the car. The back
window was broken. The back door was broken. I remember it being cold. It
was Thanksgiving. We went down for Thanksgiving, and I went to visit Lou
Harrison, who I was living with at the time. That's how I got down there. I got
down there because of Lou, but I got down there by being driven down by
M.C., with David Tudor. And I don't remember the dates. I'm very bad at dates.
MEH: And you had known Lou in New York?
RC: No. I actually saw him for the first time in New York when I auditioned for a
scholarship at Reed College for the summer dance program, and I met him
there at Reed College.
MEH: Okay. But you knew he was at Black Mountain.

RC: Well, by that time we were living together, and Vera—I remember Vera
Williams coming. I can't remember who else she came with, but I remember
she came to ask him if he would come and teach at Black Mountain College.
He asked me if I wanted to go. I was starting to study with Merce and I didn't
want to leave to go, so he went, and I went to visit him.
MEH: That first time that you went to visit, they were going down at Thanksgiving.
Did you stay beyond Thanksgiving?
RC: I stayed a little while, and then I went back, and then I came back for the
summer. And I was there for a summer with Katy Litz. I studied with her. Then
I started to dance with Katy Litz and her company and started to design
costumes for her.
MEH: So you were there that first visit and then for a summer.
RC: Yeah, right. Then I came again as, I don't know, part of a company, you know,
artists-in-residence, with Merce, when he came with his new young company. I
was part of that.
MEH: That was '53.
RC: So then the summer before was '52, and then it was the Thanksgiving before
that, so there we go.
MEH: Okay. What had you heard about the college? Did you know about it before
Lou went down?
RC: Yeah, I heard about it through a friend. I went to the art department. I
studied—I graduated from the Fine Arts Department at Cooper Union in New
York, and there was a woman there who I liked a lot, and she told me about

MEH: You were talking in the auditorium about the little programs that were printed
as cigarette wrappers. What do you remember about doing that, and how it
came about?
RC: Well, I know there was a series of concerts and for one we decided we wanted
to make programs. I was—actually my official title at that time there—we were
joking about it. I was I was "entrepreneur" for Merce's concerts. I remember
buying in the mill some blue yarn—blue fabric that was about 36 inches wide.
And we sewed it together to make a stage, to make the backdrop and to make
wings, and we decided to do a program for John's Sonatas and Interludes.
That is, I decided to do it. I took it upon myself and decided to print it on
cigarette paper, the actual paper that you roll up that has glue at one end, so
that it's—It's like tissue paper. And I remember setting up the program in the
Print Shop, and I chose the tiniest type and designed it so—it was a lot of
repetition and actually a lot of type in it. And so I had to use tweezers to set the

RC: Yeah, but I can't remember what I did. I know—I don’t know. I may have—I

the dragon. I know I helped make some of the costumes. But I can't remember

that's mostly what I did. I would sit in on other people's classes, but—
MEH: What do you remember about the last summer you were there, when Merce
brought the group of dancers down from New York?
RC: Well, it was very exciting. I mean it was one of Merce's first opportunities to
bring a group of people together where you didn't have to worry about
schedule, because people didn't have jobs so they could meet at the
appointed time, and then we'd be fed and housed, and so all we had to do was
rehearse. I remember there was a composition class, and I tried to do
something in the composition class, but it was one of my first efforts and—I
remember thinking that I wanted to try to be upright at one minute and flat on
the floor the next. I kept falling down to try to see what would happen. But I
gave that up when my pelvis and my arms became too sore [LAUGHS]. But
other than that, there was, you know, just all the excitement about all the
people who were there, you know. Franz Kline and—It was wonderful. And all
the, you know, M.C., and it was just a wonderful place to be.
MEH: That last summer you all did a tremendous amount of work. You came back to
New York and gave a performance like in December or November.

And it was only later that I found out a lot of those things. Like in his recent
books of interviews, he talks about certain of those pieces and how he made
them, and why he made them. And it's very interesting, because Merce is so
completely different than I am. He's very secretive and kind of mysterious, and
I like to tell everybody everything and explain everything, and then see what
they see in it, you know. So it's a different point of view.

MEH: It's interesting that now this many years later he's talking more about it.
RC: Yeah. Yeah. And it's a shame, because I think we would have gotten so much
from knowing what it was that he was into, you know, because the ideas were
intriguing. Actually the doing of it was interesting, interesting enough and
difficult enough, but, you know, just to do it, just to do what he was suggesting
we do was very occupying. But I think it would have been easier—I would
have liked it if he had—For instance, it wasn't until much later when he did
Summer Space, where I somehow found out that the dance was conceived of
as always being in circles, that the pathway was always a circle, and the
circles weren't always visible. That is, a circle could start onstage and continue
offstage, so that it would only be part of the circle. So he had all these
movements that were circular onstage, but only part of what the full circle was.
Not that you continue dancing offstage—which you could have done, if the
backstage was big enough. But it's a wonderful idea. And a lot of his ideas are
really wonderful, and it's delightful to know them. When we did that dance, I
wish I had known that, because it would have made my entering and exiting
much more vital, I think. But—There's a funny story about that in—In that
particular dance we were rehearsing and I was on the outside of a circle going
clockwise and Merce was going counter-clockwise, and always in rehearsal at
a certain point, upstage left, Merce would bump into me and say "Excuse me,"
because like most choreographers he always left himself for last, or rehearsed
the least, watched everybody and so he would [LAUGHS] not really know what
he was doing sometimes, you know. Of course, in this case, he kept bumping

MEH: It seems that during the 1950s, for all of you, Black Mountain was sort of a
center. People at that point were coming and going. I mean Merce and John
were in New York. M.C. had left and she was going back and forth. But maybe
really New York was more of a center and Black Mountain was more of a
fringe place. What was your feeling about Black Mountain at the time?
RC: Well, I remember thinking at that time, and it was—I don't know whether it was
Black Mountain or New York, but I remember thinking that there were only
about a hundred interesting people in the world, you know, and I thought they
were all centered around the art community, the painters and poets and the
dancers and the musicians in New York and Black Mountain. I would think
over, and just kind of count how many people there were that I thought were
very, very spectacular, and I thought ,"Well, these are them," you know. But of
course that's a very snotty attitude. I mean it's very; you know—Now I see that
there are lots more interesting people around.
MEH: Right. But the art world then was so small. I mean now it's tremendous.
RC: Yes.
MEH: But then it was much smaller and more isolated, and—
RC: Yeah [AFFIRMATIVE]. I think it's in part that the art world was responsible for
that happening, in that what they were saying and what I continue to teach is
that each person is unique and each person is an artist and everything you do

RC: Yeah, I mean they were all talking about it one way or another, you know, what

MEH: Did you work at Black Mountain at all with Betty Jennerjahn and the Light

RC: No, no. I saw some of those, but I never was part of it. And I'm not—You know,

MEH: Looking back now, they're having all of these testimonials tomorrow. What do

you, if any, really?
RC: Well, I just think the community of artists and being able to be in a situation
where you can work on something that you're interested in rather than
something that someone tells you you should be interested in, you know, or
just figuring out your own course, your own—and going with that. That, to me,
is, I think, special.
MEH: But you would not have gotten it also at Cooper Union? To be part of a

RC: Not really. Not really. I mean one got certain things there, but I wouldn't say it
was that so much. I think more it was teaching generally, teaching techniques,
which was very different. I mean that's harsh, but I mean—more or less.
