MEH: [GIVES IDENTIFICATION] Mary, how did you come to be at Black Mountain
College?
MPB: When I was in possibly ninth grade at John Burroughs School in St. Louis, Bob
Wunsch came to the school and gave a talk to the assembly about this unusual
college. I remember being just really taken with what he said and what he
described and thinking, "Boy, when I grow up, that's where I want to go."
MEH: John Burroughs, was that just a regular high school?
MPB: No, it was a private coeducational, supposedly progressive school. But thinking
about it now there wasn't anything particularly progressive about it. It was very
stringent in terms of what we were expected to accomplish. So, we worked
hard and learned a lot. Then, in the twelfth grade, a friend of mine, Suzie
Teasdale, announced to me: oh, she was not going to finish the twelfth grade at
John Burroughs; she was going to go to Black Mountain College. So I wanted
to go too. If she could do it, why I could do it. I set up a storm. My parents
wouldn't hear of it, of course. Then by Christmastime I had worn them down,

Mountain. I was seventeen, and very wet behind the ears. Naive.
MEH: Were you terribly dissatisfied with high school, or this just appealed to you so
much more—?
MPB: It appealed to me so much more, and I think I was dissatisfied perhaps with the
concentration on cliques. I'd spent years being popular. I was finally popular. I

had a yen to go do this thing that was so fresh. St. Louis is a very conventional
city. People live by conventions, and there are things that are done and other
things that are not done. The whole sort of experimental approach to things
appealed to me very strongly.
MEH: Black Mountain was willing to let you—I mean to accept you without a high

MPB: That's right. They were, I mean there were 38 students when I went in January
of 1945. It was right after The Split, The Big Split, and so a lot of the students
had left and a lot of the faculty had left. It was sort of a bad time for Black
Mountain, so they were anxious to have students who would, whose parents
could pay. They took a number of students who hadn't graduated from high
school, Suzie Teasdale being one of them and me being another. Gee, I've

MEH: Did you visit the college before you enrolled?
MPB: No, just went.

give people shots or something. You were supposed to study English and

wanted to be, or what meant the most to me in a personal way, it was art.

elementary school, although we did learn how to read and write and speak a
little French. Then when I went to John Burroughs, it was much more strict with
a lot of homework and so on, but I'd had that background.

MPB: Yeah. Yeah, it didn't bother me at all. I did very well, too. It turned out, when I

wanted to do. I was pretty much flat on my face.

MEH: How did he conduct his class?
MPB: Well, he brought in a lot of pieces of paper. He would make his own colored
