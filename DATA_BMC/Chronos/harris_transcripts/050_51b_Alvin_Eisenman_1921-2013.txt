Corrected version of an interview of Alvin Fisenman conducted
by Mary Emma Harris on April 17, 1997 at Bethany CT

MEH: All right, you were saying that you and Albers arrived at
the Yale Art School at the same time?
AE: Yes, we met for,the first time September first, which was
a couple of weeks before classes began. Albers while he was
still at Black Mountain had been a member of the Yale
University Council’s Committee on Art and he was appointed to
the Art School faculty in 1950. From its beginning in 1869 the
school had been modeled on the Bcole des Beaux Arts in Paris.
The Albers appointment was part of a plan of the Dean to change
the direction of the school. His earlier appointments of George
Howe and Louis Kahn to the architecture faculty were part of
the same effort.
MEH: Who was the dean then?
AE: Charles Henry Sawyer. He’s now in his nineties anda
retired member of the art faculty at the University of
Michigan. Sawyer was (and is) a confirmed modernist, and it was
his idea to add a graphic arts program to the school’s existing
programs, which included painting, sculpture, architecture,
city planning, and drama. As an undergraduate I had majored in
Ray Nash’s graphic arts program at Dartmouth but I had became a
practicing designer and I had no experience at all in teaching.
When I pointed this out to Sawyer he told me not: to worry, that
IT would be working with one of the great.art teachers of the
century, Josef Albers. As it happened I had hung an exhibition
of Albers’ lithographs at Dartmouth in 1940 and had been
dazzled by his work. This was what gave me the courage to take
the job and, just as Sawyer expected, working with him was a
very rewarding experience. I’m not talking here about his
classroom style, which was dramatic and inimitable. However the
principle behind his teaching was clear: Never tell students
what you want them to learn; instead, devise exercises in which
they teach themselves. This isn’t easy for a teacher to do, but
things learned this way are learned for life.
