First College Catalog
At Blue Ridge, as a result of the existing architecture, some of our most basic ideas about housing developed at the very opening on the College in September 1933,- especially:
Faculty and students should live near each other and have their meals together.
Students should each have their individual studies.
Almost all the activities of the College were located in Lee Hall with its great lobby, 100’ x 60’, where the whole community met for concerts, general meetings, community singing, Saturday night parties, and every day for tea.
One wing was a faculty dormitory, the second wing a student dormitory. The enormous dining hall had ample room for the after-supper dancing as well as for the stage.
Except for a few nearby cottages, we were all concentrated into one building, but at the same time, we were spoiled by the amount of space available. Large studies occupied by faculty and a few students were 22’ long and 10’ wide. Small studies were 14’ by 10’.
We have to move out completely every summer.
STEP 2 Gropius-Breuer Plans 1938-39
Material Exhibited: (prepared by Paul Williams)
Photos of Model
Letter of description Model
The Lake Eden property was purchased in 1937 after several years of searching for the best possible site and after an unsuccessful attempt to purchase Blue Ridge.
Gropius & Breuer were then invited to design the ideal modern college. Certain features were emphasized as a result of the favorable experience at Blue Ridge:
Unity of the community was to be emphasized by having dormitories, studies, dining room, laboratories, and shops in a single, intimately connected group of buildings and by
A large Central Hall, which had proved itself so valuable at Blue Ridge.
Individual studies for students as well as for faculty were to be emphasized despite great cost.
Faculty living quarters were to be somewhat separated as sufficient privacy had been lacking at Blue Ridge.
This plan presupposed a minimum of spending on old buildings, which were to be torn down as soon as possible.
At this time, also, there had been no thought of doing the work of construction ourselves.
An extensive campaign was to be undertaken to raise $500,000.00 to $750,000.00 for the main buildings and an initial meeting was held at the Museum of Modern Art in New York where the architects spoke and the model was shown. TIME Magazine published an article and showed a photo montage, with the buildings already in place along the lake shore.
However, by this time, (Christmas 1939), the war in Europe was already well under way and plans for the campaign were postponed until after the war.
These plans never got beyond the preliminary stage and cost $1,200.00.
STEP 3 Kocher Plans 1940-41
Material Exhibited: (prepared by Hank Bergman)
Zoning Plan
Early blueprint of studies building (does not show large lobby and social rooms adequately.)
Model of studies building
Just when all plans for building at Lake Eden had been given up because of the war, we were unexpectedly compelled to move because of the refusal of the owners of the Blue Ridge property, in the spring of 1940, to renew our lease beyond June 1941. Without a cent to start building, the only feasible plan seemed to be to adapt the buildings already here for winter use. We had to insulate buildings, install twelve heating plants, and Lawrence Kocher came to BMC as resident architect and made a fresh study of the grounds with these requirements in mind. In constant consultation with faculty and students, many of whom took up the study of architecture, he evolved a new plan with the following characteristics:
The whole college was spread out, making fuller use of the terrain at a hillside location like Blue Ridge.
The heart of the college was to be the Study Center which should eventually include not only faculty and student studies, but also laboratories, library, classrooms, living rooms, and a large central lobby to serve the same purposes that the large lobby did over at Lee Hall.
A zoning plan which envisaged a rather sharp separation of faculty and student living quarters. This particular aspect of the zoning was in response to a strong reaction on the part of the faculty to the lack of necessary privacy afforded at Blue Ridge.
Construction was to be of types adapted so far as possible to unskilled student labor.
Intensive construction and money-raising started in September 1940 and continued, through summer and all, unabated until the fall of 1942. The college had to move in June 1941 when about half the work had been done. In the fall of 1941 there were still no studies and seventy-eight students slept and studied in the lodges and there was no heat in the dining hall until Thanksgiving. Until then meals were eaten in overcoats (the fall was not like this one!). total time spent on the work program probably averaged over fifteen hours per week.
The war stopped construction.

The scheme of having a resident architect who both taught and designed had worked so well that, when Mr. Kocher was unable to return, a great effort was made to procure someone else in a similar capacity. But the demand for architects proved to be so great after the war, that the effort was finally temporarily abandoned and an arrangement worked out with the Architects’ Collaborative whereby they would visit the college and do our designing in the hope that within a couple of years they could plan to have one man in residence here to teach architecture regularly.
As they were just then unable to give the time necessary to make a complete new study and as we wished to start construction as soon as possible, it was decided to make site-plans in line with the zoning previously agreed on and to build a dormitory for forty students in conformity with the general scheme agreed on before the war.
Norman Fletcher visited the college for about a week and after numerous discussions returned to Cambridge and designed the dormitory shown. The model represents a later revision than the drawings. Construction was to have started if possible last July. Members of the community who were here last year know, but those who were not here may not know that the total cost of the planning work done was about $500.00.
TRANSITION June 1946
Material Exhibited:
Drawings of small study-dormitory unit by Don Wight
As a result of plans for a small dormitory designed by Don Wight with the collaboration of several other students, the question of the most appropriate size for a new dormitory was re-opened. It was also suggested that studies and sleeping quarters be combined in small units, as shown in Don Wight’s drawings. After much discussion, the latter idea was rejected, but as the consensus of opinion in the community was strongly in favor of a small dormitory to be built first rather than a large one, the campaign to raise funds for the latter was suspended and the whole matter postponed until a new plan could be made. The Architects’ Collaborative were willing to make such a new study in spite of the belated rejection of their other work, but pointed out that in case we should wish eventually to house all students in small dormitories, it would not be possible to fit all these dormitories into the old zoning scheme. Moreover, a good deal of question has also arisen in the community as to the desirability of separating so sharply the student and faculty living quarters from each other. A compromise seemed possible without going back to the undesirable lack of privacy experience at Blue Ridge.
Evidently, a new site plan was necessary and in order for the architects to go ahead they required some guiding principles which would both tell them what the community wanted and also take into account the short-comings of our present stage of building where there are inadequate facilities for social life outside the studies, (it having been impossible, on account of the war, to construct the large rooms originally designed for the purpose). In this respect, the immediate situation will be much ameliorated by the new temporary housing which the government is supplying.
The Board of Fellows discussed the problem of overall planning with Walter Gropius, a member of the Architects’ Collaborative, when he was here at the end of August and formulated the necessary guiding principles in five points. (See STEP 4)

Excerpts from accompanying letter from Norman Fletcher of the Architects’ Collaborative
The Architects’ Collaborative started with the following five points as guiding principles:
The first dormitory is to be small and is to be part of an overall plan for the development of the College.
This overall plan is to be sufficiently flexible so that all dormitories to house ultimately 150 students may eventually be small if we wish them to be, though the final decisions of whether later ones will be large or small will only be made after experience with the first unit.
There are to be living quarters for one or more faculty members in connection with each dormitory unit or group of small units.
Social areas in the study area are to be expanded, and in connection with the faculty living space in or adjacent to each dormitory there is to be a living room for us of faculty and students.
The zoning which is to guide the long-range building plan would include (a) one area which would be only for faculty members and their families and (b) areas which would allow for an arrangement including both student units and faculty living quarters.
Following is the letter received from the Architects’ Collaborative:
October 28, 1946
Mr. Theodore Dreier Black Mountain College Black Mountain, North Carolina
Dear Ted:
I sent along with this letter some sketches of a site plan, and a first dormitory unit. The sit plan is in four quadrants so as to adhere to a standard size sheet. The program I have been working with for the site plan is the one you wrote out for me last spring. The site plan should be criticized with the idea that it is still in a very flexible state, even though I have put it down rather definitely on paper. This is so because, even though it is just a study, I like to bring each study to a certain level of finish in order to test the optimum values in the scheme. The site plan should also be visualized with the thought that any overall and long-term plan must be reached by gradual stages, each stage carefully designed in itself. This approach will more clearly be demonstrated as we get deeper into the program requirements of what is most urgent and needed first, second, third.
As to the site plan. The entrance drive would come by the music cubicles, then follow close to the bank, making an entrance practically on the water’s edge. Visitors would park here, convenient to administration and dining hall. The theater had its own parking. In good weather, the theater could open out on the large greensward to accommodate large crowds.
The work center of the college is fairly concentrated, and the different parts of the work center become rather large. This works for efficiency, and tends to give order to a program which has many scattered small parts. The two studies wings are connected with facilities which are used by both. This central connecting wing contains classrooms, and living rooms of different sizes. Thus, living and social facilities are found convenient to the work center.
Housing facilities are placed on the steep slopes to the rear of the present lodges for students. Here also are accommodations for 4-6 faculty members. Another housing group which includes faculty cottages as well as small dormitories for students is placed in the area around the Black Dwarf and Meadows Inn cottages. There is a small housing group of two dormitory wings near the farm, with the hope that this will further encourage integration of working and living at the college. A few faculty cottages are placed freely on the slopes of the knoll and in the wooded areas.
The road pattern has been revised with the principle in mind of separating pedestrian pathways from the main traffic road. The central quadrangle by means of small footbridges. In the other neighborhood group, the road encircles the housing, the path system is in the interior of the neighborhood. This road follows approximately the path of the road which now goes by the Jalowitz cottage. I have also designed a road which takes a short-cut between the Jalowitz cottage and the farm house. This would make travelling between the farm and other part of the college much easier.
The first dormitory unit, which is drawn in black, was designed with the idea of at first keeping the road where it is, until as we gradually add the other wings, and build a few cottages we can develop our circumferential road. Black Dwarf and Roadside would come down only after the neighborhood cottages were further developed. The dormitory wings follow the slopes quite easily by virtue of the fact that the wings are connected by open arcades which have the needed number of steps in order to accommodate the grade. The dormitory wings are built in gradual sequence, and at the same time single faculty living-bedroom apartments are added to the central social wing.
Within the dormitory wing itself, there is an attempt to arrive at spatial divisions which give a measure of soundproofness, and privacy. Where the closets are not against the partition to act as a sound baffle, the partitions are covered with acoustic board. The partitions and closets go to a g’-6” height, allowing free circulation of air for good ventilation above them. Bedrooms open onto a dressing space which allows approximately 3’-6” of closet for each student. This would include dresser drawers, mirror, hanging space, etc. The dressing space can be closed off or opened by means of a flexible screen such as aeroshade makes. These screens would make a good subject for experimentation.
The cubage for the first dormitory unit (as shown at 1/8”-1’-0”) comes to 26, 091 cu. Ft. this cubage figured at 65 cents per cu. Ft., (an approximate local figure which we used earlier and which may be now somewhat higher), is $16,959.00.
If we figure labor at 35% of total cost of building (as stated in the FORTUNE survey) then the actual cost would be $11,024.00. Since there are actually eleven residents in this first unit, the cost per person is $1,000.00 This would average somewhat less as the other wings are built.
What would be a tremendous help to us would be a topography map of the site which is proposed for the first unit; that is, the area immediately to the west of Roadside and between the two roads. This should also show what trees there are, if any.
I do hope that you will find the design study thought provoking and also constructive.
If the design seems a little drastic, it must be remembered that the study is for a long range development, and be encouraged by Le Corbusier’s concept “What gives our dreams their daring is that they can be realized.”
The people here in the Collaborative send their best to you.
Sincerely, Norman C. Fletcher
It should be emphasized that the site for the first dormitory to be built now is tentative and subject to our criticisms and constructive suggestions. This also applies to the dormitory plans themselves.
Next week, after everyone has had a chance to study and think about the plans, a committee will be appointed to receive criticisms and suggestions which will in turn be passed on to the architects. This will be the time to make criticisms- while the plans are in a preliminary stage. Later they will not be invited and changes that the architects have to make later will be heavily charged for.
