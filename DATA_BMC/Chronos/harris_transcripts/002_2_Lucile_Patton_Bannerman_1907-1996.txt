MEH: What is your first name, Ms. Bannerman?
LB: Lucile.
MEH: Are you from around here?
LB: Yes. I was born and grew up in the valley, two miles from the college, and came
to the college to live in 1930.
MEH: What was your maiden name?
LB: My maiden name was Lucile Patton. My forebears were early settlers in the
region. In fact, one of my ancestors was the first white settler west of the Blue
Ridge – Samuel Davidson – and on a mountain nearby, Jones Mountain, is a
monument. He was killed by the Indians. That was a couple of years before a
group of people came from over the mountain at Old Fort and made a permanent
settlement. And that was the first permanent settlement west of the Blue Ridge.
MEH: And you were married. Your husband was president of Warren Wilson College?
LB: He was an instructor at the Asheville Farm School, when it was a boys' school.
MEH: [OVERTALK] At what time would that have been?
LB: Well, the school began in 1894, but when we were married in 1930, he was an
instructor. Then he became principal of the school, and then when it became a
junior college he became president, and was president until 1971.

MEH: And so you were here when Black Mountain . . .
LB: Yes, we were here when the college came into being. And right away Mr. Rice
and some of the men came over and introduced themselves, and we had very fine
relations, a fine relationship with them, because we had some things which they
needed and they in turn would invite us to all their activities. So there was a very
fine relationship between the colleges, or between the schools.
MEH: What did you have that they needed?
LB: They needed know-how in building and in farming. Our farm manager, Mr.
Laursen, who was of Danish descent . . .
MEH: What was his first name?
LB: Bernard was very willing and helpful. He would go up night or day, whenever he
was called, to give them advice. One evening they called him about 9:30 – I guess
he was about ready for bed – and asked him if he could come up, that one of their
pigs was farrowing, and they needed some help. So he went up and around the
pigpen stood about fifty students watching this sow have her pigs. And so he said
"Well, what seems to be the trouble?" and they said "Well, we don't know. We
called you to tell us what the trouble is." And so he said "I don't see any trouble."
"Well she's only had three pigs." “Well,” Mr. Laursen said, "That's all she's going
to have." He said "You might as well go home, because it's over." He said "Just
give her some water and go on and leave her alone and she'll be all right." He
said "She's only going to have three pigs."[LAUGHS] Then they wanted
somebody from the school to – one of our students – to be a farm manager. And
so one of our very fine boys, who'd been Mr. Laursen's right-hand man on the

MEH: Could you tell me?
LB: Yes, that was interesting, because she came to attend a conference of which my

Mountain Workers. It was made up of nurses, doctors, county agents, people in
education, ministers, and anybody really interested in working for the betterment

thinking she would come and address the group, give an address. And she wrote
back and said she'd like to come for the entire conference, which was two days,
which was a great surprise and a joy to everybody. And he never once mentioned
price to her. Somehow it didn't occur to him, or fee, and what she might charge.
So when we met her at the train, she and her secretary – Tommy she called her –
came, and she said "Well there are two things I must do while I'm in the area. One
is to visit Veterans Hospital in Asheville and the other is to see Black Mountain

College." So one of the afternoons that she was here, we drove her through the
campus of Black Mountain – this was in the early fifties, and the place was – oh,
the weeds had grown up and it just looked in such – so ill-kept. So my husband

But when we opened the envelope on the way back home, it was a contribution
for the Asheville Farm School. So of course that made us very happy.
MEH: Can you date this specifically?
LB; No, I can't. Just approximately. It might have been 1952, right in that area.
MEH: It wouldn't have been as early as, say, 1945-46?

clippings that the newsmen took, so I could...

LB: [OVERTALK] I can find that out. Yes, I'll look that up tonight.
MEH: Were you at Black Mountain for activities?
LB: Yes, we often went in the evening, for plays and musicals. Just a kind of music
we'd never heard, because there's no such thing as a civic music concert series in
Asheville in those days, so we just – it was the kind of thing we wouldn't hear
anyplace else. But of course we didn't realize how really, sort of how far out the
college was and we didn't realize at that time the talent that was there. It was just
a pleasant activity for us, and we thought "Isn't this great. We can take advantage
of this." But we had no idea what some of those people would become. Now I'm
interested in painting, and if I had had any idea the reputation that Albers would
have at one time – now – I would have gone there to take painting, but that didn't
occur to me that I had an opportunity right there, right here in the valley.
MEH: What did people in the valley think about the college?
LB: Well, they really didn't respect it very much except for a few that I told you about.
They – For instance, just recently I told somebody I was taking the retrospective
of Black Mountain College, retrospective at the college, and they said "You are!"
You know, they still – this thing lingers. And then the students could be spotted if
they were a group in Asheville, if you passed by them, you know. Somebody
would sneer and say "Black Mountain College."
MEH: How was that?
LB: Because of their dress. They just stood out, you know. They were what we came
to know – looked like the hippies of the 'sixties. They were – with their dungarees
on, not very clean, beards and long hair, and that was a very strange sight, as you

everything together and apply for accreditation." Well, he was pretty naive. My
husband told him that one thing they look at very closely is your library, and your
sources of funding – if you have good funding – and your plant. How are your
laboratories, do they come up to standard? Those are the three things they look at
most closely. And so of course nothing ever came of that, but that was – he just
came over to talk that over with my husband because we'd been working for
several years for accreditation, to bring our library up to the standard that they
were asking. And certainly Black Mountain College was in no condition to ask for
accreditation at that point.

MEH: I found several connections to Warren Wilson. In fact I think Ted Dreier's son
came here for awhile as a student.
LB: He did – He came as a day student. When the Dreier – young Dreier boy was
killed in the accident, he was standing on the running board of a car as it drove
down the North Fork Road and he fell and was killed. And I can remember they
had a memorial service for him at the college, and the people in the community,
you know, had never heard of this. It wasn't a funeral to them. And they said "The
Dreier boy was killed, and they didn't even have a funeral for him." They – you
see this memorial service again was so different that they –
MEH: And that's interesting, because that shows a difference even in this part of the
country. I mean that was their New Englander way of doing it, since he was
cremated and they had the memorial service.
LB: But they thought the body must be there, and they must have a certain kind of
service. You could only have a funeral for a person, in that way. But, as I said, Mr.
Dreier was their good public relations man. He was out in the community quite a
lot and loved by many people. I guess he came over here to the Farm School
more than any other faculty member.
MEH: I was glad you pointed that out today, because I think he was a real connection to
the community.
LB: Yes, I'm sure he had the same relationship with the people in Black Mountain that
he did with us. And he would attend social events. I mean, he was invited into
homes. So he was really a grand man. That's about . . .

MEH: More about Mr. Laursen. Just who was he?
LB: Oh. Well, he came to this country, I don't know exactly – he came probably in his
early twenties from Denmark and settled with a group of other Danes in Boston,
and the Luth – They had organized a Lutheran church there, and they were all
Lutherans, and he became a painter. He learned the trade of painting. And it was
through the John C. Campbell Folk School that he… they had some people on the
faculty there from Denmark, and he was in touch with them, and they put Dr.
Randolph in touch with Bernard Laursen in Boston and he – because he knew, he
knew folk ways from Denmark, he knew agriculture, he knew interior decoration.
He could guide students in painting, painting buildings. And we needed a farm
manager, so it was through the John C. Campbell Folk School, of course, which
was patterned after the Danish Folk School. We got in touch with Bernard
Laursen and he came – we were already here, so I would say he came in about
1932. We came in '30, so I think it was a couple of years after we arrived that
Bernard came with his family.
MEH: B-E-R-N-A-R-D?
LB: B-E-R-N-A-R-D, yes.
MEH: L-A-R-S-E-N.
LB: L-A-U-R-S-E-N. And they had one child, which was the father of Denise, which
you saw down here. And he was here and made a great contribution. He spent his
entire life here and is buried in the college cemetery. And his little son, Ernst, who
was one or two when he arrived, grew up on the farm with his father. He would sit
on the tractor when he was no older than two years old, and he went to State

MEH: Is he still here?
LB: No, he has died. He died about ten years ago. And he taught folk games. He was

summer conferences in those days. One reason, it was one of the places they
could meet in an interracial group, bring black people here, and also because of
our food. People are still – Some of these men are still talking about the
wonderful corn on the cob and the tomatoes served three times a day out of the
garden, even for breakfast. They're good with scrambled eggs, by the way, or
omelet. So it made – Those two made a wonderful contribution to the school, and
